Everyone belongs

  • Different socks.
  • Equal respect.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 00:00 to 00:45 (45 seconds). Say: Today we are preparing for Anti-Bullying Week, which runs from the sixteenth to the twentieth of November. Odd Socks Day is on Monday the sixteenth. The socks are a reminder that we can be different and still belong. This assembly will explain bullying and safe ways to get help. You will not be asked to tell a personal story. If you need support, you can speak to an adult privately.

What bullying means

  • Hurt on purpose.
  • Repeated over time.
  • An unfair balance of power.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 00:45 to 01:45 (60 seconds). Say: We use the word bullying for a pattern of deliberate hurt where someone has more power and the other person finds it hard to stop. The hurt might be physical, spoken, social or online. These features help adults understand what is happening; they are not a test you must pass before asking for help. You do not need to prove what someone intended. Tell an adult what happened and how it affected you. Read the three short lines slowly, then pause for ten seconds.

Power is not just size

  • A group, popularity or private information
  • can make it hard to stop the harm.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 01:45 to 03:00 (75 seconds). Say: Power does not only mean being bigger or stronger. A group may surround one person. Someone may use popularity, exclusion, threats or private information to make another person feel trapped. The person being hurt is not to blame for that imbalance. Think about this unnamed example: a group repeatedly threatens to share an embarrassing story unless someone obeys them. No one needs to act it out. Allow fifteen seconds to think about why it might feel hard to get away, then explain that adult help is needed.
Slide 4 of 14 · Autumn 2 · Week 2 · AssemblyBehaviour · Anti-Bullying Week

A disagreement is different

  • People can disagree
  • without targeting or frightening someone.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 03:00 to 04:00 (60 seconds). Say: People sometimes want different things or fall out. A one-off disagreement between people with similar power is not usually bullying. That does not make shouting, threats or cruelty acceptable. A fair disagreement allows people to speak, stop and seek help. Repeatedly picking on one person is different. Consider two people who want different activities and ask an adult to help them agree a turn. Leave ten seconds, then say that disagreeing respectfully does not have to damage someone's dignity.

One incident still matters

  • Do not wait for it to happen again.
  • Tell an adult about harm or fear.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 04:00 to 05:00 (60 seconds). Say: A single hurtful incident may not fit the pattern we have described, but it still matters and it may be serious. You can ask for help straight away about a threat, an injury, an upsetting message or something that makes you feel unsafe. Adults can work out what needs to happen next. You do not have to decide the correct label first. Pause for ten seconds. Remind pupils that saying it was only a joke does not cancel the effect on the person who was hurt.
Slide 6 of 14 · Autumn 2 · Week 2 · AssemblyBehaviour · Anti-Bullying Week

Online harm is real harm

  • A message or image can spread
  • and keep causing hurt.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 05:00 to 06:15 (75 seconds). Say: Bullying can happen in person and online. One message or image can be shared or seen again and again, so harm can continue even after the first post. Do not add a laughing reaction, forward it or join a pile-on. Move away from the conversation and get trusted adult help. An adult can help you report or block safely and decide what information needs keeping. Do not copy, download or forward harmful images. You are not being asked to collect evidence yourself. Give fifteen seconds to think about how refusing to share can reduce harm.
Slide 7 of 14 · Autumn 2 · Week 2 · AssemblyBehaviour · Anti-Bullying Week

A bystander notices

  • Someone watching has a choice
  • about what to do next.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 06:15 to 07:15 (60 seconds). Say: A bystander is someone who sees or knows about what is happening. They may feel unsure, worried or afraid to become involved. We should not blame someone for feeling frightened. But laughing, cheering or sharing can make the harm worse. Noticing gives us a chance to choose a safe next step. Think quietly: what could someone do that does not add an audience? Allow fifteen seconds. Explain that moving to get adult help is a useful action, not an excuse for doing nothing.
Slide 8 of 14 · Autumn 2 · Week 2 · AssemblyBehaviour · Anti-Bullying Week

An upstander helps safely

  • Support the person.
  • Get adult help.
  • Keep yourself safe.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 07:15 to 08:15 (60 seconds). Say: An upstander takes a safe action to help. That could mean telling an adult, checking privately whether someone wants company, or inviting them to sit with you when it is safe. It does not mean fighting, confronting a group or trying to investigate. You are not responsible for fixing bullying alone. Adults are responsible for taking reports seriously and acting. Pause for ten seconds. Ask pupils to keep one safe action in mind, without naming any person or event.
Slide 9 of 14 · Autumn 2 · Week 2 · AssemblyBehaviour · Anti-Bullying Week

Three safe choices

  • Do not join in.
  • Offer kind support.
  • Tell a trusted adult.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 08:15 to 09:30 (75 seconds). Say: First, do not add to the harm by joining in, laughing or sharing. Second, offer kind support if it is safe: you might quietly ask whether someone wants to walk with you to an adult. Third, tell a trusted adult what you noticed. If there is immediate danger, get an adult straight away and stay at a safe distance. Give twenty seconds for pupils to rehearse these steps silently or with their usual aid. Invite one voluntary general example if appropriate. Do not ask what happened to anyone here.
Slide 10 of 14 · Autumn 2 · Week 2 · AssemblyBehaviour · Anti-Bullying Week

Help in school

  • Talk to a teacher, tutor
  • or another trusted adult.
  • Ask for a private conversation.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 09:30 to 11:00 (90 seconds). Say: You can begin with a teacher, your tutor or another adult you trust in school. You can ask to speak privately. If speaking is hard, you can write, draw, point or use your usual communication aid. You might say: I am worried about something that is happening. Can you help me speak privately? Presenter: explain the actual help route here using staff roles, including how pupils reach the adult responsible for keeping them safe. Do not invent an office, form or contact method. Give twenty seconds for a silent practice of the first sentence. If one adult is unavailable, ask another.
Slide 11 of 14 · Autumn 2 · Week 2 · AssemblyBehaviour · Anti-Bullying Week

Keep asking for help

  • Tell what happened.
  • Say if it continues.
  • Ask what will happen next.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 11:00 to 12:00 (60 seconds). Say: Tell an adult what happened, where or when it happened if you know, and whether you feel safe now. You can bring a trusted person to help you explain. Ask what will happen next and when someone will check in. If the harm continues or you do not feel heard, tell that adult again or speak to another trusted adult. This is not your fault and you are not making trouble by asking for safety. Allow a short quiet pause. Adults cannot promise secrecy when someone may be at risk.
Slide 12 of 14 · Autumn 2 · Week 2 · AssemblyBehaviour · Anti-Bullying Week

Difference and belonging

  • We can differ in our bodies, beliefs,
  • backgrounds and ways to communicate.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 12:00 to 13:00 (60 seconds). Say: People have different interests, bodies, backgrounds, beliefs and ways of communicating. Nobody should be targeted because of a difference. Respect does not mean everyone has to enjoy the same things, share private information or be friends with everyone. It means treating people with dignity and keeping them safe. Think of one way a group can make room for different needs, such as allowing a quiet response or explaining a game. Give fifteen seconds. Do not ask anyone to identify or explain a personal characteristic.
Slide 13 of 14 · Autumn 2 · Week 2 · AssemblyBehaviour · Anti-Bullying Week

Odd Socks Day

  • Monday 16 November
  • Wear odd socks if you choose.
  • No buying or explaining needed.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 13:00 to 14:00 (60 seconds). Say: On Monday the sixteenth of November, odd socks can be a simple way to celebrate individuality. Taking part is optional. Use socks you already have if you want to join in; no purchase or donation is needed. You do not have to show your socks, explain your choice or be photographed. Someone wearing matching socks deserves exactly the same respect. The point is belonging, not judging clothes. Leave ten seconds to think about how we can include everyone in an optional activity.
Slide 14 of 14 · Autumn 2 · Week 2 · AssemblyBehaviour · Anti-Bullying Week

Choose one helpful action

  • Notice. Support. Tell.
  • Everyone deserves to feel safe.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 14:00 to 15:00 (60 seconds). Say: Choose one safe action you could take: do not join in, offer kind support, or ask an adult for help. In tutor time we will practise a short help-seeking sentence using a fictional situation. No personal stories are required. If you are worried now, speak privately to an adult after the assembly or sooner if you need help immediately. Allow fifteen seconds for a private commitment. Thank pupils for listening and explain where adult support is available today, using the confirmed school route.
