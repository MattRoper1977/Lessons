Democracy and our voice

  • British Value: Democracy
  • A voice. A choice. A fair process.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 00:00 to 00:45 (45 seconds). Say: Today we are preparing for the awareness week from the twenty-third to the twenty-ninth of November. Our focus is democracy: having a voice in decisions and choosing people to represent us. We will also look at how a proposal can become a law. No one will be asked about their family's politics or voting choices. You can think privately, pass or use your usual way to communicate.

Why voices matter

  • Decisions affect different people.
  • Listening helps us understand.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 00:45 to 01:45 (60 seconds). Say: A decision about a shared space can affect people in different ways. Someone might need quiet; someone else might need room to move. Listening helps us notice needs we have not thought about. Democracy gives people ways to share views, choose representatives and question decisions. It does not mean we will always agree or get our own way. Think quietly about a shared routine you would like to improve. Allow fifteen seconds. Do not ask anyone to name another pupil or disclose a personal need.

Voting makes a choice

  • Understand the options.
  • Choose freely.
  • Count fairly.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 01:45 to 03:00 (75 seconds). Say: Voting is one way to choose between options or candidates. A fair process explains the choices, who may vote and how the result will be decided. People should not be pressured or threatened into choosing a particular answer. A private ballot can help protect a free choice. Different elections use different counting rules. We are not discussing voting ages or a real election today. Think about why understanding both options matters. Give fifteen seconds, then explain that counting fairly is only useful if the process is fair too.

A representative listens

  • A chosen person speaks for others,
  • including people who chose differently.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 03:00 to 04:00 (60 seconds). Say: A representative is someone chosen to speak and act for other people. An elected national representative serves everyone in their area, not only the people who voted for them. They listen to concerns, debate proposals and question the people running public services. A representative is not the same as the government. Some representatives are part of government, but many are not. At school, a pupil representative should also seek different views and report back. Pause for ten seconds: how could a quiet pupil's view be heard?

More than winning

  • Explain decisions.
  • Hear different views.
  • Keep listening afterwards.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 04:00 to 05:00 (60 seconds). Say: Democracy does not end when votes are counted. People should be able to ask why a decision was made, hear the reasons and suggest future changes. A representative should keep listening, including to people who preferred another option. Losing a vote does not mean losing your voice or your dignity. Think quietly about a fair way to disagree with a decision. Leave fifteen seconds. Offer the example of giving a reason calmly and asking for a review, rather than insulting a person.

A law begins as a proposal

  • A proposed new or changed law
  • is called a bill.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 05:00 to 06:15 (75 seconds). Say: A parliament is a law-making assembly. In the national system we are learning about, a proposal for a new law or a change to an existing law is called a bill. Many bills are proposed by government, and some by other members. Introducing a bill does not make it a law. It needs discussion and checks. Think of a proposal as an idea that must be examined, not an instruction everyone must already follow. Pause for ten seconds, then ask pupils to hold the difference between proposal and law in mind.

Debate and check the detail

  • Ask who is affected.
  • Test the reasons.
  • Suggest changes.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 06:15 to 07:15 (60 seconds). Say: A bill goes through stages. Members debate its main ideas, and detailed checks can lead to suggested changes. A suggested change is called an amendment. People ask how a proposal would work and who might be helped or harmed. Members vote at stages of the process. This is more careful than one quick vote on a slogan. Think for ten seconds about one question you would ask before changing a shared rule. Invite a voluntary general question if time allows, with no real political example needed.

Two chambers examine a bill

  • An elected chamber.
  • A reviewing chamber.
  • A chance to check again.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 07:15 to 08:15 (60 seconds). Say: There are two chambers, or groups of members, in this law-making system. One is elected; the other is mainly appointed. Bills can normally start in either chamber. In the usual route, both examine the bill and agree the same wording. Changes can go back and forth before agreement. That is why a bill does not usually become law after just one vote. This is a simplified main route; there are special procedures for some bills. Pause so pupils can link the phrase check again with the second chamber.

Formal approval makes an act

  • After the required checks,
  • formal approval turns a bill into law.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 08:15 to 09:30 (75 seconds). Say: After the required law-making stages, formal approval by the head of state turns a bill into an act, which is a law. This formal approval is called royal assent. It is a formal step, not another public vote. A law may start working straight away, on a later date or in stages. The proposal, debate, agreement and approval are different steps. Give fifteen seconds to think back through that order. We are learning the main outline, rather than every special case or legal detail.

The rule of law

  • Everyone is subject to the law,
  • including people who hold power.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 09:30 to 11:00 (90 seconds). Say: The rule of law means power is not a licence to ignore the law. People, public bodies and leaders are subject to it. Laws should be applied fairly, and people can use lawful routes to challenge decisions. Courts have a different job from making a classroom rule: they interpret and apply law in cases brought before them. A person can disagree with a law and seek change peacefully through lawful action. Give twenty seconds to consider why leaders must also follow rules. Invite one voluntary general reason; do not start a discussion about a real person's case.

Rights still matter

  • School choices must respect
  • rights, safety and the law.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 11:00 to 12:00 (60 seconds). Say: A majority is more than half of the votes counted in a simple choice. But a popular choice does not make every action acceptable. In school, we cannot use a vote to remove someone's legal rights or ignore a safety duty. At school we must also protect access and support needs. Imagine most people want a noisy activity while someone needs a quiet space. A fair solution keeps that person's needs in view. Leave ten seconds. Democracy and the rule of law work together; they are not permission to exclude someone.

Your voice in school

  • Share an idea with a tutor
  • or a pupil representative.
  • Ask for a response.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 12:00 to 13:00 (60 seconds). Say: You can raise an idea through a tutor, a pupil representative or the school's existing pupil-voice route. Presenter: explain the actual route using roles, without inventing a council, contact method or meeting. You can speak, write, draw, point or use your usual aid. A useful proposal explains the problem, an achievable change and who it might affect. Ask what happens next and how the decision will be explained. A suggestion does not guarantee a change, but it deserves a clear and respectful response.

Practise a fair choice

  • Two safe options.
  • Reasons before votes.
  • Support for every voice.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 13:00 to 14:00 (60 seconds). Say: Imagine a class choosing between a quiet reading start and a quiet drawing start. This is a practice example, not a decision being made for this school. Both options would need to be safe, feasible and accessible, with alternatives for individual needs. Before voting, hear a reason for each, explain the counting and tie rules, and allow private choices or a pass. Think quietly about which reason you would want to hear. Leave fifteen seconds. We will practise with a real feasible proposal in tutor time, with adult support.

Use your voice with care

  • Listen. Give a reason.
  • Ask what happens next.
Presenter notes: Elapsed 14:00 to 15:00 (60 seconds). Say: We have explored voting, representation, the main route from a bill to a law, and the rule of law. The same habits can help us at school: listen carefully, give a reason and ask how a decision will be made. In tutor time, suggest one feasible improvement to a shared routine in your usual mode. You may pass or keep your contribution private. Leave fifteen seconds for a final thought. Thank pupils and remind them how to raise an idea through the confirmed school route.
