What Getting Better Would Look Like

BUILD · Art · Week 1. Trinity Arts Award Bronze. What Getting Better Would Look Like.

Objective: choose one art-form skill to develop, and write the measure that will show it changed.

Success evidence: one skill card with one skill named, one measure with a number in it, the proof page named, and today's date.

Your skill comes off your own artwork and off the skill cards in the approved pack. You talk about marks, tools and numbers. You are not asked about your home, your family, your beliefs or how you look. Nobody in this room is asked about theirs.

Identity here means what your own work keeps choosing. Today you choose one skill to build on.

40 minutes. Offline.

This deck evidences Bronze Part A, Explore the arts as a participant. Part A requires active participation in any art form, improvement, and a summary of what they learnt; this deck sets the measure that improvement will later be shown against. Bronze attempted rule, quoted from the register: "ONE Attempted allowed overall. Any Not Attempted = Below Pass." The evidence file cap for the whole Bronze level is 10 files, so plan what is kept. The three assessment areas are art form knowledge and understanding, creativity, and communication.
Delivery is through a validated centre; confirm local validation and an adviser trained at Bronze before assessment. Neither is asserted as completed here. Core, Extend and Challenge are support routes inside Bronze, not different awards. The prompts and sample quantities are teaching scaffolds, not extra assessment requirements. Scribe only the pupil’s own words. Store evidence by part; one cumulative file per part is a suggested four-file plan within the ten-file cap.

A skill is one thing your hands do

Last lesson you made one piece.

You wrote three words for what it does well.

You wrote one word for what it does not.

That one word is where today starts.

Today you pick one skill to build.

A skill is one thing your hands do.

Shading is a skill. Art is not a skill.

Art is a whole art form, and it is too big.

Too big means nobody can tell if it changed.

Read the skill-is-one-thing line once and leave it on the board. Without it pupils write down a whole art form and there is nothing to measure in six weeks.

You need three things: your baseline sheet, one skill card and a pencil.

3 min
Starter

Starter · create the need

Two plans, six weeks apart

Here are two plans from two makers.

Maker one wrote: I will get better at art.

Six weeks on, nobody could tell.

There was nothing to hold up and check.

Maker two wrote: I will shade five steps with no gaps.

Six weeks on they held up the strip.

The gaps had gone, so the change showed.

Both makers worked just as hard.

One plan could be checked. One could not.

  • 1 · pick one skill, not a whole art form.
  • 2 · make the skill small enough to see.
  • 3 · set a measure you can count.
  • 4 · write it down and date it.

A measure is the proof you will look for later.

I Do

I Do · model

I choose my skill and my measure

My baseline word was flat.

Flat means my work has no dark and no light.

So my skill is shading. I write that word on the card.

Shading is one thing my hand does.

Now I make it smaller still.

I pick shading with a pencil, in one small box.

A small skill is easy to test.

Next comes the measure, and this part is hard.

A measure has a number in it, or a can-do.

I write: I can shade five steps from light to dark.

Five is the number, so five can be counted.

Then I add where the proof will live.

My proof is one tone strip on page four of my arts log.

I name the page, not the whole folder.

In the arts log is not a place. Page four is.

Last I write today's date on the card.

Write the card where the group can see all four lines at once: skill, measure, proof, date. The four lines are the thing being taught, not the shading.

Watch the order. Skill first, then measure, then proof.

A measure needs a count or a feature someone can check.

We Do

We Do · everyone commits

Sort the cards, then match the measure

Six cards are on your table. Each one holds a line a maker wrote.

Sort them into two piles: a skill, and not a skill.

Three cards say: blend two pencil tones. Cut a straight edge in card. Mix a colour to match a swatch.

Three cards say: be better at art. Do more work. Try harder.

A skill card names one thing your hands do.

Now take the skill pile and the three measure cards.

Match each skill card to the measure that checks it.

The measure cards say: five blends with no line. One edge with no wobble. One mix held next to the swatch.

Every card gets a match, even when you are not sure.

A guess I can see beats a blank card.

Then hold up the card your table argued about most.

I Do

I Do 2 · connect

A measure somebody else can check

Now look at two measures for the same skill.

The skill is mixing colour.

The first one says: get better at mixing.

Ask who checks that, and there is no answer.

The second says: mix one green that matches the swatch.

Anyone can hold the mix next to the swatch.

So your measure needs two things.

It needs a number, or a can-do.

It needs a thing to hold it against.

That thing can be a swatch, a strip or a ruled line.

It can also be your own week one piece.

Old work is the fairest thing to check against.

You are not racing anybody else in this room.

One more rule: one skill only.

Two skills on one card is two cards, and both get dropped.

  • 1 · name the skill in three words or less.
  • 2 · put a number in the measure.
  • 3 · name what you will hold it against.
  • 4 · name the page the proof will live on.

5 min
We Do

We Do 2 · lab

Test the model’s measure

Use the model skill card shown earlier. Your own piece comes next.

Work in twos with the model skill card.

Read the model’s measure out loud.

Then ask the check question: how would I know?

If they can tell you how, put a tick by it.

A tick means the measure can be proved.

If they cannot, write the word how by it.

How means the measure still needs a number.

Then help them add one number, and only one.

Do not change their skill. The skill is theirs.

Then swap roles, with two minutes each way.

You are not judging the art. You are testing the words.

Say it once to an adult

Read your measure to the adult at your table.

They read it back to you word for word.

Then they ask the check question: how would I know?

You answer with the proof and the page.

If you cannot, add a number and say it again.

That adult is your audience today.

They write your measure in the class record, next to your name.

Now pick one thing that happens next. Keep the measure, make it smaller, or add one step.

Write your pick on the card, under the date.

Your card goes in the arts log folder, not in your bag.
