Choosing A Practitioner, And Saying Why

BUILD · Art · Week 5. Trinity Arts Award Bronze. Choosing A Practitioner, And Saying Why.

Objective: choose a real artist and say why their work draws you in.

Success evidence: your chosen artist’s name, a named work, a reason linked to that work, and a source you can find again.

My Creative Identity is about the choices you make in art. You can keep your private life private. This lesson uses public sources about real artists.

40 minutes. Use the checked source pack, your Part C file, paper and a pencil.

This deck evidences Bronze Part C, Arts inspiration: simple research into a real artist, craftsperson or practitioner’s practice, career, life and work, and why chosen. C1 establishes the choice and reason; C2 completes the four research areas. The source of award requirements is tools/artsaward/SPEC.json, Bronze only. ONE Attempted allowed overall. Any Not Attempted = Below Pass. The 10-file cap applies to the whole Bronze portfolio, not each part or lesson. Append today’s evidence to the existing Bronze-C file and record the actual file name plus page, slide or timecode. The three assessment areas are art form knowledge and understanding, creativity, and communication. Core, Extend and Challenge vary support within Bronze; all retain the same required evidence. Only an adviser trained at Bronze may assess; delivery is through a validated centre. Staff must confirm both, not assume either. Adults may scribe the pupil’s exact words but must not write a reason or research account for them. No practitioner meeting or booking is required for Bronze Part C. The strand is My Creative Identity: discuss artistic choices, not pupils’ private lives.
Before the lesson, prepare a named, checked source pack with at least two genuine choices. Suggested public primary sources: Barbara Hepworth Estate, Biography, https://barbarahepworth.org.uk/biography/ (1903, 1921–24 and 1925 entries); its home page and linked Assembly of Sea Forms record, https://barbarahepworth.org.uk/; Henry Moore Foundation, Discover Henry Moore, https://henry-moore.org/discover-and-research/discover-henry-moore/, and Henry Moore’s story, https://henry-moore.org/discover-and-research/discover-henry-moore/henry-moores-story/. These are research sources, not places to visit. Staff check each selected passage, work caption and public URL before use. Give each artist a page with a clearly credited work image or accessible description, its title and medium, and short, faithful source extracts covering practice, career, life and work. Keep the original beside any plain-language gloss. Number the pack pages and identify the publisher, page title, URL and section on each. Select art-relevant public life facts, such as where an artist worked; omit distressing biographical detail. Have large print and a read-aloud version ready. No invented fallback artist or biography: if a source fails, use a second checked source. The model below is conditional on the checked Hepworth pages being in hand; otherwise model the same process with the actual pack and its exact facts. The deck does not certify a local pack as prepared.

3 min
Retrieval

Arrival · retrieve

Lay your own art beside the two artist pages. Look at the work, not the faces. Which choice in your own piece do you want to know more about? It could be a soft line, a rough edge, a sound or a space.

An artist is someone who makes art. A craftsperson makes work through a skilled craft. Practitioner is a word that can include both. Today you choose one real person whose work gives you a reason to look closer.

You do not need to want their job. You do not need to like all their work. Choose a feature that sparks a useful question. Keep that question beside your piece.

4 min
Starter

Starter · create the need

Look at these two reasons: I picked this person because their name came first. I picked this person because I want to learn how they use space.

The first tells us how the list was set out. It tells us very little about the art. The second gives a line of thought to follow. We still need to point to the work where that thought began.

Fame is not proof that a person is right for your choice. A long page is not proof that its facts are true. A source is the place a claim came from. Check who made that source and what it shows.

  • A named work lets someone find what you looked at.
  • A feature says what caught your eye or ear.
  • A reason says why that feature matters to your choice.

Keep all three. They make your choice clear without a tale about your own life.

7 min
I Do

I Do · model

I have the checked Hepworth source page in my hand. First I read the name: Barbara Hepworth. I use the work named on that page, Assembly of Sea Forms. I check the caption before I write.

The caption gives white marble as the material. That is a source fact. It gives me a place to start. A photo alone might not let me tell marble from plaster. The label helps me avoid that guess.

Here is a model reason: I choose Hepworth because this use of stone makes me want to learn how a hard block can become a new form. That is a reason to research. It does not claim that I know how she made this work yet.

I write the title, then the source page and its publisher. I add the public link from the pack. I can now return to the fact that sparked my question.

This is my model choice. Yours may differ. A colour, beat, shape or making skill could be your starting point. Keep the fact and your reason on separate lines.

5 min
We Do

We Do · everyone commits

Sort three kinds of note: fact from a source, my view, and a claim to check. Write those three headings on your page.

  • The checked caption names white marble.
  • I want to find out how stone can be shaped.
  • The maker chose white to make all viewers feel calm.

The first belongs with source facts. The second is a view and a research wish. The last guesses the maker’s aim and every viewer’s response. We need a source before we can claim the aim. We cannot speak for all viewers.

Place each note under a heading before you compare. You can move a note after a reason is given. Keep a question mark on a claim still to check. A gap is a useful clue for research.

5 min
I Do

I Do 2 · connect

I put two boxes beside my choice. One holds what the source says. One holds what I think. Both matter, but they do different jobs.

I might write: It is made from marble, so I want to find out how she worked with stone. The first half needs its source. The second half is my choice.

I would not write: She loved stone more than all other things. I do not have proof of that. I can replace the guess with a question: What did she say about using stone?

An exact question helps next week’s search. We will look at practice, career, life and work. Today’s choice gives that search a clear focus.

4 min
We Do

We Do 2 · lab

Use a second work from the checked pack. Work with a partner, or ask an adult to be your checking partner. Each person makes their own choice.

Point to a feature. Say what it makes you want to learn. The checker asks: Which work? Which feature? Where is the source? They do not choose for you.

Now swap roles. A checker can help find a title or read a label. They cannot tell you what you must like. If you change your choice, say what new detail changed your mind.

Keep one short note from this check. It might say: I added the work title so someone can find it. This is a check of your explanation, not a vote on your taste.

9 min
Independent work

Independent · evidence

Choose your real artist from the checked pages. Open your Bronze-C file, or draft on paper to add to it. Put the artist’s exact name at the top.

Name the work you used. Describe the feature that drew you in. Say why you want to learn more about it. Add a question to carry into the next lesson.

Keep the source title, publisher and public link with your account. Add the pack page so the source is quick to find today. A photo without a name or caption will not show whose work you chose.

  • Core: use the labels and these stems: I chose… In the work called… I notice… I want to learn… My source is… A reader or scribe can help. Keep your own reason.
  • Extend: write the same name, named work, reason and source in your own short account.
  • Challenge: keep that evidence and compare your chosen feature with another checked work. Explain why your first choice suits your art interests.

These are ways to work, not extra award rules. Short spoken words can be as clear as a long page. Ask a scribe to write your exact words, then check what they wrote.

Check your choice with the source still open. Does the name match? Is the named work theirs? Does your reason point to a feature of that work?

Put a small mark by a fact you checked. Put a question mark by something you still need to find out. Keep questions as questions; do not fill them with a guess.

Add your account to the Bronze-C file. Record its actual file name plus the page or slide. For speech, give the file name and timecode. “In my arts log” does not tell a reader where to look.

Keep your source note and next question together. Next time, you will build a fuller picture of this real person’s practice, career, life and work.
