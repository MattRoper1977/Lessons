The Experience, Captured

BUILD · Art · Week 5. Trinity Arts Award Bronze. The Experience, Captured.

Objective: Experience the arts, review a craft choice and its effect, then share your view and record whom, how and where the evidence is saved.

Success evidence: a record of the experience, your view of its quality and effect, and a located record of sharing that view with another person.

Everything you capture comes from the work in front of you, or from a card in the approved pack. You write about the work: colour, scale, material, sound. You are never asked about your home, your family, your body, how you look or what you believe. Nobody in this room is asked what they believe. Identity here means what you as a maker keep choosing, read off artwork and never off a life.

Nothing is booked. No event and no place is named in this deck. We may go to them. They may come to us. We may meet live on a screen. An online exhibition counts as well. The frame works the same under all three.

40 minutes. Paper and a pencil are all you need to capture.

This deck evidences Bronze Part B, Explore the arts as an audience member. The register says Part B requires attending at least one arts event or experience, reflecting on quality and creative impact, and views shared with at least one other person, evidenced. The register also notes that an online exhibition is legitimate. Nothing is booked: the event comes from EVENT_SLOT, which is UNCONFIRMED, and no venue, organisation, practitioner or date is named anywhere in this deck. It runs unchanged under R1 pupils visit, R2 they come in, and R3 a live remote exchange. Where the experience has not yet run, a card from the approved pack stands in and the rows are labelled preparation, because the slots file counts desk research, archive and video as preparation only. Bronze attempted rule, in the register's words: ONE Attempted allowed overall. Any Not Attempted = Below Pass. The evidence file cap is 10 evidence files for the whole Bronze level, so today's frame sheet joins the Part B file rather than opening a new one. The three assessment areas are art form knowledge and understanding, creativity, and communication.
Delivery is through a validated centre; confirm local validation and an adviser trained at Bronze before assessment. Neither is asserted as completed here. Core, Extend and Challenge are support routes inside Bronze, not different awards. The prompts and sample quantities are teaching scaffolds, not extra assessment requirements. Scribe only the pupil’s own words. Store evidence by part; one cumulative file per part is a suggested four-file plan within the ten-file cap.
Read the current EVENT_SLOT from tools/artsaward/SLOTS.json before delivery. Reserve a genuine audience experience, including an online exhibition, then review and share in this sequence. Allow the event its real running time; this forty-minute lesson does not limit a longer event or claim all sixty Bronze hours. If the slot is still empty, use only the labelled preparation route and return to the actual experience and share later. An archive clip or desk card is preparation, not proof of attendance. The pupil must reflect on quality and creative impact, not just list details. Add pages to Part B; record the actual event, recipient, method and file plus page/slide/timecode after it happens.

3 min
Starter

Starter · create the need

A list is not yet a review

Listen to two practice views of a made-up line drawing.

The first says: there are six lines and a white space.

The second says: the thin lines leave a clear space, so my eye rests there.

Both views name things in the work.

Only the second links a choice to what it does.

A review gives your view, with a reason from the work.

You can like a work and still spot a weak choice.

You can dislike it and still explain a strong choice.

There is no right feeling to copy from the adult.

Which view gives a reader a reason they can check?

Point at first or second, then name the useful words.

5 min
I Do

I Do · model

Choice, quality, effect

Watch how I build a review of the practice drawing.

First I point at the narrow gap between two lines.

Next I name the choice: the lines are close together.

Then I judge its quality: the gap is clear all the way down.

That tells you how well the choice works.

Last I say its effect: my eye follows the gap down the page.

That tells you what the choice did for me.

I join those ideas with because.

The close lines guide my eye because the gap stays clear.

Quality does not mean that every mark must be neat.

A rough sound or torn edge can work well for its purpose.

I would judge that choice by what it helps the work do.

My view is an example. Do not copy it into your own review.

Your reason comes from the real work you see or hear.

Model explanations

  1. My eye follows the gap down the page. — 3 · Effect. The last note gives the effect on this viewer.
  2. The lines are close together. — 1 · Choice. Start with the choice you can point to in the drawing.
  3. The gap stays clear from top to bottom. — 2 · Quality. Then judge how the choice works, using a detail we can check.

Share the view, keep the proof

Writing a review and sharing it are two different steps.

A page in a closed folder has not reached an audience.

I can read my view to a member of staff.

I can show a peer my review and talk it through.

I can play my spoken review to a small group.

The person needs to receive my view, not just hold the paper.

Then I keep a record of who received it and how.

A short note or a sound clip can show that step.

Initials are one option, not a special rule of the award.

The file name alone may leave a reader searching.

Give the page, slide or time in the file as well.

For example: Bronze-B, page three, review shared with class staff.

Use the real file name and page for your own work.

Independent work

Independent · evidence

Experience, then give your view

Now take part as an audience member in the experience in use.

Look or listen first. Give the work time to unfold.

An adult will give you a quiet cue to make a note.

Choose one moment or one part that stayed with you.

Name a craft choice you saw or heard there.

It might be a colour, a shape, a beat or a material.

Describe how well that choice worked, with a reason.

Then say what effect it had on you as an audience member.

You may record a second moment if it helps you compare.

Build your short review from those notes, in your own words.

Check that your view points back to the actual work.

Do not use the made-up line drawing as event evidence.

If this lesson is preparation, keep that label and return to this step after the real experience.

Keep a note of the event or exhibition you actually experienced.

It can be added to your Part B file with your review.
