Their Practice, Career, Life And Work

BUILD · Art · Week 6. Trinity Arts Award Bronze. Their Practice, Career, Life And Work.

Objective: research four sides of your chosen artist and show where each fact came from.

Success evidence: your own account of practice, career, life and work, a source for each, and your reason for choosing this real artist.

My Creative Identity is about the choices you make in art. You can keep your private life private. This lesson uses public sources about real artists.

40 minutes. Use the checked source pack, your Part C file, paper and a pencil.

This deck evidences Bronze Part C, Arts inspiration: simple research into a real artist, craftsperson or practitioner’s practice, career, life and work, and why chosen. C1 establishes the choice and reason; C2 completes the four research areas. The source of award requirements is tools/artsaward/SPEC.json, Bronze only. ONE Attempted allowed overall. Any Not Attempted = Below Pass. The 10-file cap applies to the whole Bronze portfolio, not each part or lesson. Append today’s evidence to the existing Bronze-C file and record the actual file name plus page, slide or timecode. The three assessment areas are art form knowledge and understanding, creativity, and communication. Core, Extend and Challenge vary support within Bronze; all retain the same required evidence. Only an adviser trained at Bronze may assess; delivery is through a validated centre. Staff must confirm both, not assume either. Adults may scribe the pupil’s exact words but must not write a reason or research account for them. No practitioner meeting or booking is required for Bronze Part C. The strand is My Creative Identity: discuss artistic choices, not pupils’ private lives.
Before the lesson, prepare a named, checked source pack with at least two genuine choices. Suggested public primary sources: Barbara Hepworth Estate, Biography, https://barbarahepworth.org.uk/biography/ (1903, 1921–24 and 1925 entries); its home page and linked Assembly of Sea Forms record, https://barbarahepworth.org.uk/; Henry Moore Foundation, Discover Henry Moore, https://henry-moore.org/discover-and-research/discover-henry-moore/, and Henry Moore’s story, https://henry-moore.org/discover-and-research/discover-henry-moore/henry-moores-story/. These are research sources, not places to visit. Staff check each selected passage, work caption and public URL before use. Give each artist a page with a clearly credited work image or accessible description, its title and medium, and short, faithful source extracts covering practice, career, life and work. Keep the original beside any plain-language gloss. Number the pack pages and identify the publisher, page title, URL and section on each. Select art-relevant public life facts, such as where an artist worked; omit distressing biographical detail. Have large print and a read-aloud version ready. No invented fallback artist or biography: if a source fails, use a second checked source. The model below is conditional on the checked Hepworth pages being in hand; otherwise model the same process with the actual pack and its exact facts. Retrieval

Arrival · retrieve

Find the artist and work you chose last time. Read your reason for that choice. What did you want to learn? Keep that question beside the checked source pack.

Today we build a picture with four parts. Practice means how the person makes art. Career means their path in art. Life means public facts about their life. Work means a piece they made.

A good account needs all four. Four notes on the same sculpture will not tell us about a career. A list of dates will not show how the artist works. We will use a different question for each part.

Your own life is not the subject of this task. Use art-relevant public facts about your chosen artist.

4 min
Starter

Starter · create the need

Imagine a reader finds this note: A sculptor was born, went to art school and made a sculpture. The note has facts, but it gives very little to learn from.

Which sculptor? What did they learn? How did they make work? Which piece could we look at? A broad word can hide the thing we need to know.

Now set out four headings. Under each, write a question.

  • Practice: what tools, materials or ways of making did they use?
  • Career: how did they learn, work or develop as an artist?
  • Life: what checked public fact helps place their art in a time or place?
  • Work: what is a named piece, and what can I say about it?

These questions guide a search. They do not set a fixed number of facts for the award. A clear, checked note can do more than a page of copied text.

7 min
I Do

I Do · model

I open the checked Hepworth pages. I read a small section, then choose the fact that answers my question. I do not copy the whole page.

For life, the 1903 entry places her birth in Wakefield. For career, the 1921–24 entry says she studied sculpture at the Royal College of Art. These facts do different jobs. One places a life; the other shows a step in training.

For practice, the 1925 entry tells us she learnt marble carving from Giovanni Ardini. I keep his name because it tells us who taught that skill. For work, the caption names Assembly of Sea Forms and white marble. I keep the title so the reader can find the piece.

I add the source beside each note. I use the page title and year heading for the life, career and practice notes. I use the work caption for the last note.

Before I close the page, I read my note back against it. A shorter sentence must keep the same meaning. A source about one skill does not prove she used it in every work.

We Do

We Do · everyone commits

Sort the model notes into practice, career, life and work. Point to the words that helped you sort them.

  • Birth in Wakefield.
  • Study of sculpture at art college.
  • Learning to carve marble with a named teacher.
  • A named sculpture with its material in the caption.

Birth and place sit under life. Study is a step on the career path. Learning to carve tells us about practice. The named sculpture is work.

A fact can have links to more than one heading. Training can shape practice. Put the note where it answers your question best, then explain the link. Do not count the same thin note four times and leave the questions unanswered.

Check the source too. A correct heading cannot make an unsupported fact true. Leave a note marked “to check” if you cannot find it.

5 min
I Do

I Do 2 · connect

Here is a common trap. A source names a place where an artist lived. I write: That place made all their art look calm. The place is a fact. My claim about all their art is a leap.

I can ask: Did this place affect their work? That is a fair question. To answer it, I need words from the artist or a sound source that makes the link.

I can also give my own view: This work feels calm to me. I label it as my view and point to a feature. It is not a fact about the artist’s life.

Good research can include an honest gap. It does not need a smooth story at the cost of truth.

We Do

We Do 2 · lab

Choose a short passage about your own artist. Work with a partner or an adult. First, read it together. Then cover the words and say what you learnt.

Write a short note in your own words. Uncover the source. Check names, dates and meaning. You may keep an exact name or title; you do not need a new word for it.

Now test the trail. Give the checker your source title, link and pack page. Can they find the passage? If not, add the section name or heading.

A search result alone is a clue. Open the source it points to. Check the publisher and the full passage. If two sources disagree, keep both notes and ask for help checking the difference.

Independent work

Independent · evidence

Build your own account under the four headings. Use the real artist you chose. Keep your reason for that choice at the top, or revise it if your research changed your view.

Explain their practice, career, life and work. Include a named work. Put the source beside each fact: publisher, page title and public link. Add the pack page or source section so it is easy to find.

Use your own short words. You can speak, type or write. A scribe may record exactly what you say. Check the words before they join your evidence.

  • Core: use the four headings and question stems. Ask for reading or scribing support. Give your own account of all four areas, your reason and sources.
  • Extend: write all four areas as a short account. Keep your reason and a source for each fact.
  • Challenge: keep all four areas and compare what two checked sources add. Explain one limit or difference. Do not invent a fact to fill a gap.

If a heading is empty, return to its question. Ask for the relevant source page. Add what the source supports, not what you think ought to be true. Keep your art question in view while you search.
