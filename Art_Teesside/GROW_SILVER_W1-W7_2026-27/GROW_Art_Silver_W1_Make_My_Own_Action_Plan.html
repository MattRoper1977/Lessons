Make My Own Action Plan

GROW · Art · Trinity Arts Award Silver. Make My Own Action Plan.

Objective: Turn your chosen arts challenge into your own practical action plan and test whether a step will work.

Use your own artwork, interests and practical choices. You can choose an abstract subject; you do not need to disclose private experiences.

Retrieval

Arrival · retrieve

Keep the aim in view

Find your chosen challenge and the work that helped you choose it. Read or explain the artistic effect you want to explore.

Look at your starting evidence. Which skill needs practice before that effect is likely to work?

Today you will decide what to do, in what order, and how to check your progress. Those decisions belong to you.

A plan is useful when it helps you take the next action. A long list of impressive words is less useful than a clear step you can begin.

Keep your baseline nearby. It will remind you where you started when the work changes.

If your challenge is still uncertain, use the trial to clarify it before filling a plan with unrelated tasks.

3 min
Starter

Starter · create the need

An action and its purpose

Compare two kinds of note: a note that names an activity and a note that explains what the activity will test.

Drawing some shapes is an action. Trying different sizes to see which shape attracts attention gives that action a purpose.

The second note tells you what to look for afterwards. It also helps you decide whether another trial is needed.

Your action steps should connect to your own aim in the same way. A neat plan can still miss the point if the tasks do not develop the chosen skill.

You can use words, sketches or a short recording to make that connection clear.

I Do

I Do · model

Adult model: plan through small layout studies

Watch the adult draw a large shape and two smaller shapes on scrap paper. They move the large shape towards one edge in a second quick sketch.

These small layout studies are called thumbnails. They let a maker test an arrangement before spending time on detail.

Use arrows on a thumbnail to show the order in which you want the eye to move. Then cover the arrows and look again. If the route is unclear in the shapes themselves, try a change in size or spacing. The arrows explain a plan; they do not fix the artwork.

The adult looks at each sketch from a little distance. They ask where the eye goes first and whether the smaller shapes support that focus.

They do not improve every detail at once. Their first practical step is a layout test, because that is what their own example needs.

Next they decide what evidence would help: the two sketches kept side by side and a note of what they notice.

They name the paper and pencil needed, choose a realistic time for the test, and leave time to compare before moving on.

This is the adult's demonstration of a planning method. Your plan may need tone trials, joining tests or a different arts process.

Start with the skill your challenge actually needs. Decide what action will test it, what you need to do that action, and what result you will inspect.

Then choose the next step from what you find. Planning includes room to respond, not just a promise that every trial will succeed.

We Do

We Do · everyone commits

Visible cards: find the useful check

Match each trial to a check that fits its purpose. Explain what you would look at rather than simply calling the work good.

Use these examples to practise the method. They are not a list of tasks everyone must place in their plan.

If a different check would better fit the artist's intention, explain your reason.

  • A · Try light pencil layers for a gradual tone change.
  • B · Test a folded foot on a paper form.
  • C · Compare two small arrangements of the same shapes.
  • 1 · View from the side and see whether the form stands.
  • 2 · Notice where the eye goes first in each arrangement.
  • 3 · Look for sudden stripes between light and dark areas.

I Do

I Do 2 · connect

Make the plan workable

Choose an order that lets you learn before you commit to a larger piece. A small material test may need to come before a final join or detailed surface.

Name the resources you will actually use. Include the support you need to work safely and access the task.

Decide when and where the practice can happen. Check the real timetable with staff instead of assuming unlimited time or tools.

Leave space to pause, look back at the result and decide what comes next. Drying, clearing tools or setting up again may affect the order.

Plan how to keep changing states before you revise them. Separate samples, a permitted photo or a short recording can preserve a useful comparison.

Include a chance for another person to review the challenge and time for your own final review later. These are two distinct views, not a tick in the plan today.

If something is unavailable, choose an alternative that still develops the intended arts skill.

5 min
We Do

We Do 2 · lab

Test one of your own steps

Choose a step from your emerging plan and try a small version now. Use the actual tool or an agreed safe equivalent.

If you are testing a layout, make quick thumbnails without detailed decoration. If you are testing a join, use scrap pieces before attaching your main work.

Follow your own instructions. Notice any missing resource, unclear order or step that takes longer than expected.

Ask someone to read or hear the step. Can they understand what you intend to do and check?

Keep the test result. Decide whether to change the step, add a reminder or keep it as it is. Explain that choice from what happened, not from a guess.

Independent work

Independent · evidence

Make the action plan you will use

Set out your aim and practical steps in a form you can return to while working. A clear sketch sequence may suit you better than a large table.

For each step, make its purpose clear. Add the resources, time or support needed and how you will check the result.

Include your decision from the test. If you changed the order or method, keep a brief reason with the plan.

Check the whole sequence against the challenge. Remove a task that does not help, or explain why it is needed.

Keep the baseline and plan in Unit 1, Part 1A. Record the filename and page, slide or timecode. Later practice evidence belongs in the separately labelled 1B section.

  • Core · use open prompts to speak, point or write your own plan. Keep your chosen aim, practical steps, resources, time, support and progress checks. Test a step and record your own adjustment. Save the plan with an exact location.
  • Extend · organise the same complete plan in a layout you can use. Explain why the steps fit your challenge and use the test to improve their order or detail. Keep the same evidence and your own decisions.
  • Challenge · keep the same complete plan. Compare two workable ways to tackle a difficult step, then choose one with a reason. Add a sensible alternative for a resource or access problem.

Exit ticket

Exit · Audience and Influence

Show the next action

Use your plan to point out the first action you will take. Show what you need and what you will check afterwards.

Tell staff about any resource or support still to arrange. Keep that as a real next action rather than pretending it is settled.

Save the tested plan where you can find it at the next practice session. It should guide your work and allow sensible changes as you learn.
