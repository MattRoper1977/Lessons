Compare The Reviews And Judge Progress

GROW · Art · Trinity Arts Award Silver. Compare The Reviews And Judge Progress.

Objective: Use actual challenge work and another person’s review to make your own final judgement of progress.

Success evidence: Ongoing practice evidence, a real review of the challenge by someone else, the pupil’s own final review and separate exact locations for both reviews.

Use your own artwork, interests and practical choices. You can choose an abstract subject; you do not need to disclose private experiences.

40 minutes. Keep original work before making changes. Ask staff about safe tools and access.

Retrieval

Arrival · retrieve

Bring the whole challenge into view

Open your aim, action plan and process record. Put the starting work beside a later state that helps show what happened.

Check that the practice actually took place. A plan and a final picture alone may hide the decisions made between them.

Find the trials, changes and reasons you kept. You will use them to review the challenge rather than rely on memory.

You need two distinct reviews: another person's review of the challenge and your own final review.

A comment about one mark may help. It may not review the whole challenge.

Show the reviewer your aim and work. Listen to their view. Then decide what your own evidence supports.

3 min
Starter

Starter · create the need

Progress depends on the intention

Place two versions of a piece where you can view them in the same light. Look from a similar distance and angle.

These simple choices make comparison fairer. A darker photograph does not necessarily mean the artwork gained stronger tone.

For a paper form, view the base and the side, not only the front. If the plan concerned stability, test it on the same flat surface. If it concerned shape, compare the silhouette from the intended view.

Read the original intention before judging the result. A rough texture may serve one purpose, while a smooth transition suits another.

Ask which features changed and whether those changes helped the intended effect. Improvement does not always mean adding detail or making the surface neater.

You may find a strength that grew and a difficulty that remains. Both belong in an honest review.

I Do

I Do · model

Adult model: connect a judgement to work

The adult places their own hatching trials in order. They remind the class of the intended effect before judging the result.

First they look at line spacing. Then they look at where the second layer crosses the first. They point to the actual patches, rather than saying every later patch is better.

They test the view from a little distance. A dark area may draw attention while still keeping a visible line texture.

The adult explains what the work shows and what it cannot show. A preserved sample supports a claim about line quality. It does not prove how long the adult practised unless that time was recorded.

This demonstration models a method of looking and judging. It is not a review written for your portfolio.

Use the same questions with your own art. What was the aim? What actions took place? What effect can be seen?

An outcome may be partly successful. You can explain what worked, what remains uncertain and what you would change next.

Keep the judgement specific to your work. Another maker may prefer a different effect without making your own intention wrong.

Visible review cards: what is still needed?

Choose a card and keep your first answer ready. Point, write or hold its letter. Reveal your choice together, then explain your reason before hearing the adult’s model.

Read these example record types. Decide which could support a real review and which leave something important missing.

The cards are teaching examples, not statements about your own challenge. Explain what you would need to see or hear before accepting the record.

A review need not be long. It must concern the real challenge and give a clear view.

  • A · A general “looks great” comment with no link to the challenge.
  • B · Another person’s own view of the aim, changes and result, linked to the work.
  • C · An adult’s completed review copied into the pupil’s section.
  • D · The pupil’s own judgement of the actual process and outcome, with reasons.

7 min
I Do

I Do 2 · connect

Keep two voices distinct

Give the other reviewer enough context to form a fair view. Show your aim, a starting point and useful evidence of the changes.

Ask what progress they notice, what supports their view and what could develop further. Let them reach their own judgement.

Let the reviewer see the work before showing them your preferred judgement. Ask them to point to a detail that supports their view. If they judge a feature you did not intend to change, discuss that difference. It may reveal another possibility without replacing the aim you chose.

They may speak, write or use another agreed format. Keep their actual words or an accurate record they can check. Note who they are or their role.

Now consider your own view. You may agree with part of their review and question another part. Explain your reason through the work.

Do not rewrite their response to make it more positive. Do not copy it and call it your own final review.

Explain the whole challenge. What did you intend? What did you do? What happened? What did you learn from the process?

Keep a separate location for each review, even if they sit in the same part file.

We Do

We Do 2 · lab

Ask for the real outside review

Show the challenge evidence to your agreed reviewer. Give a short account of the aim without telling them what they should think.

Let them examine the work and explain their view. Ask a clear follow-up if you need to understand a comment.

Record their review in the agreed way. Read back any notes to check that they represent the review fairly.

If the person cannot review it yet, record that as a next action. A reply you imagine they might give is not evidence.

Keep the reviewer's identity or role with the record. Add the filename and page, slide or timecode so you can return to their actual words.

9 min
Independent work

Independent · evidence

Make your own final review

Return to the original aim and the evidence of your practice. Describe what you actually did, including a choice that changed the work.

Judge the outcome against that aim. Point to what supports your view and acknowledge anything the evidence cannot settle.

Consider the outside review. Explain where it helps you see the work differently, or why your own judgement differs.

Say what you learnt from the process and choose a useful next step. An aim that was not fully met can still teach you something worth explaining.

Use your own words or chosen communication format. If a required element is missing, identify it honestly and arrange completion.

Save your final review under Unit 1, Part 1B. Locate the process evidence, outside review and your own review separately.

  • Core · use prompts and communication support to show the actual challenge work. Gather another person’s real review and make your own final review. Keep the aim, process, outcome, outside view and your judgement clear. Give each review its own exact location.
  • Extend · make the same complete evidence-based final review in your own format. Compare your view with the other person’s review. Explain agreement or difference. Choose a reasoned next step.
  • Challenge · keep both real reviews and the full practice record. Look for evidence that suggests different views. Explain any limits in your judgement. Say what another trial could clarify.
