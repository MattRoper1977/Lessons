LAUNCH · Art · Week 7. Trinity Arts Award Silver Unit 1. The First Paragraph Of A Critical Review.

Objective: turn last week's three moments into the opening of a critical review, writing one claim, one moment underneath it as evidence, and one line naming the reader it is written for.

Success evidence: one claim sentence, one moment written underneath as evidence, one named reader, and the paragraph dated on your record sheet.

Everything you write comes from the approved event pack and your own record sheet from last week. You are not asked about your home, your family or your own days out.

40 minutes. Offline.

3 min

Arrival · retrieve

A review is not a report of an evening

Last week you wrote three moments and one verdict.

Those are notes, and nobody reads notes.

A critical review is written for a reader who was not in the room.

That reader wants your judgement first, not the evening in order.

So the paragraph opens with the claim, and the moment arrives underneath it as proof.

This is the opposite of how most people speak, which is why it has to be practised.

Say claim first, evidence second, and leave it on the board. Pupils default to chronology the moment the reminder comes down.

You need three things on the desk. Your Week 6 record sheet, the event pack and the review frame.

3 min

Starter · create the need

Two openings, one event

Opening A begins: I arrived at six and found my seat.

It is true, and it tells the reader nothing they came for.

Opening B begins: the ending works because it takes the light away.

The reader knows what is being argued before the first full stop.

Both writers saw the same event. Only one of them opened with a claim.

  • 1 · write the claim.
  • 2 · write one moment that proves it.
  • 3 · say what that moment did to people.
  • 4 · name the reader you are writing for.

A claim can be argued with, and that is why it is worth marking. A recount cannot be argued with, and that is the problem.

7 min

I Do · model

One claim, one moment, one reader

I take my strongest moment from last week, the single lamp at the close.

I do not open with the lamp. I open with what the lamp lets me argue.

My claim: the ending works because it makes the stage smaller, not louder.

Now the evidence, and the evidence is the moment, placed and timed.

In the last two minutes the lighting fell to one lamp, and the audience around me stopped shifting.

Then one line on the effect. The quiet was made by a choice, not by luck.

Last comes the reader, named in one line.

I write it for someone deciding whether to go and see the work themselves.

That line changes my words. A first-time reader needs the craft term explained; another maker does not.

Name the four parts aloud as you write them. The order is the lesson, not the show.

Notice the length. Four sentences, and I stopped.

3 min

We Do · everyone commits

Sort the openings, then match the missing part

Eight opening lines are face up on your table.

Sort them into two groups: claims and recounts.

A claim argues something. A recount only says what happened next.

Now take the claim pile and match each one to the part it is still missing.

It is the moment, the effect or the reader.

Two cards argue and then prove nothing. They stay in the claim pile and still lose a part.

Hold up the card your table argued about longest.

7 min

I Do 2 · connect

The reader decides the words

The same claim can be written twice and land only once.

Write it for another maker and you can say the word palette and stop.

Write it for a first-time reader and palette needs three words after it.

Neither version is better. One of them is wrong for the reader you named.

So the reader line is not decoration. It is the instruction you gave yourself.

It also tells the assessor why you chose the words you chose.

  • 1 · the claim, in one sentence.
  • 2 · one moment, placed and timed.
  • 3 · the effect on people.
  • 4 · the reader, named from the pack.

If the reader changes, go back and change one craft term. Do not rewrite the claim.

5 min

We Do 2 · lab

Two questions on your partner's opening

Work in twos and swap sheets.

Read your partner's first sentence only, then stop.

Ask the first question. What is being argued here?

If you cannot answer from that one sentence, write claim buried beside the line.

Then read the rest and ask the second question. Which reader is this for?

If you cannot tell, write reader missing instead.

Hand it back and say which of the two you wrote.

Then swap back and fix only the line that was named.

9 min

Independent · evidence

Your opening paragraph, four sentences

Work on your own, using last week's sheet and the frame.

Write the claim first, in one sentence, before you look at the moment again.

Then write the moment underneath it, with its place or its time.

Then write what it did to people, as something they did or felt.

Then name your reader in one line, from the pack's audience list.

Stop at four sentences. The rest of the review is Week 8.

  • Supported · a claim and one moment underneath it, carrying a place or a time.
  • Standard · claim, moment, effect and a named reader, in four sentences.
  • Stretch · as Standard, and name the craft term you would change for a different reader.

If you finish early, write the sentence you would delete first if you had to lose one.

3 min

Exit · Audience and Influence

Read the first sentence to an adult

Read only your first sentence to the adult at your table.

They say back what they heard you argue.

If they heard an evening instead of an argument, move the claim to the front now.

Then choose what happens next. File it, add the second moment, or change the reader.

Write that choice and today's date on the sheet before you leave.

The paragraph goes in the arts log folder with last week's record sheet.
