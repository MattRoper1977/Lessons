Arrival Task – What Do You Already Know?

1. Read it

How many millimetres in 1 centimetre?

Ten. 1 cm = 10 mm.

2. Spot it

Which tool checks a right angle — try square or saw?

The try square. The saw cuts; it doesn't check.

3. Point to it

Point to the '0' on a tape measure.

The hooked end. Everything is measured from there.

1. The big idea

Finish it: measure twice, cut ____.

Once. You can always measure again; you cannot un-cut.

2. Today's work

Today is 'The Blueprint'. What do you think we'll be doing?

A sensible prediction — we check it at the end.

3. Success check

By the end you should be able to mark a cut line accurately. What will that take?

Name one thing you'll need to do or try.

1. Define it

Why is the blueprint written in millimetres and not centimetres?

Millimetres are finer. A half-centimetre guess becomes a 5 mm error, and five of those is a board that doesn't fit.

2. Prove it

Why measure from the hook and not from the end of the tape?

The hook IS the zero. Measuring from the metal end adds a few millimetres to every single cut.

3. Flip it

Measuring twice takes longer. So why is it faster?

Because re-marking costs seconds and re-cutting costs a board. Slow at the pencil, quick at the saw.

The Model — Measure Twice, Cut Once

Key Idea: The pencil decides where the saw goes. Get the mark right and the cut looks after itself.

👀 Look: three tools, three different jobs — and none of them cuts anything.

Step 0 of 4

Find the zero before you find the number.

The hooked metal end of the tape IS zero. Measure from there, every time. Start from the tip of the casing and you add a few millimetres to every cut you make today.

The try square makes the line square — your eye does not.

Hold the stock tight against the edge of the timber, then draw along the blade. A line that looks straight and a line that IS square are two different things, and the saw can tell.

Measure twice means measure again, out loud.

Say the number to the person next to you before you mark it. Re-checking is not doubting yourself — it costs five seconds and it saves a board. Celebrate the re-check.

Measure the person, not just the plank.

A planter is the right height when the people using it can reach it. Standing adult: about 900 mm to the rim. Seated user or wheelchair: about 600 mm, with knee room underneath. A child: lower again. Before we cut a single leg, we measure a real person — a chair, a tape and a strip of masking tape on the wall is the whole rig.

You can measure yourself, or measure an adult, or hold the tape. The mark goes on the wall, not on a person. Any of those three is doing the job.

Scaffolding – Supported

Access & sensory notes (staff)
· Fine motor: holding a try square tight to the edge while drawing is a two-hand job. Clamp the timber so one hand is freed, and offer the triangular-grip pencil.
· Reading the tape: a pupil who cannot resolve the small marks is not failing at maths. Offer the steel rule, or pre-mark the tape with tape flags at the target numbers.
· The ergonomics rig is a chair and a tape. No wheelchair or adjustable bench needed — if one is available it is a bonus, not a requirement. A pupil who uses a wheelchair should be asked, not measured at: their own reach is the best data in the room, and only if they want to give it.
· Scribing counts: the pupil names the number, the adult writes it. The judgement is the evidence, not the handwriting.
· Celebrate the re-check out loud. This is the lesson where "I measured it again" should get more praise than "I finished first".

Sentence starters

I measured ______ mm.

I used the ______ to mark it.

I checked it ______ times.

Scaffolding – Standard

The order, every time

1. Find the zero (the hook). 2. Measure and say the number out loud. 3. Measure again. 4. Square the line with the try square. 5. Mark it with a sharp pencil.

If the two measurements disagree, measure a third time before you mark anything.

Scaffolding – Stretch

To find an error before the saw does, check the blueprint against itself: do the parts add up to the whole? Does each piece leave enough timber on the board it comes from?

When you find one, say how you knew — that reasoning is the evidence, not the error.
