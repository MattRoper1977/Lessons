Stage 1 · Opening

Microscopy and total magnification

Learning intention: Identify the key parts of a light microscope, compare magnification and resolution, and calculate total magnification using eyepiece and objective lenses.

Today I can

  • I can name the eyepiece, objective lenses, stage, light source, coarse focus and fine focus on a light microscope.
  • I can explain the difference between magnification (making an image look bigger) and resolution (distinguishing two close points as separate).
  • I can calculate total magnification by multiplying eyepiece magnification by objective magnification.

Work in your book first. Point, speak, sign, draw, type, write or direct an adult. You may pass.

Stage 3 · Starter

Starter · 4-beat model and retrieval

Whiteboard 4-beat sequence

Beat 1: Clip the prepared slide onto the stage and switch on the light source.
Beat 2: Select the lowest-power objective lens (×4) first for the widest field of view.
Beat 3: Turn the coarse focus knob slowly until the image comes into view.
Beat 4: Turn the fine focus knob to sharpen the detail, then rotate to ×10 or ×40.

Ready: step 0 of 4

Class fingers or thumbs vote

Why must you always start focusing with the ×4 low-power objective lens rather than the ×40 high-power lens?

Hold up 1, 2, 3 or 4 fingers (or point), then tap to check.

Retrieval 1: Name the three objective lens magnifications on a standard school light microscope.

Clue

Check Panel 1 of the diagram.

Answer: ×4 (low power), ×10 (medium power), and ×40 (high power).

Retrieval 2: What is the role of iodine solution or methylene blue when preparing a microscope slide?

Clue

Cytoplasm and nuclei are mostly colourless without stain.

Answer: It is a stain that adds contrast so transparent cell structures such as the nucleus become visible.

Retrieval 3: Why do we lower the thin glass coverslip onto the specimen at an angle using a mounted needle?

Clue

Air bubbles look like dark rings that block the view of cells.

Answer: To push air out steadily and avoid trapping air bubbles under the coverslip.

Stage 4 · I do 1

I do 1 · Light microscope parts and total magnification

I do 1 · Light microscope parts and total magnification

Step 1: Locate the two lenses in the light path: the eyepiece lens at the top (usually ×10) and the rotating objective lens above the stage (×4, ×10 or ×40).

  1. Step 1: Locate the two lenses in the light path: the eyepiece lens at the top (usually ×10) and the rotating objective lens above the stage (×4, ×10 or ×40).
  2. Step 2: Write the rule: Total magnification = Eyepiece magnification × Objective magnification.
  3. Step 3: Substitute the lens values: for a ×10 eyepiece and ×40 objective, Total = 10 × 40 = ×400.
  4. Step 4: Include the × symbol before the number to show it is a magnification factor (no length units).

Worked example 1

Worked example 1: A student views onion epidermal cells using a ×10 eyepiece lens and a ×40 high-power objective lens. Total magnification = eyepiece × objective = 10 × 40 = ×400. If the student instead knows the total magnification is ×400 and the eyepiece is ×10, they rearrange: Objective = Total ÷ Eyepiece = 400 ÷ 10 = ×40.

Stage 6 · I do 2

I do 2 · Magnification versus resolution and electron microscopes

I do 2 · Magnification versus resolution and electron microscopes

Step 1: Define magnification: how many times larger the image appears compared with the actual object.

  1. Step 1: Define magnification: how many times larger the image appears compared with the actual object.
  2. Step 2: Define resolution: the smallest distance between two points that can still be distinguished as two separate points.
  3. Step 3: Compare light microscopes (max magnification ~×1500, resolution ~200 nm, can view living cells) with electron microscopes (max magnification ~×2,000,000, resolution ~0.2 nm, dead specimens in a vacuum).
  4. Step 4: Explain the biological impact: electron microscopes have higher magnification AND higher resolution, allowing scientists to see ribosomes and internal mitochondrial membranes.

Worked example 2

Worked example 2: Exam question — "Explain why ribosomes can be seen with an electron microscope but not with a light microscope (2 marks)." Model answer: Ribosomes are around 25 nm wide, which is smaller than the ~200 nm resolution limit of a light microscope (1). An electron microscope has a much higher resolution (~0.2 nm), so it can distinguish ribosomes as separate structures (1).

Stage 7 · We do 2

We do 2 · method order and data table

Order the scientific method or reasoning steps

  1. Clip the slide onto the stage and select the lowest-power (×4) objective lens.
  2. Place a thin layer of specimen on the slide, add one drop of stain, and lower the coverslip at an angle.
  3. Use the coarse focus knob to locate the cells, then use the fine focus knob to sharpen the image.
  4. Rotate the nosepiece to the ×10 or ×40 objective lens and readjust with the fine focus knob only.

Use move up and Move down to place the steps in order, then check.

Practical apparatus and safety

Carry the light microscope with two hands (one holding the arm, one supporting the base). Keep lamps and leads away from water. Handle glass slides and thin coverslips carefully over the bench; report any cracked or broken glass to an adult immediately. Wash hands after using biological stains such as iodine or methylene blue.
