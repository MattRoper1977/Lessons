Stage 1 · Opening

Active transport in root hair cells and the gut: adaptations and oxygen–uptake data

Learning intention: Explain how root hair cells and small-intestine villi use active transport (with carrier proteins, many mitochondria, and large surface area) and interpret graphs linking oxygen concentration and respiration to the rate of uptake.

Today I can

  • I can explain how a root hair cell absorbs mineral ions from dilute soil against the concentration gradient.
  • I can explain how cells lining the gut villi absorb glucose into the blood when blood glucose is already higher.
  • I can read and explain a graph of rate of mineral uptake against oxygen concentration by linking oxygen to aerobic respiration and energy.

Work in your book first. Point, speak, sign, draw, type, write or direct an adult. You may pass.

Stage 3 · Starter

Starter · 4-beat model and retrieval

Whiteboard 4-beat sequence

Beat 1: Beat 1 · Root hair cells: pump mineral ions (like nitrate) from dilute soil into the concentrated cell against the gradient using energy from many mitochondria.
Beat 2: Beat 2 · Gut villi: pump glucose from the small intestine into the blood when blood glucose is already higher, so no glucose is wasted.
Beat 3: Beat 3 · Myth-buster: Roots do NOT "suck minerals in like a straw" — carrier proteins in the membrane pump ions using energy from respiration.
Beat 4: Beat 4 · Oxygen–uptake graph: as oxygen rises, aerobic respiration rises, releasing more energy so active transport uptake rises.

Ready: step 0 of 4

Class fingers or thumbs vote

A graph shows the rate of nitrate uptake by plant roots falling sharply when oxygen around the roots is removed. Oxygen is not the mineral being absorbed. Why does removing oxygen reduce nitrate uptake?

Hold up 1, 2, 3 or 4 fingers (or point), then tap to check.

Retrieval 1: Why is the claim "roots suck minerals in like a straw" scientifically wrong?

Clue

Straw myth busted.

Answer: There is no suction of individual ions; specific carrier proteins in the root hair cell membrane pump mineral ions against the concentration gradient using energy from respiration.

Retrieval 2: Why does the small intestine need active transport as well as diffusion to absorb glucose after a meal?

Clue

Why gut needs active transport.

Answer: Right after a meal, gut glucose is high so diffusion works; later, gut glucose falls below blood glucose, so active transport is needed to pump the remaining glucose into the blood against the gradient.

Retrieval 3: When reading the oxygen vs uptake graph (Panel 3), what should you always do before explaining the biology?

Clue

Describe pattern before explaining.

Answer: Read both axes (x = oxygen concentration, y = rate of mineral uptake) and describe the pattern first ("as oxygen increases, uptake increases").

Stage 4 · I do 1

I do 1 · Root hair cells and gut villi — same mechanism in two different organisms

I do 1 · Root hair cells and gut villi — same mechanism in two different organisms

Step 1: Root hair cell (plant): Soil water is dilute (e.g. 0.4 mmol/dm³ nitrate), while inside the root hair cell is concentrated (14.0 mmol/dm³). Carrier proteins pump nitrate IN against the gradient.

  1. Step 1: Root hair cell (plant): Soil water is dilute (e.g. 0.4 mmol/dm³ nitrate), while inside the root hair cell is concentrated (14.0 mmol/dm³). Carrier proteins pump nitrate IN against the gradient.
  2. Step 2: Gut villus cell (human): Late in digestion, gut lumen glucose (2.0 mmol/dm³) is lower than blood glucose (5.5 mmol/dm³). Carrier proteins pump glucose into the blood against the gradient.
  3. Step 3: Shared adaptation 1 (Energy): Both cell types contain MANY MITOCHONDRIA to release energy by aerobic respiration.
  4. Step 4: Shared adaptation 2 (Surface area): Both have a LARGE SURFACE AREA (long thin root hair; folded villi and microvilli) to hold more carrier proteins.

Worked example 1

Worked example 1 (Gut glucose WAGOLL): "Glucose is absorbed from the small intestine into the blood by active transport because the glucose concentration is already higher in the blood than in the gut lumen (1). Moving glucose from low to high concentration (against the concentration gradient) requires energy from cellular respiration (1), so the epithelial cells of the gut villi contain many mitochondria and have a folded surface for a large surface area (1)."

Stage 6 · I do 2

I do 2 · Reading and explaining the Oxygen vs Rate of Mineral uptake graph

I do 2 · Reading and explaining the Oxygen vs Rate of Mineral uptake graph

Step 1: Step 1 (Read both axes): x-axis = Oxygen concentration (% of air saturation); y-axis = Rate of nitrate ion uptake (arbitrary units).

  1. Step 1: Step 1 (Read both axes): x-axis = Oxygen concentration (% of air saturation); y-axis = Rate of nitrate ion uptake (arbitrary units).
  2. Step 2: Step 2 (Describe the pattern): From 0% to 15% oxygen, as oxygen concentration increases, the rate of nitrate uptake increases steeply (from 4 to 48 units); above 15% oxygen, the line levels off at 50 units.
  3. Step 3: Step 3 (Explain the rise): More oxygen allows a higher rate of aerobic respiration in the root hair cells' mitochondria, releasing more energy for carrier proteins to pump nitrate against the gradient.
  4. Step 4: Step 4 (Evaluate the plateau): Above 15% oxygen, oxygen is no longer the limiting factor — all membrane carrier proteins are working at full capacity.

Worked example 2

Worked example 2 (Root hair & Oxygen graph WAGOLL): "A root hair cell absorbs mineral ions by active transport because the ions are more concentrated inside the cell than in the dilute soil. As oxygen concentration increases from 0% to 15%, the rate of mineral uptake rises from 4 to 48 arbitrary units because more oxygen allows more aerobic respiration in the mitochondria, releasing more energy for carrier proteins. Above 15% oxygen, the graph levels off because all carrier proteins are saturated."

Supported exit ticket

1. Name the part of a root hair cell that supplies energy for active transport.

Expected point

Mitochondria (by respiration).

2. When oxygen around roots goes down, does mineral uptake go up or down?

Expected point

It goes down (falls).

Independent exit ticket

1. Why does the gut use active transport, not just diffusion, to absorb glucose into the blood?

Expected point

Because blood glucose concentration is often higher than in the gut, so glucose must be moved against the concentration gradient using energy from respiration.

2. Explain why mineral uptake by roots falls when the oxygen concentration around the roots is reduced.

Expected point

Less oxygen means less aerobic respiration in mitochondria, so less energy is released to power active transport of mineral ions.

Aspire exit ticket

1. Explain the two-stage pattern on a graph of mineral uptake against oxygen concentration (steep rise from 0–15% O₂, then plateau above 15% O₂).

Expected point

From 0–15% O₂, increasing oxygen increases aerobic respiration and energy supply for active transport; above 15% O₂, oxygen is no longer limiting because all membrane carrier proteins are working at maximum capacity.

2. How do root hair adaptations separately address (a) the direction of mineral transport and (b) the rate of mineral transport?

Expected point

(a) Many mitochondria and specific carrier proteins pump ions uphill against the gradient; (b) the long thin hair extension increases surface area so more ions can be pumped at once.

Tier 3Mineral ionA dissolved inorganic nutrient (such as nitrate NO₃⁻ or magnesium Mg²⁺) that plants absorb from soil for healthy growth.Nitrate mineral ions are needed to make amino acids and proteins in plants.

Tier 2Limiting factorThe variable that restricts the rate of a process when it is in short supply.At low oxygen levels, oxygen is the limiting factor for active mineral uptake.
