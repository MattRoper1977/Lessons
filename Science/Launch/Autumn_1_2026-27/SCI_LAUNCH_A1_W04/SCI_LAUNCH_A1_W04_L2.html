Stage 1 · Opening

Diffusion in the lungs: alveoli adaptations and testing breath

Learning intention: Apply diffusion to gas exchange in the alveoli, link each structural adaptation to a diffusion rate factor, and use the limewater test as evidence that the body removes carbon dioxide.

Today I can

  • I can state the direction of net diffusion for oxygen (alveolus to blood) and carbon dioxide (blood to alveolus).
  • I can link the three alveolus adaptations (large surface area, one-cell-thick walls, good blood supply) to the factors that speed up diffusion.
  • I can predict, record and explain the limewater test comparison between room air and breathed-out air.

Work in your book first. Point, speak, sign, draw, type, write or direct an adult. You may pass.

Model clue: Three-panel model of gas exchange in the lungs: alveolus and capillary gas directions, three structural adaptations, and the clear-to-cloudy limewater CO2 test.

Starter · 4-beat model and retrieval

Whiteboard 4-beat sequence

Beat 1: Beat 1 · Direction: Oxygen (O2) diffuses IN to blood; Carbon dioxide (CO2) diffuses OUT to air.
Beat 2: Beat 2 · Three adaptations: Huge surface area, 1-cell-thick walls, rich blood supply.
Beat 3: Beat 3 · Physical reason: More room to cross, shorter path, steep concentration gradient.
Beat 4: Beat 4 · Limewater evidence: Clear -> cloudy proves CO2 is present (faster in exhaled air).

Ready: step 0 of 4

Class fingers or thumbs vote

Why does a rich, continuous blood supply around each alveolus speed up gas exchange?

Hold up 1, 2, 3 or 4 fingers (or point), then tap to check.

Retrieval 1: Why does oxygen diffuse from the alveolus into the blood without needing to be pumped?

Clue

High O2 in air -> low O2 in arriving blood.

Answer: Oxygen is at a higher concentration in inhaled alveolar air than in arriving deoxygenated blood, so it diffuses passively down its gradient.

Retrieval 2: Why does carbon dioxide diffuse in the opposite direction (from blood into the alveolus)?

Clue

High CO2 in arriving blood -> low CO2 in alveolar air.

Answer: Respiring body cells produce CO2, so arriving blood has a higher concentration of CO2 than alveolar air.

Retrieval 3: What is the positive chemical test for carbon dioxide gas?

Clue

Clear -> cloudy = carbon dioxide present.

Answer: Bubbling the gas through clear limewater turns the limewater cloudy (milky).

Stage 4 · I do 1

I do 1 · How alveolus adaptations maximise diffusion rate

I do 1 · How alveolus adaptations maximise diffusion rate

Step 1: Trace the two gradients: arriving blood is low in O2 and high in CO2; alveolar air is high in O2 and low in CO2. Each gas diffuses down its own gradient.

  1. Step 1: Trace the two gradients: arriving blood is low in O2 and high in CO2; alveolar air is high in O2 and low in CO2. Each gas diffuses down its own gradient.
  2. Step 2: Adaptation 1 — Millions of alveoli create a huge surface area (~70 m² in adults), so many gas molecules cross the membrane at the same time.
  3. Step 3: Adaptation 2 — Both the alveolus wall and capillary wall are one flattened cell thick, making the total diffusion distance extremely short.
  4. Step 4: Adaptation 3 — Good blood supply (dense capillaries) and ventilation constantly refresh the blood and air, keeping the concentration gradient steep.

Worked example 1

Worked example 1 (Alveolus evidence Model): If baseline gas exchange index = 1.0, doubling the surface area raises the index to 2.0 (twice as much membrane room). Halving the wall thickness raises the index to 2.0 (half the diffusion distance). Doubling blood flow rate to maintain a 2× steeper gradient raises the index to 2.0. When the lungs combine all three adaptations at once, the exchange index multiplies, explaining how gas exchange keeps pace with cellular respiration.

Stage 6 · I do 2

I do 2 · The limewater comparison and closing the evidence loop

I do 2 · The limewater comparison and closing the evidence loop

Step 1: Set up the controlled comparison: Tube A receives room air; Tube B receives breathed-out air over the same observation window and comparable gas delivery.

  1. Step 1: Set up the controlled comparison: Tube A receives room air; Tube B receives breathed-out air over the same observation window and comparable gas delivery.
  2. Step 2: Safe participation rule: no pupil is required to breathe into apparatus or exercise; observing, timing, recording, or analysing the supplied data record are all full Science roles.
  3. Step 3: Observe and record: Tube A (room air, ~0.04% CO2) stays clear during the window; Tube B (exhaled air, ~4% CO2) turns cloudy/milky within seconds.
  4. Step 4: Close the evidence loop: "The limewater went cloudy faster with breathed-out air, so breathed-out air has more carbon dioxide, because respiration in body cells makes CO2 and gas exchange in the alveoli removes it."

Worked example 2

Worked example 2 (Supplied comparison Record): In an adult-operated demonstration over a 30-second observation window with equal gentle pump/delivery rates: Tube A (room air) remained clear at 30 s ("not seen within the observation window"). Tube B (breathed-out air) turned distinctly cloudy at 12 s. Conclusion: Breathed-out air contains a higher concentration of carbon dioxide than room air because body cells produce CO2 during aerobic respiration and it diffuses from the blood into the alveoli to be exhaled.

Practical apparatus and safety

Prepared limewater comparison uses centre-approved apparatus (calcium hydroxide solution is irritant; wear eye protection if handling liquid, use one-way delivery or syringe/pump, never suck liquid back or share mouthpieces). No pupil is ever required to provide a breath sample or exercise; observer, timer, recorder and supplied-record analyst are full Science roles.
