LAUNCH · ASDAN · Week 6. Personal and Social Development, Recording Achievement. Record It On The Day, Reflect On It After.

Objective: write two evidence records from the approved activity log, turn one into a reflection using the four-part rule, and name the detail memory would have lost.

Success evidence: two completed record cards, one four-part reflection, and one named lost detail.

Everything you record today comes from the approved activity log and your own folder. You are not asked about your home, your family, your beliefs or your circumstances. The physical section log is judged on the days you did and the measure you set, never on bodies, weight or how anyone looks.

40 minutes. Offline.

A record is written while you can still see it

An evidence record is a short written account of work you actually did.

It carries a date, the activity, your job in it, and one thing you decided.

Written on the day, a record holds detail. Written next week, it holds a summary.

An assessor cannot sign a summary. A summary could be anybody's afternoon.

You are not being asked to write more. You are being asked to write it sooner.

Say the sooner-not-more line once and leave it up. Pupils who hear evidence hear length, and length is not what is missing.

Your activity log lists what you did this week. It gives you the date and the session.

The record card turns one line of that log into evidence an adult can sign.

You need three things on the desk. The activity log, two blank record cards, and the reflection frame.

Two records, one week apart

Two learners did the same community task on Tuesday.

One wrote her record card that afternoon. It names the price she checked and the person she asked.

The other wrote hers the following Monday. It says the task went well and the group worked hard.

Both are honest. Only one of them can be signed off.

The Monday record did not lose detail because that learner was careless. Detail is the part that fades first.

What fades is exactly what an assessor needs. The decision, the number, the words you used.

  • 1 · date it on the day.
  • 2 · name the one thing you decided.
  • 3 · write what changed because of it.
  • 4 · check an adult could sign it.

Four lines take about three minutes. Rebuilding them a week later takes longer and proves less.

Writing one record card

My log line says Tuesday, café session, kitchen team.

That is the activity. On its own it is not yet evidence.

So I fill the card in order. Date first: Tuesday the fourth.

Activity next: the café session. My job: taking orders at the counter.

Now the line that carries the marks. The one thing I decided.

A customer asked for a drink we had run out of. I offered two we did have.

That is a decision. It has a moment in it, and it has me in it.

Then the effect. She took the second one, and the queue kept moving.

Last line: next time I would read the stock list before the session opens.

Notice what I left out. I did not write that the session was busy.

Busy is a fact about the room. It proves nothing about me.

Write the card in real time, including the pause where you choose which moment counts as the decision. That pause is the skill being modelled.

Every line on my card could be checked by somebody who was there.

Sort the record cards

Six filled record cards are on your table. Each one came from a real session.

Sort the six cards into two groups: signable and not signable.

Then match each not-signable card to the line it is missing. No date, no decision, or no effect.

Sort every card, including the ones you argue about. A pile somebody can see beats a card left out.

Two cards carry a decision that is really a feeling. Match those to the missing decision.

If your table splits on one card, read its decision line out loud on its own. Place the card by that line, not by the whole story around it.

When we compare piles, name your group first and give your reason second.

Turning a record into a reflection

A record says what happened. A reflection says what that tells you about your own skill.

The four-part rule is printed on your frame. Decision, effect, skill, next time.

Watch me use it on the card I wrote a moment ago.

Decision: I offered two other drinks instead of saying no.

Effect: the customer bought one, and the queue did not stop.

Skill: I can offer an alternative under pressure without asking a supervisor first.

Next time: I would learn the stock list, so the alternative comes faster.

The third part is the one being assessed. Skill is what the criterion asks for.

So test each sentence with one question. Does it name something I can do?

It went well is not a skill. It is a verdict with nobody inside it.

If your sentence holds only events, add the words this shows I can, and finish it.

  • 1 · copy the decision straight off the card.
  • 2 · write the effect it had.
  • 3 · name the skill it proves, starting with this shows I can.
  • 4 · write one thing you would do differently.

Find the skill sentence

Work in twos. Swap frames and read your partner's reflection silently.

Point at the sentence that names a skill. Do not read it out for them.

If you cannot find one, say which sentence comes closest.

They add the missing words in one clause while you watch.

A skill sentence holds a verb you could do again tomorrow. Offer, check, ask, plan. Point at that verb.

Most reflections stop at the effect. The effect is the easiest part to write.

Then swap back and check your own the same way.

Independent · evidence

Two cards, one reflection, one lost detail

Work on your own now. Your activity log stays open on the desk.

Fill two record cards from two different lines of that log.

Every card takes the date, the activity, your job, one decision and its effect.

Use a different session for each card. Two cards from one session prove one thing twice.

Then take the stronger card and write the reflection under it, using all four parts.

Then write the one detail you would have lost by waiting a week.

  • Supported · fill one card in full, and write the decision with the effect it had.
  • Standard · fill two cards, write the four-part reflection, and name one lost detail.
  • Stretch · as Standard, and say which criterion you would offer the reflection against.

If you finish early, read both cards and underline every word an adult could check.

Then cross out any word that only says how the session felt.

Exit · Audience and Influence

Read the reflection to an adult

The adult at your table is your audience. They are listening as an assessor would.

Read your reflection to them, all four parts, in order.

They say one thing back. The skill they heard, or that they could not hear one.

Could not hear one is information. It names the sentence to rebuild next.

Then choose what happens next. Write a second reflection, ask for a witness signature, or file both cards as they are.

Write that choice and today's date on the top card before you leave.
