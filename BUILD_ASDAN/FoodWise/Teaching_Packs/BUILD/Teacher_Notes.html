This guide contains answers. Open the lesson slideshow for the pupil view. Use fictional examples and accept speech, pointing, drawing or adult scribing.

Week 1 · 40 minutes

Food Groups

Open lesson slideshow

Objective

Recognise the main food groups and identify the ingredients in a simple combination food.

  • I can sort familiar ingredients into food groups.
  • I can name two groups in a combination food.
  • I can help plan a snack within a pretend budget.

Preparation

  • Print a five-group mat and eight ingredient cards per pair. Cards may stay uncut if cutting would add load.
  • Read the pretend wrap and shop examples. Decide whether pupils need a two-choice group prompt, counters or adult scribing.
  • Use paper resources throughout. If showing packaging, use clean photographs or images; any real-food extension needs a separate teacher check against school procedures and care plans.

Materials

  • Pupil task sheet and group/card resource
  • Pencils or highlighters
  • Ten 10p counters per pair or calculator
  • Five labelled spaces on a table; no food or cooking tools required

40-minute plan

TimeActivity
3 minSettle, choose a response method and recognise an ingredient.
6 minTeach group names and model the potato/carrot distinction.
8 minPairs sort eight ingredients and swap jobs.
7 minModel and complete the combination-wrap task.
8 minPlan a two-item snack with 100p and explain the check.
5 minReview examples and complete the exit check.
3 minReturn cards and reset the workspace.

Vocabulary

  • ingredient: One of the foods used to make a dish.
  • food group: A way of putting similar foods together in the Eatwell Guide.
  • combination food: A dish made from ingredients from different groups.
  • budget: The amount of money available to spend.

Model the task

Place potato with starchy foods and say “I am checking the ingredient, not its colour.” Then unpack the pretend wrap ingredient by ingredient. Model 50p + 40p using counters and physically leave 10p aside.

Support

Start with bread and apple and two named group areas. Read ingredient cards aloud. Accept pointing, placing, drawing, signing or an exact scribed explanation. Offer one step at a time and a quiet observation role.

Stretch

Add potato to the sort and justify its placement. Make two different snack pairs under 100p and calculate the difference between their totals. Do not add personal food questions.

Fallback

Use the worksheet without scissors, counters or a screen. The adult reads each item and pupils point to a printed group name. Model prices as tens on paper.

Safety and sensitive discussion

Keep examples fictional and prices invented. Avoid comments about pupils’ food, body, household resources or eating. There is no required tasting or food handling. Pass, observe and quiet participation are valid choices; discuss any disclosed concern privately through school procedures.

What to check

Observe one unaided or prompted ingredient match, recognition of two groups in the wrap and one check of the pretend budget. Record the actual response and assistance, for example “placed milk with dairy after two choices”. This is a classroom observation, not an accredited award decision.

Common misconceptions

  • Potato is placed with starchy foods in this grouping, even though it grows as a vegetable.
  • A combination food does not need one single group label; identify its ingredients.
  • A food group is not a guarantee that a product is suitable for someone. Allergens and individual needs still require adult checks.
  • These group cards do not set exact amounts or require every snack to include all groups.

Week 2 · 40 minutes

A Balanced Plate

Open lesson slideshow

Objective

Use food groups to plan a simple meal, recognising variety across a day or week rather than requiring every group on every plate.

  • I can identify groups in a mixed dish.
  • I can plan a meal from a set of ingredient cards.
  • I can explain a budget or teamwork choice.

Preparation

  • Print the café cards and task sheet. Keep the prices visible while modelling addition.
  • Plan flexible pair roles; an adult can be the partner where collaboration would increase distress.
  • Read the fictional-day example in advance. Its purpose is group identification; it is not a quantified meal plan and must not become a pupil food diary.

Materials

  • Pupil café planning sheet
  • Group reference and café cards
  • Pencils, counters or calculator
  • Paper for drawing a meal; no food or cooking equipment needed

40-minute plan

TimeActivity
3 minSettle and retrieve the rice food group.
5 minExplain variety across a day/week and model the rice bowl.
7 minIdentify groups in three named dishes.
5 minModel the café brief, prices and worked total.
10 minPlan a café meal and swap planner/checker jobs.
5 minSort the fictional-day ingredients and discuss the single-plate misconception.
3 minExit explanation and specific feedback.
2 minReturn resources and reset.

Vocabulary

  • variety: A range of different choices.
  • plan: Decide what to do before starting.
  • combine: Put ingredients together.
  • balance: Looking at the range of food groups across a day or week.

Model the task

Put rice, beans and tomato cards together. Name each group, then count 40p + 40p + 30p = 110p and show 10p remaining from 120p. Say “This meets our café design brief.” Explicitly distinguish the exercise brief from a rule about every meal.

Support

Use just rice, beans and one vegetable card first. Provide a 120p counter mat and remove each cost in turn. Pupils may point, arrange, draw, sign or dictate. Read the fictional menu aloud and offer one group choice at a time.

Stretch

Create both the rice and roll versions and compare their totals. Explain how one dish contains more than one group, and identify the group represented once in the fictional-day example.

Fallback

Deliver from the paper sheet only. An adult draws three ingredient boxes and reads out the prices. Pupils can choose with eye gaze, pointing or speech; scribe the actual answer.

Safety and sensitive discussion

Avoid personal meal logs, public discussions of household budgets, food rankings and body-related explanations. Respect cultural choices, sensory preferences and pass/observe participation. Real cooking would require teacher-approved ingredients, allergen controls and individual care plans; this lesson has no required food contact or tasting.

What to check

Check a mixed-dish group match, a plan meeting the fictional brief, and one reason grounded in cost or group evidence. Record the planner/checker action observed and assistance given. Invite a choice of card size or role for next time and acknowledge how it will be used.

Common misconceptions

  • A single meal does not have to show all five groups; the wider day or week matters.
  • Food colour cannot establish its group or suitability. Read the named ingredient.
  • Spending exactly the full budget is allowed, but spending less also meets the brief.
  • A paper plan records planning. It must not be recorded as an observed cooking or safe-tool achievement.

Week 3 · 40 minutes

Reading Labels

Open lesson slideshow

Objective

Find important label information and compare the same measure on two fictional labels, asking an adult when suitability is uncertain.

  • I can find ingredients, dates and storage instructions.
  • I can explain a use-by warning and an allergen-check action.
  • I can compare the same measure on two labels.

Preparation

  • Print fictional labels A/B large enough to read; MILK and CELERY are emphasised with capitals in this teaching format.
  • Use the stated October 2026 scenario dates. Display “Pretend today…” on each case so the activity does not depend on the real lesson date.
  • Read the dated-food and allergen teaching notes. Keep all tasks paper based; pupil label skills never replace staff ingredient approval, allergen controls or individual care plans.

Materials

  • Two full fictional label extracts per pupil/pair
  • Three date/storage case cards
  • Pupil worksheet, pencil or highlighter
  • Calculator or 10p/20p counters for price comparison; no edible samples

40-minute plan

TimeActivity
3 minSettle and locate an ingredients-list example.
6 minModel ingredients, highlighted allergen and storage checks.
5 minExplain use-by versus best before using one dated case.
7 minComplete Label A information hunt.
7 minRead and decide the three date/storage cases.
7 minCompare the two per-100g values and equal-size prices.
3 minExit safety action and corrective feedback.
2 minReturn labels and reset.

Vocabulary

  • allergen: Something that can cause an allergic reaction in a person who is allergic to it.
  • use-by: A date used for food safety.
  • best before: A date used for food quality.
  • per 100g: The amount in 100 grams, used for a like-for-like comparison.
  • storage: How and where a product must be kept.

Model the task

Trace the Label A ingredients with a finger to MILK. Trace the date words before reading the date. Read the storage and after-opening statements in full. Then align the two per-100g lines: 0.60g and 0.30g. Explain only what those numbers establish, and separately identify Label B’s CELERY warning.

Support

Offer one enlarged label at a time and read the exact wording aloud. Use separate prompts: ingredients, date, storage. Accept pointing, signing, circling or scribing. For the comparison, use 0.60/0.30 as 60/30 hundredths only if decimals are accessible; otherwise let the adult identify the values and ask the pupil to choose the matching smaller-value card.

Stretch

Explain why A’s 200g and B’s 300g portion lines cannot be compared as equal portions. Calculate the 0.30g difference in salt per 100g and the 20p price difference, then state one safety question these figures cannot answer.

Fallback

Read the labels from the printed resource. Pupils point to a date, ingredient or storage line. Use the date cases orally and record their exact decision. No internet, photographs, packaging or food is required.

Safety and sensitive discussion

Use fictional allergy scenarios without inviting disclosure. Follow existing school care plans and food procedures for any real products. Keep food-contact and tasting optional and outside this paper lesson. Do not rank pupils’ choices or ask about personal eating, bodies or household shopping. Refer a real uncertainty to the responsible adult.

What to check

Record whether the pupil located an ingredient warning, date and storage line; distinguished one safety-date case from the quality-date case; and compared matching units. Correct “smells fine means safe” before closing. Note actual support and avoid recording paper rehearsal as successful food preparation or formal accreditation.

Common misconceptions

  • A tomato picture does not mean a soup contains only tomatoes; the ingredient list here includes milk.
  • The 14 regulated allergens are not the only possible allergies. Absence of highlighted words or a “may contain” warning does not prove suitability.
  • Use-by is about safety and best before about quality. For these never-cooked/never-frozen cases, do not eat after use-by even if smell or appearance seems normal.
  • Poor storage can create a problem before the date. A date on its own does not prove a product is safe.
  • A smaller per-100g salt value or price is a numerical fact, not a judgement about the entire food or a person’s needs.

Booklet answers

1. Find the information

2. Choose the next action

    Kitchen hygiene and safety

    Open lesson slideshow

    Objective

    Follow a hygiene routine, recognise hazards and practise an agreed tool skill safely.

    • I can show the handwashing and ready-to-work routine.
    • I can stop and ask before an unsafe action.
    • I can explain how one rule protects the team.

    Preparation

    • Check actual ingredient labels, use-by dates, storage instructions and individual plans; agree any substitution before the session.
    • Prepare handwashing, clean worktops, separate clean utensils and waste area; adult manages cleaning chemicals according to the product label.
    • Keep chilled ingredients at 0–5°C until needed; check the fridge thermometer. Return unused chilled ingredients promptly and follow pack opening instructions.
    • Set up only the tools approved for this class. Have banana pieces or a non-food model ready; no heat, grater or peeler is required.

    Materials

    • Soap, water, disposable towels, aprons and adult-managed cleaning supplies.
    • Stable boards, approved table knife, banana or safe practice model, printed routine and hazard cards.

    40-minute plan

    TimeActivity
    3 minSettle, choose role and recall safety.
    7 minDemonstrate routine and tool stop/placement.
    7 minRead and sort hazard situations.
    12 minSupervised circuit and observation.
    5 minRecord and explain the process.
    4 minAdult checks tools are accounted for and manages washing sharp items separately; pupils wash safe utensils, clear waste, clean the workspace as directed and wash hands.
    2 minIndividual exit check.

    Vocabulary

    • hygiene: Actions that help keep food and equipment clean.
    • hazard: Something that could cause harm.
    • cross-contact: An allergen moving from one food or surface to another.
    • supervision: An adult checking and supporting the activity.

    Model the task

    Think aloud while washing, checking the board and parking the table knife. Deliberately narrate a stop when information is missing; never stage a real unsafe action.

    Support

    BUILD core: one instruction at a time, two hazard choices and an agreed observer or practical role. Adults prepare food pieces and maintain direct tool supervision. Accept pointing, sequencing, demonstration and scribing.

    Stretch

    Explain two ways a used spoon could contaminate ready-to-eat food and describe separate prevention steps.

    Fallback

    Complete the written hazard sort and rehearse the routine without ingredients. Model safe tool placement using a printed tool card; label this as simulation.

    Safety and sensitive discussion

    Check the school risk assessment, pupil food/allergy and swallowing plans privately, and actual ingredient labels before the lesson. Use the school response plan if anyone becomes unwell. An adult approves substitutions and supervises tool work. No tasting or food contact is required: observer, reader or recorder roles count. Never force physical guidance; agree support and use demonstration, adapted tools or adult preparation. Do not record identifiable pupil details in website files.

    What to check

    Observe the hygiene sequence, response to stop cue and safe tool placement. Record independent, prompted or not yet shown. A correct spoken safety explanation can be credited separately from an observed practical skill.

    Common misconceptions

    • An apron alone does not make food work hygienic.
    • A visible clean surface does not prove an allergen is absent.
    • Completing today’s check does not qualify a pupil to use tools unsupervised.

    Open lesson slideshow

    Objective

    • I can follow the agreed preparation routine.

    • Print recipe and task sheet; make ingredient amounts visible at each station.

    TimeActivity
    3 minRoles, readiness and recall.

    Week 6 · 40 minutes

    Plan a meal to a brief

    Open lesson slideshow

    Objective

    Plan a fictional meal using food groups, a budget and safe preparation checks, then evaluate the plan.

    • I can choose components that fit the fictional brief.
    • I can add the given costs and identify preparation checks.
    • I can explain one change using evidence.

    Preparation

    • Print complete wrap recipe, illustrative cost cards and fictional brief.
    • Prepare component word cards or drawing boxes; use empty clean packaging or printed examples, not real food, for this planning lesson.
    • Explain that all prices are invented and no real shopping or making is required today.

    Materials

    • Full wrap recipe, illustrative price cards, paper, pencils, scissors for adult-cut cards, optional calculator.

    40-minute plan

    TimeActivity
    3 minRecall food groups and introduce fictional club.
    6 minRead brief and choose components.
    7 minModel 125p cost and alternative.
    12 minCreate a component, cost and safe-order plan.
    6 minEvaluate against the brief and improve one point.
    4 minReturn cards, clear desks and organise plans.
    2 minIndividual exit check.

    Vocabulary

    • plan: Decide steps and resources before making.
    • budget: The money limit for a task.
    • component: One part of the meal.
    • evaluate: Check a plan or product against its brief.

    Model the task

    Build a fictional plan aloud, total 70p + 30p + 25p, compare with 150p and circle the missing real label check. Show that a correct sum cannot answer an allergy question.

    Support

    BUILD: use three component cards, preprinted amounts, 10p/5p counters or a calculator, and two verdict choices before introducing need evidence. Allow pointing, drawing, oral response and scribing.

    Stretch

    Compare the £1.25 plan with the £1.40 alternative, explain what evidence each needs, then calculate two lunch sets. Derive wrap costs from whole packs if appropriate.

    Fallback

    All materials are printed and the core lesson is already a non-food offline task. Adult can read and scribe while the pupil selects component cards.

    Safety and sensitive discussion

    Check the school risk assessment, pupil food/allergy and swallowing plans privately, and actual ingredient labels before the lesson. Use the school response plan if anyone becomes unwell. An adult approves substitutions and supervises tool work. No tasting or food contact is required: observer, reader or recorder roles count. Never force physical guidance; agree support and use demonstration, adapted tools or adult preparation. Do not record identifiable pupil details in website files.

    What to check

    Check an accurate fictional plan, correct supported calculation, sensible sequence, a distinct adult approval check and one evidence-based improvement. Do not score personal food preferences or infer practical competence from planning.

    Common misconceptions

    • A menu is a plan, not evidence of food handling.
    • Meeting the cost limit does not establish safety.
    • The supplied £1.25 is an invented ingredient-used cost, not a current shopping bill.
