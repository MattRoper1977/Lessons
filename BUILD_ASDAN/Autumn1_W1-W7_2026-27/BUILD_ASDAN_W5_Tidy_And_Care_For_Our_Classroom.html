Tidying is a routine, not a mood

A tidy routine is a list. You do it the same way whether or not the room looks bad.

Doing it only when it looks bad means it never gets done early enough.

Our routine has four steps. Bins. Benches. Kit back. Floor.

Bins first, because a full bin makes every other step harder.

Floor last, because everything else puts things on it.

Hold the order. Pupils who start with the floor will sweep twice and blame the routine.

You need the tidy card, the eco job list, and the partner message sheet.

The card stays in your hand the whole time. Put it down and you will miss a step.

Nobody is checking whether the room looks nice. They are checking whether the four steps happened.

That is easier, and it is fairer, because it does not depend on anyone's taste.

Two rooms at four o'clock

In the first room, someone tidied the bits they could see.

In the second, someone did four steps in order.

Both rooms look fine from the door.

Only one of them has the kit back in the right box for tomorrow.

Looking fine is not the same as being ready.

  • 1 · bins out first.
  • 2 · benches wiped.
  • 3 · kit back in its box.
  • 4 · floor last.

Look at the second room again. The kit is in the right boxes.

Tomorrow morning somebody will open one of those boxes and find what they expected.

That is what tidying is for. It is a message to the next person.

Four steps, in order, out loud

I pick up the card first. The card leads and I follow it.

Bins. I take the bin out and put a new bag in. Tick.

Benches. I wipe each one and check underneath. Tick.

Kit back. Every box has a picture on it, so the kit goes where the picture says.

A box with the wrong kit in it is worse than an empty box, because tomorrow nobody finds it.

Floor. Once, in one direction, and then I sign the card.

Four ticks and a signature. Anybody can check what I did.

Model the kit boxes carefully. Wrong kit in a right box is this room's most common problem.

Notice I do not stop halfway to do the floor. The floor waits.

Every time you sweep early you sweep twice, and the second sweep is the one nobody has time for.

Sort the jobs

Six job cards are on your table. Sort them into every session and once a term.

Wiping the benches. Sorting the recycling boxes. Emptying the bin.

Cleaning the windows. Putting the kit back. Checking the plant.

When your table is done, hold up the card you argued about most.

Two of the six could be either. Say which, and say what would decide it.

An eco job needs a number

An eco job is one thing we do for the whole half term to waste less.

It needs a number at the start, or you will never know if it worked.

Count the bags of rubbish this week. Count them again at half term.

A photo works too, as long as it is taken from the same place both times.

So today the eco job starts with a count or a photo, before anything else.

Write the number down. A number nobody wrote down is a number nobody has.

Take the photo from the same spot both times. Tape a mark on the floor if you need to.

A photo from a different angle proves nothing, however good it looks.

Run the routine for a partner

Work in twos, one bench between you.

One runs the four steps out loud, the other holds the card.

Say each step before you do it.

The person with the card ticks only what they heard said.

Then swap and run it the other way.

If your partner skips a step, say the step name and let them do that one.

Do not restart the routine. The point is that each step is separate.

Card, count, and a first message

Work on your own. Finish your tidy card and sign it.

Then write the number or take the photo for your eco job.

Then write the first message to our partner, in three lines.

Line one is who we are. Line two is what we are doing. Line three is what we would like to ask.

No addresses and no phone numbers. Just the three lines.

  • Supported · finish the tidy card and write the count.
  • Standard · finish the card, write the count, and draft the three-line message.
  • Stretch · as Standard, and say what you expect the count to be at half term.

Sign the card with your name, not your initials.

Write the count in a box, not in a sentence, so the second count sits under it.

Read your three lines back to yourself before you show them to anyone.

Read the message to an adult

Read your three lines to the adult at your table.

They read them back exactly as you wrote them.

If that is not what you meant, say it again and they write the new one.

Then choose what happens next: send this one, change line three, or wait a week.

Your choice goes on the sheet before you leave.

The tidy card goes on the hook by the door and the count sheet goes in the folder.

TA Brief

Use the least prompt needed. Keep adult preparation and exact-word scribing separate from pupil authorship. Guidance is hidden from the learner surface by default.
