Check the records before you believe a story, then cost only what was used.

  1. Gauge: 2,000 mL before service. That rules out Account B (1,820 mL).
  2. Cups: nine cups held 210–230 mL (mean 220 mL). 9 × 220 = 1,980 mL, plus 20 mL left = 2,000 mL. Generous pours lost the tenth drink.
  3. Stock: 10 of 50 cups = ⅕ of £5.00 = £1.00. The other 40 stay in stock.

Step 1 of 5 · Organise like a business spec

Instead of scrappy sums, a proper 5-column costing table separates pack price from amount used — and always includes packaging.

Think, pair, share: What happens to our profit figure if we forget the domed cups row?

packaging overhead = cups, lids, straws, bags or pots

Step 2 of 5 · Proportional unit splitting

Unused stock stays in the cupboard for the next batch, so we charge only the fraction consumed (£10 ÷ 5 = £2.00).

Quick check: If a pack holds 40 portions and we make 10, what fraction do we divide by?

amount used = pack price × fraction used

Step 3 of 5 · Batch total & break-even floor

Batch total is £6.00. Dividing by 10 gives 60p per cup — our break-even floor (selling at 60p makes £0.00).

Think, pair, share: Why must pennies be converted cleanly (50p = £0.50) before adding on a calculator?

break-even floor = minimum price to cover costs (£0 profit)

Step 4 of 5 · Think like a café manager

At £1.50, just 4 sales pay back the entire £6.00 batch (4 × £1.50 = £6.00). Every cup sold from #5 to #10 is pure profit.

Stretch check: Why does a buffer above break-even protect us if 1 cup gets spilled?

gross margin % = (gross profit ÷ takings) × 100

Step 5 of 5 · High-status evidence

  • In your book or Studio: a clean 5-column table + a 2–3 sentence commercial pitch beats 6 pages of fill-in-the-gap worksheets.
  • Quote exact numbers: batch cost, break-even floor, selling price, and profit on 10.

House rule: calculators allowed; verify 1 real pack price online (zero shop names) + exact Contains: allergens.
