Opening · reference

Why the bus boycott happened and what changed

Explain causes and consequences of a protest movement.

Enquiry: Why did the boycott happen, and what followed?

  • I can sort causes, the trigger and consequences.
  • I can make my own diagram with clear links.
  • I can explain reasons and results in a short paragraph.

In Montgomery, Alabama, USA, racist bus rules kept Black and White riders apart. Black residents and local groups had challenged unfair treatment for years. Rosa Parks was arrested on 1 December 1955. The boycott began on 5 December. Protest, shared work and a legal case all mattered.

You may point, write, type, speak privately or direct a scribe. Reading aloud and public answers are optional. You may pass.

These cards are new factual teaching summaries, written in 2026. No old text, speech, testimony or picture is copied. A–H are the sort set. P1 is a process note to use beside the sort.

E New ways to travel

During the boycott, 1955–1956

Many people walked or shared car rides instead of using buses. Volunteers arranged lifts and raised funds. Staying off buses changed daily travel. Shared help also made it easier to keep the protest going.

Research basis: H1, H3, H5.

A An arrest

1 December 1955

Rosa Parks was arrested after refusing to give up her bus seat. Organisers called for people to stay off buses on 5 December. This event helped set off the boycott.

Research basis: H1.

G A model for later protests

After the boycott, in later years

The boycott showed how people could keep a joint protest going without using violence. Later rights campaigns could draw on that example. It was one influence among many.

Research basis: H1, H3.

C Unfair bus rules

Before December 1955

Black passengers faced racist seating rules and unfair treatment on Montgomery buses. These problems had lasted for years. They gave people reasons to seek change.

Research basis: H1, H2.

H Fewer bus fares

During the boycott, 1955–1956

When many passengers stopped using buses, the bus company took in less fare money. This put pressure on the company. It did not, by itself, change the law.

Research basis: H5.

D Links that lasted

After the boycott, in later years

The campaign built links among people and groups working for civil rights. Those links helped support joint work beyond Montgomery in the years that followed.

Research basis: H3.

F Earlier calls for change

1954, before the boycott

The Women’s Political Council asked for fairer bus treatment before 1955. It made requests in 1954. Little changed. These problems gave people a reason to seek stronger action.

Research basis: H1, H2.

B A change to bus seating

20–21 December 1956

After the court order reached Montgomery, the boycott ended on 20 December. People returned to buses on 21 December with the old seating rule removed. The legal case helped secure this change.

Research basis: H1, H4.

P1 A legal case alongside the protest

5 June and 13 November 1956

Four Black women continued the court case Browder v. Gayle. Federal judges ruled against bus segregation on 5 June. The US Supreme Court upheld the ruling on 13 November. The order reached Montgomery in December.

P1 is not a ninth sort card. Link this legal process to card B. Refusing bus travel did not itself overturn the law.

Research basis: H4.

Word help · reference

Words for causes and effects

Open word help
cause
A reason why something happened.
long-term cause
A problem or condition that built up over time.
trigger
An event that helps set off action.
consequence
An effect or result of an event or action.
short term
During or near the time of the campaign.
long term
Continuing into later years.
boycott
Not using or buying something as a protest.
segregation
Rules that keep groups apart unfairly.
legal case
An issue taken to a court for a decision.
link
An explanation of how one thing affected another.
limit
What a change did not achieve.
MIA
The group that helped lead the bus boycott.

Adult preparation

Adult notes

Read Teacher_and_Cover_Plan and Staff_Answers before teaching.

Teach racism factually and calmly. No copied slurs, discriminatory seating, victim simulation, role play or personal disclosure. Do not ask pupils to defend racist rules or imagine being a targeted person. Historical names and dates are allowed; no living private identities or local school details are used.

Agree the quiet signal, usual supervising adult, safe place and return check before starting. Keep all pupils supervised. If the sole adult cannot safely supervise separate spaces, pause the group or offer a calm area in the room while using the normal support route.

Acknowledge distress without probing. Stop the demand and offer the agreed pause without sanction or a public explanation. Follow existing safeguarding arrangements for disclosures. Do not require a catch-up discussion while a pupil is upset.

Stop racist or flippant remarks calmly and firmly, name the harm without repeating a slur, correct the fact and reset respectful language. Check those affected and follow up privately. Use school procedures for repeated, targeted or serious incidents; do not stage a debate about whether racism is valid.

Offer the neutral task and a private check when ready. Record deferred work as not yet evidenced. A pause task or partner’s answer does not establish the history outcome.

Offer to the Communication portfolio. Keep the pupil’s own idea, an actual adult audience reply and one NS. The pupil responds in the same colour, on the same page or usual mode before R. E follows actual evidence banking. A shared answer cannot establish every pupil’s understanding.

Read exact words as access; record reasoning prompts separately. A–H are the eight sort cards. P1 is a process note. Keep each pupil’s own diagram and paragraph. A shared sort is not evidence of every pupil’s understanding.
