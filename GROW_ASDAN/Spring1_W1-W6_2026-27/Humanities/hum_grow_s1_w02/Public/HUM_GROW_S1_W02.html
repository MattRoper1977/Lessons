Opening · reference

The Montgomery bus boycott

Use sources to investigate a campaign for rights and change.

Enquiry: What helped the campaign change bus rights?

  • I can find facts in short source summaries.
  • I can name a maker and say what a source can show.
  • I can use two sources to support my own conclusion.

In Montgomery, Alabama, USA, racist bus rules kept Black and White riders apart. Black residents had challenged these rules for years. Rosa Parks was arrested on 1 December 1955 after refusing to give up her seat. The bus boycott began on 5 December. Many people worked together to seek change.

You may point, write, type, speak privately or direct a scribe. Reading aloud and public answers are optional. You may pass.

Read the other cards with the adult to add to the enquiry. These summaries are not the old records themselves.

These five cards are new teaching summaries. The lesson writer made them in 2026. No old text or picture is copied. The record or history behind each card has its own maker and purpose.

S1 A call to stay off buses

Early December 1955 | Campaign leaflet

Jo Ann Robinson and other organisers helped spread a leaflet. It asked Black residents to avoid the buses on Monday 5 December. The first call was for one day. The protest then grew into a much longer boycott.

Maker behind this card: Women’s Political Council members and local leaders.

Purpose: To get people to join a protest.

Ask: Does a call to act prove that everyone joined?

Research basis: H1, H2.

S2 The early demands

About 8 December 1955 | Letter to the bus company

The MIA was the group that led the boycott. Its early demands included polite treatment, seats taken in order of arrival, and Black drivers on mainly Black routes. The seating plan still placed Black riders from the back and White riders from the front. These demands did not yet seek fully mixed seating.

Maker behind this card: MIA leaders, Martin Luther King Jr and U. J. Fields.

Purpose: To ask the bus company to change its rules.

Ask: Does a list of demands prove they were met?

Research basis: H1, H3.

S3 Keeping the protest going

A later history, updated in 2022, about 1955–1956 | History article

Many people walked or shared car rides so they could stay off buses. Volunteers drove and helped arrange lifts. The MIA raised money to support this work. The campaign needed many people and steady effort, not just one famous leader.

Maker behind this card: US National Park Service.

Purpose: To explain how the boycott worked.

Ask: Can a short history tell us about every journey?

Research basis: H4, H1.

S4 The court ruling

5 June and 13 November 1956 | Court decisions described in a later history

Four Black women challenged bus segregation in the case Browder v. Gayle. On 5 June, federal judges ruled against the segregation laws. On 13 November, the US Supreme Court upheld that ruling. The legal case mattered as well as the boycott.

Maker behind this card: Courts made the rulings; the King Institute wrote the later history.

Purpose: To explain court decisions about bus segregation.

Ask: Does a court ruling tell us how every rider was treated?

Research basis: H5, H4.

S5 A call to end the boycott

20 December 1956 | Prepared public statement

The court order had reached Montgomery. King announced that the boycott was ending. He asked Black residents to return to buses the next morning with the seating rule removed. His call shows what the leaders wanted people to do next. It does not, by itself, show every bus journey.

Maker behind this card: Martin Luther King Jr for the MIA.

Purpose: To announce the next step after the court order arrived.

Ask: How could we check whether the plan happened?

Research basis: H6.

Adult brief · reference

Adult preparation

Adult notes

Read Teacher_and_Cover_Plan and Staff_Answers before teaching.

Teach racism factually and calmly. No copied slurs, discriminatory seating, victim simulation, role play or personal disclosure. Do not ask pupils to defend racist rules or imagine being a targeted person. Historical names and dates are allowed; no living private identities or local school details are used.

Agree the quiet signal, usual supervising adult, safe place and return check before starting. Keep all pupils supervised. If the sole adult cannot safely supervise separate spaces, pause the group or offer a calm area in the room while using the normal support route.

Acknowledge distress without probing. Stop the demand and offer the agreed pause without sanction or a public explanation. Follow existing safeguarding arrangements for disclosures. Do not require a catch-up discussion while a pupil is upset.

Stop racist or flippant remarks calmly and firmly, name the harm without repeating a slur, correct the fact and reset respectful language. Check those affected and follow up privately. Use school procedures for repeated, targeted or serious incidents; do not stage a debate about whether racism is valid.

Offer the neutral task and a private check when ready. Record deferred work as not yet evidenced. A pause task or partner’s answer does not establish the history outcome.

Offer to the Communication portfolio. Keep the pupil’s own idea, an actual adult audience reply and one NS. The pupil responds in the same colour, on the same page or usual mode before R. E follows actual evidence banking. A shared answer cannot establish every pupil’s understanding.

Read exact wording as access; record reasoning prompts separately. A statement of aims is not proof of results. The core finish is three source checks plus a conclusion using two codes and one limit. No live archive, copied words or images are needed.
