Opening · reference

An account of the Montgomery bus boycott

Plan and write a structured historical account.

Enquiry: How did the campaign grow, and why did it matter?

  • I can plan all five parts of the campaign account.
  • I can draft events in date order and explain reasons and results.
  • I can explain why it mattered, with evidence and a limit.

The Montgomery bus boycott took place in Alabama, USA, in 1955–1956. Black riders faced racist seating rules. Earlier requests, Parks’s arrest, shared protest and a separate legal case all help explain the campaign. Your account will link the setting, reasons, events, results and why it mattered.

You may point, write, type, speak privately or direct a scribe. Reading aloud and public answers are optional. You may pass.

A–F are new factual teaching summaries based on our earlier lessons. No old text, speech, testimony or picture is copied. The model opening is newly written by the lesson author. It is not a quote.

A A setting and reasons for change

Before December 1955; requests in 1954

Montgomery bus rules treated Black riders unfairly. These rules had lasted for years. In 1954, the Women’s Political Council asked for fairer bus treatment. Little changed. These grievances gave people reasons to protest.

Earlier learning: Week 2 S1; Week 3 C/F.

B An arrest helped set off action

1 December 1955

Rosa Parks refused to give up her seat on a Montgomery bus. Police arrested her. Organisers then called for people to stay off buses on 5 December. The arrest was a trigger, not the only cause.

Earlier learning: Week 2 context; Week 3 A; Week 4 B.

C Many people sustained the boycott

From 5 December 1955, during 1955–1956

The boycott began on 5 December 1955. Many Black residents avoided the buses. They walked or shared rides. Volunteers and local groups raised funds and arranged lifts. Shared effort helped the campaign last.

Earlier learning: Week 2 S1/S3; Week 3 E; Week 4 C.

D A separate legal route

5 June and 13 November 1956

Four Black women continued the case Browder v. Gayle. Parks was not a plaintiff. Federal judges ruled against bus segregation on 5 June. The US Supreme Court upheld the ruling on 13 November. Legal action mattered alongside protest.

Earlier learning: Week 2 S4; Week 3 P1; Week 4 D.

E The boycott ended and buses changed

20 and 21 December 1956

After the court order reached Montgomery, the boycott ended on 20 December. People returned to integrated buses on 21 December 1956. The bus seating rule had changed. This did not end racism or every unfair rule.

Earlier learning: Week 2 S5; Week 3 B; Week 4 D.

F Why the campaign matters

After December 1956, in later years

The campaign showed how shared protest could last. Its networks and example helped later civil rights work. Parks’s part mattered, but so did many residents, organisers and the court case. This was one influence among many, not the end of racism.

Earlier learning: Week 3 D/G; Week 4 C/D.

Choose one route. Keep your own five-part plan and drafted account in your book, typed or exactly scribed as you direct.

Supported

  1. Use the planner and all six cards with exact adult reading if helpful. Plan one heading at a time in your book, using key words and codes.
  2. Use the dates bank to place at least three events in order. Keep a cause, the court case, results and a limit.
  3. Use the frame or direct a scribe for five very short paragraphs. Keep your own ideas. Make one edit and say why.

Independent

  1. Make a five-part plan with key facts and card codes. Select dates and connective words that help your explanation.
  2. Draft five short paragraphs. Explain causes, ordered events, results and significance. Keep the court route separate and the shared work in view.
  3. Check the dates, because links and limit. Improve one part and give a reason. No fixed word count is needed.

Aspire

  1. Keep the same plan and five-part draft. Link paragraphs so the reader can follow how the campaign developed.
  2. Weigh shared protest and legal action without treating either as the only cause. Explain how daily travel changed and also helped sustain action.
  3. Make the significance precise: one influence among many. Revise an overclaim or weak link, then explain the improvement.

Plan in any language. The adult may read cards exactly. Keep a note of any help with your reasoning.

Word help · reference

Words for a historical account

Open word help
account
A piece of writing explaining what happened and why it mattered.
background
The setting that helps us understand events.
cause
A reason why something happened.
trigger
An event that helps set off action.
chronology
Events placed in time order.
consequence
An effect or result of an event or action.
significance
How and why something matters in history.
paragraph
A group of sentences about one main part of the account.
connective
A word or phrase that links ideas.
boycott
Not using or buying something as a protest.
segregation
Rules that keep groups apart unfairly.
plaintiff
A person who brings a case to court.

First, next and later show order. Because and as a result need a supported link. A later date alone does not prove that one event caused another.

Adult brief · reference

Adult preparation

Adult notes

Read Teacher_and_Cover_Plan and Staff_Answers before teaching.

Teach racism factually and calmly. No copied slurs, discriminatory seating, victim simulation, role play or personal disclosure. Do not ask pupils to defend racist rules or imagine being a targeted person. Historical names and dates are allowed; no living private identities or local school details are used.

Agree the quiet signal, usual supervising adult, safe place and return check before starting. Keep all pupils supervised. If the sole adult cannot safely supervise separate spaces, pause the group or offer a calm area in the room while using the normal support route.

Acknowledge distress without probing. Stop the demand and offer the agreed pause without sanction or a public explanation. Follow existing safeguarding arrangements for disclosures. Do not require a catch-up discussion while a pupil is upset.

Stop racist or flippant remarks calmly and firmly, name the harm without repeating a slur, correct the fact and reset respectful language. Check those affected and follow up privately. Use school procedures for repeated, targeted or serious incidents; do not stage a debate about whether racism is valid.

Offer the neutral task and a private check when ready. Record deferred work as not yet evidenced. A pause task or partner’s answer does not establish the history outcome.

Offer to the Communication portfolio. Keep the pupil’s own idea, an actual adult audience reply and one NS. The pupil responds in the same colour, on the same page or usual mode before R. E follows actual evidence banking. A shared answer cannot establish every pupil’s understanding.

Read exact words as access; record reasoning prompts separately. Keep each pupil’s own plan and account. A shared answer does not show each pupil’s understanding. This is drafting practice, not the Week 6 assessment.
