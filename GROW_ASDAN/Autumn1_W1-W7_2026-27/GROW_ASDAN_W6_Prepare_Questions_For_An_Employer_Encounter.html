An open question buys you an answer

Next week an employer visits our class community project.

You get about ten minutes with them. Your questions have to work first time.

A closed question can be answered with yes or no.

An open question makes the visitor explain something to you.

Open questions start with what, how or why.

Say the ten-minute limit out loud. The time pressure is what makes the open-question rule worth learning, so do not skip it.

You need three things on the desk. The employer card, the topic list and your project log.

The topic list tells you what this visitor has agreed to talk about.

If a question is not on a topic from that list, it does not get asked.

Ten minutes, two kinds of question

Here are two questions asked at last term's visit.

The first was: do you like your job? The answer was yes.

That took four seconds and gave the class nothing to write down.

The second was: what does a normal Monday look like for you?

The visitor talked for two minutes and named six different tasks.

The same visitor and the same ten minutes gave a very different amount of evidence.

The second question was not cleverer, only harder to answer in one word.

  • 1 · read the employer card.
  • 2 · pick a topic from the approved list.
  • 3 · open with what, how or why.
  • 4 · check that yes or no cannot finish it.

Building one question from the card

I take the employer card for the site manager and read all three of its duties.

Now I pick one topic from the approved list. I pick training.

Next I choose my opening word. I choose how, because how asks for a process.

My question is: how did you learn to do this job?

Then I run the check. Could the visitor answer that with yes or no?

They could not, so it stays on the log.

Watch what I did not ask: nothing about the visitor's family or their pay.

The card and the list are the guard, and they tell me what this person came to talk about.

Model the check out loud, including the moment of doubt. Pupils copy the steps they see you do, not the ones you describe.

One more thing about the check. Say the yes answer in your head, and if it sounds like a real reply the question is still closed.

Sort them, then match them

Eight question cards are on your table. Read them all first.

Sort the eight cards into two groups: open and closed.

Turning a closed question open

You do not throw a closed question away. You repair it.

Take this one: do you work weekends? That earns a yes and stops.

Change the opening word. Ask: what does a weekend shift involve?

The topic did not change, only the first word.

There is a second repair: add and why to the end.

Now ask: what does a weekend shift involve, and why is it different?

That question gets you a task list and a reason in one go.

Be careful with why on its own. Why can sound like a complaint.

Put why after a what, and it sounds like interest instead.

  • Say the question out loud once.
  • Answer it yourself in one word.
  • If that worked, change the opening word.
  • Ask it again and listen for a longer reply.

Test a question on your partner

Work in twos. Read one of your questions to your partner.

Your partner answers it as briefly as they honestly can.

If one word finished it, change your opening word and ask them again.

Then swap over. You answer theirs in the same short way.

Do not be kind here. A one-word answer now saves a wasted question on the day.

Your partner answers as the worker on the card, not as themselves.

Four questions, checked and ordered

Work on your own now. Write four questions on the project log.

Each one uses a topic from the approved list. Write that topic beside it.

Each one starts with what, how or why.

Run the yes-or-no check on all four and tick the box.

Use four different topics. Four questions about training is one question asked four times.

Then number them one to four, with your best question first.

Ten minutes may only fit two of them, so the order matters.

  • Supported · two questions from the topic list, both checked.
  • Standard · four questions on four topics, all checked and numbered in order.
  • Stretch · as Standard, and add the follow-up you would ask after question one.

A follow-up is the question you ask because of the answer you were given.

If you finish early, repair one closed card from the We Do pile.
