Arrival Task

Arrival Task – What Do You Already Know?

Instructions: Answer in your books from memory. This activates what you already know about electricity before we start.

1. 🟢

Name three things in your home that use electricity.

Any electrical appliance: TV, phone charger, lamp, kettle, fridge, games console, etc.

2. 🟢

What do all electrical devices need to work? Think about what they are plugged into or what powers them.

They need a power source (battery, mains supply) and a complete circuit for electricity to flow through.

3. 🟢

What happens when you flip a light switch to the off position?

The circuit is broken (there is a gap), so electricity can no longer flow and the light goes off.

4. Lead-in ⚡

If you could look inside a wire, what do you think is actually moving when electricity flows?

Answers vary. Accept: electrons, tiny charged particles, electrical charge. The key idea is that something physical is moving through the wire.

1. 🟠

Name four components you might find in an electrical circuit.

Any from: cell/battery, wire, switch, lamp/bulb, resistor, ammeter, voltmeter, motor, buzzer, LED.

2. 🟠

Explain why a torch stops working when you remove one of the batteries.

Removing a battery breaks the circuit — there is no longer a complete loop for the current to flow through, so the lamp goes off.

3. 🟠

What is the difference between a cell and a battery?

A cell is a single unit that provides voltage (e.g. one AA). A battery is two or more cells connected together.

4. Lead-in ⚡

Why do you think scientists use symbols for circuit components instead of drawing pictures?

Symbols are quick to draw, universally understood, consistent, and make circuit diagrams clear and easy to read anywhere in the world.

1. 🔵

Draw, from memory, a simple circuit containing a cell, a switch, and a lamp. Use any symbols you know.

A closed loop with standard symbols: cell (long and short line), switch (line with gap), lamp (circle with cross).

2. 🔵

Explain the difference between a circuit where two lamps are in a single loop versus a circuit where they are on separate branches.

Single loop (series): both lamps on the same path, if one breaks both go out, they share the voltage so are dimmer. Separate branches (parallel): each lamp has its own path, if one breaks the other stays on, each gets full voltage.

3. 🔵

Christmas tree lights used to all go out when one bulb broke. Modern ones don't. Explain why, using what you know about circuits.

Old lights were wired in series — one break stops all current. Modern lights are wired in parallel — each bulb has its own path, so others keep working when one fails.

4. Lead-in ⚡

Why do you think there are strict rules for drawing circuit diagrams? What could go wrong if everyone drew them differently?

Without standard rules, diagrams would be ambiguous — different people could interpret them differently, leading to incorrectly built circuits, safety hazards, or communication errors between engineers and scientists worldwide.

📐 The 4 Golden Rules

1. Always draw straight lines with 90° corners (no curves).

2. Use standard symbols — never pictures.

3. The circuit must be a complete loop (no gaps).

4. Components go on straight sections, never on corners.

✅ Good vs ❌ Bad

🌍 Why This Matters

Every electronic device you own — your phone, PlayStation, TV — is a circuit. Electricians, engineers, and technicians use these exact symbols every day. If you can read a circuit diagram, you can fix things, build things, and understand how your world works.

🌟 Why standard symbols? An engineer in Japan, a scientist in Brazil, and a student in London all use the same symbols. It's a universal language for circuits.

I Do: Series vs Parallel Circuits (2.12)

Learning Objective: Understand the differences between series and parallel circuits.

🔴 Series Circuit

One single path for current to flow through.

• Current is the same everywhere.

• Voltage is shared between components.

• If one component breaks → all stop.

🔵 Parallel Circuit

Multiple paths (branches) for current.

• Voltage is the same across each branch.

• Current splits between branches.

• If one branch breaks → others keep working.

💡 Real World: Houses are wired in parallel so each appliance gets the full voltage (230 V) and you can switch them on/off independently. Old Christmas lights were in series — one bulb out = all out!

I Do: Ammeters & Voltmeters

Key Idea: Ammeters go IN SERIES (in the loop). Voltmeters go IN PARALLEL (across a component).

Ⓐ Ammeter — Measures Current

• Connected in series (part of the loop).

• All the current flows through it.

• Unit: Amps (A).

• Think: Ammeter = Amps = in the loop like A chain.

Ⓥ Voltmeter — Measures Voltage

• Connected in parallel (across a component).

• Measures the energy difference across the component.

• Unit: Volts (V).

• Think: Voltmeter sits on a V-shaped bridge across the component.

Where Do They Go?

🌟 Common mistake: Putting a voltmeter in series or an ammeter in parallel. An ammeter in parallel can cause a short circuit — very dangerous!

Exit Ticket – Check Your Learning

Instructions: Answer independently. No notes.
🟢 I'm confident — I could teach this
🟡 Getting there — need more practice
🔴 I'm stuck — I need help

1) Draw the circuit symbol for a lamp.

Circle with a cross inside.

2) Is this series or parallel? "Two lamps on one single loop."

Series.

3) What happens in a series circuit if one lamp breaks?

All the lamps go out because the circuit is broken.

1) State how an ammeter and a voltmeter must be connected.

Ammeter in series (in the loop). Voltmeter in parallel (across the component).

2) Give two differences between series and parallel circuits.

Series: one loop, current same everywhere, voltage shared, one break stops all. Parallel: branches, voltage same across each, current splits, one break doesn't affect others.

3) State two of the four golden rules for drawing circuit diagrams.

Any two from: straight lines with 90° corners, standard symbols, complete loop, components on straight sections.

1) Explain why placing an ammeter in parallel across a battery is dangerous.

An ammeter has very low resistance. In parallel, it creates a path with almost no resistance — a short circuit. A huge current flows, which can overheat wires, damage the ammeter, or start a fire.

2) Explain why houses are wired in parallel. Give two reasons.

1. Each appliance gets the full mains voltage (230 V). 2. Appliances can be switched on/off independently — a fault in one doesn't affect others.

3) A student connects 3 lamps in series. They are dim. She reconnects them in parallel and they are brighter. Explain why.

In series, total resistance = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ (higher), so less current flows and voltage is shared. In parallel, each lamp gets the full battery voltage and has its own path, so more current flows through each — they glow brighter.
