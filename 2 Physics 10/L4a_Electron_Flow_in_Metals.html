We draw current flowing + to −, but electrons really flow the other way. Why do scientists still use the old direction?

A) electrons really do flow + to −B) they agreed + to − before electrons were discovered, and kept it as the standardC) it makes the maths easier

B) conventional current (+ to −) was agreed before electrons were discovered, so it became the global standard

1. 🔵

A hairdryer draws 10 A on UK mains (230 V). Calculate its resistance. Show working.

R = V ÷ I = 230 ÷ 10 = 23 Ω

2. 🔵

Explain why increasing the voltage across a fixed resistor increases the current, using ideas about charge.

Higher voltage gives more energy per coulomb, pushing electrons faster through the circuit. Since current is the rate of flow of charge, faster electron flow = higher current. R stays constant, so by V = IR, if V↑ then I↑.

3. 🔵

Two resistors of 10 Ω and 20 Ω are in series with a 6 V battery. Calculate the current.

R_total = 10 + 20 = 30 Ω. I = V ÷ R = 6 ÷ 30 = 0.2 A

4. Lead-in ⚡

In the circuit above, the voltage across the 10 Ω resistor is 2 V and across the 20 Ω it's 4 V. What do you notice when you add these together? Why?

2 + 4 = 6 V, which equals the battery voltage. In series, the voltages across each component add up to the total supply voltage.

💡 Did You Know?

An electron in a wire actually moves incredibly slowly — about 0.1 mm per second (slower than a snail!). But the electrical signal travels at nearly the speed of light because each electron pushes the next one, like dominoes.

I Do

I Do: Electron Flow in Metals (2.16)

Key Idea: Electric current in solid metallic conductors is a flow of negatively charged electrons.

⚡ What Actually Moves?

Inside a metal wire, there are billions of free electrons — they are not locked to any single atom.

When a battery provides voltage, it pushes these free electrons through the wire — this is current.

Electrons are negatively charged, so they flow from the negative terminal to the positive terminal.

🔄 Two Directions!

Conventional current: + → − (what we draw on diagrams)

Electron flow: − → + (what actually happens)

Scientists agreed on conventional current before discovering electrons, and it stuck.

🌍 Why This Matters

When engineers design fairy lights, phone chargers, or car dashboards, they need to know exactly how voltage is shared between components in series. Get the calculation wrong and the component burns out — or doesn't work at all. This is real engineering maths.

💡 Why do metals conduct? Metal atoms are arranged in a lattice. The outer electrons are delocalised (free to move). Non-metals like plastic don't have free electrons, so they are insulators.

Inside a Metal Wire

If the question says "describe the current in a metal wire" — say: "a flow of negatively charged electrons". This is the exact spec wording worth 1 mark.
⚠️ Misconception alert: "The lamp uses up the electrons."
Truth: Electrons are never used up — the same electrons keep looping round the circuit. It's energy that gets transferred to the lamp.

🔑 Keywords to Know

ElectronA tiny negatively-charged particle. Carries current in metals.
ChargeA property of particles. Measured in coulombs (C).
Conventional currentThe "pretend" flow from + to − (opposite to electrons).
SeriesComponents on a single loop. Current same, voltages add.
