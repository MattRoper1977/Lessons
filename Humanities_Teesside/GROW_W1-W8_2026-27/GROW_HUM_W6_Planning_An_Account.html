Planning an account

GROW Humanities · Autumn 1 · Week 6 · 40 minutes

Today we put the parts of an account in order, and rank our evidence before we write a word.

3 min
Arrival · retrieve

Retrieving what an account needs

Last week you judged how significant something was. This week you plan the account that carries the judgement.

The four parts are: claim, evidence, explanation, conclusion.

Arrival decision: Take the four part cards and the three evidence cards from the envelope. Everyone starts with the same seven.

In the envelope are four part cards and three evidence cards. The part cards say claim, evidence, explanation and conclusion. The evidence cards are a photograph with a date, a line from a newspaper, and a table of numbers.

Have every learner spread all seven cards before any is placed. A plan built card by card tends to end up in the order they came out of the envelope.

3 min
Starter · create the need

Two plans for the same account

Two plans, same event, same three sources.

Plan A: evidence, evidence, evidence, claim.

Plan B: claim, strongest evidence, explanation, conclusion.

Plan B tells a reader what it is arguing in the first line. Plan A makes them wait, and then hope they agree.

Two things separate them. The order of the parts, and which evidence went first. Both are decided before any writing starts.

Try it: look at your three evidence cards. Which one would you put first? Hold that. Do not move it yet.

Do not name the strong card. Which card is strongest, and why, is the ranking the learners are here to make.

4 min
I Do · model

The order of the four parts

The diagram shows the four parts in order, with an arrow underneath. The claim comes first so the reader knows what is being argued.

The conclusion is not a summary. It answers so what: what the reader should now think that they did not think before.

Modelled: claim, the dock changed the town. Evidence, the table of numbers. Explanation, four thousand jobs in one place pulls houses and shops after it. Conclusion, so the street plan we still use was set by one decision.

Account is a piece of writing that argues one thing and backs it up.

Read the modelled plan aloud twice, once with the explanation and once without. Hearing the gap is faster than being told about it.

3 min
We Do · order and rank

Sequence the parts, then rank the evidence

Card A: a photograph with a date on the back. What can it prove on its own?

Card B: one line from a newspaper. Who wrote it and when?

Card C: a table of numbers over ten years. What does the shape of it show?

Order first: put the four part cards in order along the table, then rank the three evidence cards, strongest first. Everyone commits at the same time, and then we hold up the top card together.

There is no single right ranking. There is a ranking you can defend. When we check, we ask what work the card does for the claim, not which card looks most impressive.

Take the top card from a learner who has not spoken today. Scribe the reason exactly; the reason is the assessed part, not the order.

3 min
I Do 2 · connect

What makes one piece of evidence stronger

Look at the picture. Three tests decide the ranking. Close to the event. Wide enough to cover time. Hard for somebody to explain away.

The strongest card is usually the one that scores on two tests, not the one that looks most striking.

Ranked together: the table opens, because it is wide and hard to argue with. The photograph goes second, because it is close. The newspaper line goes last until we know who wrote it.

Keep the tests on the table. A learner ranking by how interesting a card is has used a real reason; name which test it is not.

We fill one grid as a class. Everyone writes the explanation box on a whiteboard first, then we hold up the boards at the same time.

Claim and evidence are already on the board. Your job is the explanation: why does that card support that claim?

Scribe exactly. The explanation in the learner own words is the assessed evidence for this outcome.

16 min
Independent · evidence

Your record: a plan somebody else could use

Produce one plan grid. The test is whether another person could write the account from it without asking you a question.

◆ Supported

Four boxes filled with card letters and a scribed reason for the top card.

▲ Standard

Four boxes in sentences, with the evidence ranked and one reason written.

★ Stretch

Four boxes in sentences, ranking with reasons, and what the weakest card would need to become the strongest.

Check yourself: is your claim in the first box, not the last?

Check yourself: does your explanation box say why, not what?

Check yourself: can you defend the card you put first?

A finished grid: claim, the dock changed the town. Evidence, card C, the ten-year table. Explanation, four thousand jobs in one place pulls houses and shops after them. Conclusion, the street plan we use was set by one decision.

Record route and scribing on the evidence line, keeping adult support visible and separate from the learner own words.

4 min
Exit · Audience and Influence

Audience receives; influence changes something

Give your plan to a partner. They write the first sentence of the account from it and read it back.

Did the sentence argue something other than your claim? Change one box so the plan says what you meant. That change is your influence.

Next week you write the account from this plan and assess it.

Close by naming one learner whose plan changed after a partner wrote from it. That is the influence record.
