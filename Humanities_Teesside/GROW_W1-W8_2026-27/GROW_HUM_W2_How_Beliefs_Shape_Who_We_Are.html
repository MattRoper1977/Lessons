Staff and source drawer: profile cards come from the school-approved local source pack; the set holds Christian, Muslim, Sikh, Hindu, Jewish, Buddhist and non-religious cards in no fixed order, so no order suggests a rank, and each card states a belief and what the person does because of it in plain words.

Arrival Task

Arrival Task – What is identity?

Nobody is asked what they believe. Every task uses the supplied profile cards. You can put every chain in order without saying a word about your own home. Nothing you say leaves this room.
You can work from the strips alone. You can move the strips instead of writing. You can dictate to an adult and the words stay yours. You never have to say what you believe. Each one is a full route.
Identity means the things that make you who you are: what you do, what you care about, and how other people know you.

Identity is who you are. It is made of many parts: family, friends, hobbies, home town, and what you believe.

A belief can shape a choice. A choice made again and again becomes a habit. A habit becomes part of who you are.

Arrival decision: Pick up one profile card from the set on your table. Every card gives a role, a belief and what the person does, and never a name.

On the table are eight profile cards from the local source pack. A nurse who fasts in Ramadan. A bus driver who goes to church every Sunday. A shopkeeper who wears a turban. A student who believes in fairness and gives up a Saturday to help at a food bank. Each card gives a belief and what the person does, and nothing else.

Offer the strips first. A learner who says the card does not tell them what the person feels inside is right; the chain describes what a belief shapes, not what a person is. Do not ask any learner which card is closest to their own home.

Starter

Starter: One belief, one week

Picture a shopkeeper in a Teesside town. The card says the shopkeeper believes that every person is equal in the eyes of the divine.

In one week: the shopkeeper wears a turban. The shopkeeper keeps a free kitchen at the place of worship, open to anyone. Every customer is served the same way.

The question: which of those things comes from the belief, and which order do they go in?

Most people spot all three. The belief comes first. The choices come next. Done every week, they become habits. And the habits are how the town knows this shopkeeper.

That is the skill for today. Find the belief, then follow it in order: belief, choice, habit, part of who they are. The belief is on the card. The chain is what you build.

Try it: cover the card. Say the belief from memory, then say one choice that comes from it. Then uncover the card and check the order.

Keep the chain on the card. If a learner starts to build a chain about a classmate, redirect to the card: the task is about how a belief shapes a life, never about who in the room believes what.

I Do

I Do: From belief to who you are

Look at the picture. Four boxes, one chain. The belief is what the person holds to be true. The choice is one thing they decide to do because of it. The habit is that choice made again and again. The last box is how other people know them.

A chain with all four links in order is a description of how a belief shapes identity. That is the outcome of today.

Modelled: the nurse. Belief: fasting in Ramadan brings a person closer to the divine and to people who go hungry. Choice: no food or drink from dawn to sunset for a month. Habit: every year, the same month, the same fast. Who I am: the nurse is known at work as the one who plans the year around Ramadan and brings food in to share when it ends. Four links, one order.

Describe means to say what happens and in what order, so that someone else could follow it.
Model the non-religious card with the same care as the religious ones. Learners who only see faith chains learn that no religion means no beliefs, which is not true of most people in this area.

We Do

We Do: Put the strips in order

Strip A: decides to stop eating meat, and keeps to it every day of the year.

Strip B: is known by friends as the one who brings the best vegetable dishes to every party.

Strip C: believes that all living things deserve care and should not be harmed.

Sequence first, then rank: put the three strips in order on your whiteboard, belief first and who they are last. Then rank the three parts of daily life this belief shapes most: food, days, or how the person treats others. Everyone sequences, then we hold up the boards together and build the class chain as one.

Evidence check

The order must run belief, then choice and habit, then who they are. A strip that starts with “believes” goes first, and the strip that says how others know the person goes last. The rank is of parts of daily life, never of beliefs.

Take the first chain from a learner who has not spoken today. Scribe the order and the rank exactly; the wording is the evidence. Do not ask anyone whether a card matches their own home.

I Do

I Do: One part among many

The diagram above shows one person with six boxes inside. Belief is one box. The arrows show it shaping two others: food and days, and how the person treats people.

Two people can hold the same belief and the arrows can point to different boxes. One shows it in food, one in how they treat others. Both are that belief, lived.

Said together: a belief is one part of who a person is. It shapes choices, the choices become habits, and the habits are how other people come to know that person. The same belief can be lived in more than one way.

Hold the equal-box rule. If a learner asks which belief shapes a person most, answer that the boxes are the same size for every card in this lesson, because the outcome is to describe how a belief shapes a life, not to compare beliefs.

Scribe exactly. A chain in order and a rank in a learner own words is the assessed evidence for this outcome. Never turn the rank into a rank of beliefs; it is a rank of parts of a day.
