Arrival Task

Arrival Task – What is a world view?

Nobody is asked what they believe. Every task uses the supplied clue cards. You can label all of them without saying a word about your own home, and nothing you say leaves this room.
You can work from the word bank alone. You can point at a label instead of writing it. You can dictate to an adult and the words stay yours. You never have to say what you believe. Each one is a full route.
World view means the big ideas a person holds about life, what matters and how to live. It can be religious or not.

A belief is something a person holds to be true. A world view is the whole set of big ideas a person lives by.

A community is the people who share a place: a street, a town, a school.

Arrival decision: Pick up one clue card from the set on your table. Every card shows a building, an object or a day, and never a person.

On the table are eight clue cards from the local source pack. A building with a tall spire. A building with a dome. A hall where people meet on a Sunday with no religious symbols at all. A shop with a festival poster in the window. Each card gives a clue and nothing else.

Offer the word bank first. A learner who says they cannot know what anyone in a building believes is right; the label names the world view a place serves, not a person. Do not ask any learner which card matches their own home.

Starter

Starter: One street, many world views

Picture one street in a Teesside town.

On it: a church, a mosque, and a corner shop that sells food and lamps for a festival. There is also a hall where a walking group meets on a Sunday morning.

The question: how many different world views can you spot on that one street?

Count them. Most people find at least four. Two are religious buildings. One is a shop that serves a festival. One is a group that has chosen a Sunday walk over a service, and that is a world view too.

That is the skill for today. Spot the clue, then put the right label on it. The clue is always something you can see. The label is the world view it points to.

Try it: cover the word bank. Say one clue from the street and one label for it. Then uncover the bank and check your label is there.

Keep the labels on the buildings and objects. If a learner starts to label a classmate, redirect to the card: the task is about what the community holds, never about who holds what.

I Do

I Do: From clue to label to note

Look at the picture. Each box does one job. The clue is what you can see. The label is the world view the clue points to. The note says what the clue tells us about how people live.

A label with no note is a start. A note with no label is a guess. Both together is the full skill.

Modelled: the building with the spire. Clue: a tall spire, a cross on top, a board with service times. Label: church, Christian. Note: people gather here to worship, often on a Sunday, and the hall is used for a food bank in the week. Label from the bank, note in my own words.

Identify means to say what something is and to point to the clue that told you.
Model the non-religious card with the same care as the religious ones. Learners who only see faith buildings labelled learn that no religion is a gap rather than a world view held by many people in this area.

We Do

We Do: Label the clues on the street scene

Clue 1: a building with a dome and a tall tower, busiest on a Friday afternoon.

Clue 2: a shop with a poster for Diwali and a shelf of sweets and small lamps.

Clue 3: a hall with a notice for a Sunday morning walking group and no religious symbols.

Label first, then annotate: write one label from the word bank next to each clue on your whiteboard. Then add one short note under each label saying what the clue shows. Everyone labels, then we hold up the boards together and annotate the class street scene as one.

Evidence check

Every label must come from the word bank. Every note must point at something on the card. A note that says what a person believes is not allowed. A note that says what the building or shop is for is exactly right.

Take the first label from a learner who has not spoken today. Scribe the label and the note exactly; the wording is the evidence. Do not ask anyone whether a label matches their own home.

I Do

I Do: Many world views, one community

The diagram above shows one community with seven boxes inside it. Every box is the same size, because no world view is ranked above another.

The people in one box may be many or few. The box stays the same size either way, because the task is to identify, not to decide who is right.

Said together: our community holds many world views. Some are religious and some are not. We can tell which ones are here by the clues in our streets, and we can say what each clue shows without saying anything about any one person.

Hold the equal-box rule. If a learner asks which world view is the biggest here, answer that numbers differ from street to street and that the boxes stay the same size in this lesson, because the outcome is to identify, not to compare.

Exit Ticket

Exit Ticket — Audience receives; influence changes something

Read one label and its note to a partner. The listener says back which clue on the card told you.

Did the listener point at a different clue, or none? Change one word in your note so it points at the clue you meant. That change is your influence.

Next week you look at how a belief can shape who a person is. The labels you wrote today are where that starts.

Close by naming one learner whose note changed after their partner pointed at the wrong clue. That is the influence record. Never name any learner world view.
