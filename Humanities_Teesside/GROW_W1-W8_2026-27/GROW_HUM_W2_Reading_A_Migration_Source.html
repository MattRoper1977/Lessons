3 min
Arrival · retrieve

What we already know about sources

Last week we put events on a timeline. This week we read a source about one of them.

Every source was made by someone, at some time, for some reason. Those three facts change what it can tell us.

Arrival decision: Use the source card on the table, or choose a second card from the pack. Both are full answers. Nobody will ask which one you picked.

Before we start, look at the source card on your table. It is a reproduction of a 1950s recruitment poster inviting workers from the Caribbean to apply for jobs on London's buses. The caption tells you who made it and when. Everyone in this room can work with this card from start to end.

Offer the source card first, not as a fallback. A learner who links the poster to a relative is not prompted for more; note the part of the source they used. Wait; take the least prompt that works.

3 min
Starter · create the need

Why is looking not the same as reading?

Here are two answers about the same poster.

Answer A: “It is an old poster about buses.”

Answer B: “It is a poster made by a transport company in the 1950s, to persuade workers in the Caribbean to come and drive buses in London.”

Answer B tells you more than Answer A. Answer A describes the picture. Answer B says who made it, when, who it was for and why. That is reading a source, not just looking at it.

The need for this lesson is the label. A source has parts: the maker, the date, the audience, the purpose and the content. Each part is a label. By the end you will be able to label all five and say what they tell you.

Try it in your head: think of a poster on a wall in this school. Who put it there? Who is it for? What does it want you to do? You have just labelled three parts of a source.

Do not accept "it is an old poster" as complete and do not treat it as wrong. Name the gap: we have the content, we still need the maker, date, audience and purpose.

4 min
I Do · model

The five parts of a source

The diagram above shows the poster in the middle with five labels around it: content, maker, date, audience and purpose. The lines show which part of the source each label points to.

Notice what the diagram does not claim. It does not say the poster tells the whole story of migration. A poster is one voice, made for one reason. That is why the annotation, not just the label, matters.

Watch me do it: I read the caption. It says a transport company made it in 1956. That gives me the maker label and the date label. I read the big words: drivers wanted. That is content. The poster was shown in the Caribbean, so the audience is workers there. I label what I can read first. Then I work out the rest.

Audience means the people a source was made for.
Purpose means the reason a source was made.

Read the diagram clockwise from content, naming each label before any card is placed. A learner who labels the audience as "bus passengers" has read the picture rather than the caption; ask which part of the source told them.

3 min
We Do · label the source

Label three parts together

Part 1: the caption says a transport company made it. Which label is that?

Part 2: the big words say drivers are wanted. Which label is that?

Part 3: the poster was put up in the Caribbean. Which label is that?

Label first: write the right label next to each of the three parts on your copy of the source. Use the five labels from the diagram. Everyone labels at the same time. Then we hold the copies up and check together.

Part 3 is the one people disagree on. Some will label it audience. Some will label it purpose. Both are reading the source. When we check, we ask which part of the source you used. We do not check the word alone. A label with a pointed finger is reading.

Have every table label before any answer is taken, so nobody copies. A label changed after the check is a success, not a correction.

3 min
I Do 2 · connect

Shows, suggests, cannot tell

The diagram above has three boxes: shows, then suggests, then cannot tell. A finding you can point to on the source goes in the first box. A finding you can argue from the source goes in the middle. Anything not on the source goes in the last box.

Here is an annotation that puts a finding in the wrong box: "The poster shows that everyone who came got a good job." Nothing on the poster shows that. It belongs in the last box.

Fixing it together: move it. "The poster cannot tell us what jobs people got after they arrived." Now the annotation says only what the source can carry. The words cannot tell are the honest words for a source.

Point to each box as you say it. A learner who writes a hopeful claim under "shows" has made the most common source error; show the third box rather than marking the finding wrong.

We annotate one finding on the board first. Nobody writes yet. Everyone puts one finding on a whiteboard with its box name. Then we show them all at once. Then we agree the box.

The finding for this is about the audience. What does the poster show about who it was for? Write the finding and the box, not a describing word.

Take the finding from a learner who has not yet spoken today. Scribe exactly what is said; do not improve the wording.

16 min
Independent · evidence

Your record: reading a migration source

Make one record. You may use the poster card, or a second source card from the pack, or both. All three are complete answers.

◆ Supported

Label three parts of the source using the supplied labels. An adult may scribe your exact words, and the words stay yours. Point to the part first, then the label.

▲ Standard

Label all five parts. Then write three findings: one the source shows, one it suggests, one it cannot tell. Point to the source for the first two. Use a separate row for each finding.

★ Stretch

Label and annotate the source, then answer: how far can this one source tell us why people came to Britain in the 1950s? Give a reason that names a limit.

Check yourself: does every label point to a part of the source?

Check yourself: does every finding name its box?

Check yourself: can you point to the source for everything in the shows box?

Here is a finished row: Shows. A transport company in Britain wanted drivers in 1956. I can point to the caption and the big words. One finding, one box, one pointed finger. And someone who has never seen the poster can check it.

Nobody is asked to explain a choice to work from the source card. Record the route used and any scribing on the evidence line, keeping adult support visible and separate from the learner's own words.

4 min
Exit · Audience and Influence

Audience receives; influence changes something

Read one finding to a partner or to an adult. The listener says back the box they heard, in their own words.

Did the listener hear shows where you meant suggests? Then change that one word. That change is your influence. It belongs to you, not to them.

Next week you will use sources like this one to explain a cause and a consequence of migration. Keep your annotated source. It will help you then.

Close by naming one change a learner made after hearing their partner. That is the influence record, and it belongs to the learner.
