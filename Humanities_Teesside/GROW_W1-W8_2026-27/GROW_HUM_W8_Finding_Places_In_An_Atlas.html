Arrival Task

Arrival Task – Retrieving what an atlas is for

No learner is asked where their family is from. Every card is a place on the map. The task is finding it in the book, and nothing you say leaves this room.
You can work with the index open. You can dictate to an adult and the words stay yours. You can point on the map instead of writing. Each one is a full route.
Atlas is a book of maps with an index that tells you which page a place is on.

A map shows you where. An atlas is a book of maps with a way in.

Next to the page number is a grid reference, like 14 C3.

Arrival decision: Take an atlas and the place cards from the box. Everyone works with the same class set.

On your table there is an atlas and twelve place cards. Some name a country. Some name a continent. Some name an ocean. All twelve are in the index of that atlas.

Have every learner open the index before any map page is turned. A class that goes straight to the map is browsing, not locating.

Starter

Starter: Two ways of finding the same place

Two ways of finding the same country.

Way A: turn pages until you see it.

Way B: look it up in the index, go to that page, and use the grid reference.

Way B is quicker for any place you can name. Way A is only quicker when you do not know the name and you are looking at a shape.

The need for this lesson is knowing which way to use. Both are real routes, and picking the wrong one costs you the lesson.

Try it: take the card in front of you. Do you know its name? Then the index is your route. Do not open the map yet.

Do not name the route for them. Choosing between the index and the map is the decision the outcome asks for.

I Do

I Do: Sorting places by what they are

The diagram sorts twelve places into three boxes. Read the names off it rather than out of your head, because that is what an atlas asks you to do.

Some cards are harder than they look. A very large country can feel like a continent, and the index settles it: it is listed as a country.

Modelled: I take a card. I read the name. I look it up in the index. The index tells me the page and the grid reference, and it tells me what kind of place it is. Then I sort the card.

Grid reference is a column letter and a row number that meet on the square you want.
Insist the index is checked before a card is sorted. The lesson is the route, and a correct sort made from memory has skipped it.

We Do

We Do: Sort the cards, then match each to its grid reference

Card 1: a country. Which continent is it inside?

Card 2: an ocean. Which continents does it lie between?

Card 3: a continent. How many countries can you find on its page?

Sort first: sort your twelve cards into the three boxes, then match each card to the grid reference from the index. Everyone commits at the same time, and then we hold up the first grid reference together.

Evidence check

Two tables may write a different grid reference for the same place. Large places cover several squares, and both can be right. We ask what is at that reference, not who wrote which.

Take the grid reference from a learner who has not spoken today. Turn to it with the class so the check is public, not private.

I Do

I Do: Which route is quicker

The picture shows both routes side by side. Each has three steps, so neither is lazy. The difference is what you already have.

Saying which route you used, and why, is part of the answer today. It is what turns finding into locating.

Decided together: the card names a country, so we have a name, so we take the index route. Three steps and we are on the square.

Do not let the index route become the only route. A learner given a shape and no name has to use the map, and that is the skill the other half of the outcome asks for.
