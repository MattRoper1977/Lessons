One Story, Four Retellings

GROW · Humanities · Week 5. RE and World Views. One Story, Four Retellings.

Objective: compare how the approved sources retell the Christmas nativity, record two details that appear in only one of them, and explain in one sentence what a retelling emphasises by what it leaves out.

Success evidence: two details recorded with the source they appear in, and one sentence on what a retelling emphasises by leaving something out.

Everything comes from the approved source cards. You report what each source says; nobody is asked what they believe.

40 minutes. Offline.

Arrival · retrieve

Arrival · retrieve

A retelling is a set of choices

Nobody who retells a story tells all of it. There is never time.

What gets kept and what gets left out is a choice, even when nobody notices making it.

Comparing two retellings shows you those choices from the outside.

That is a skill you use in every subject, not only this one.

Today the story is the nativity and there are four sources on the sheet.

Keep the comparison between the sources on the sheet. Do not ask the room what they were taught at home.

You need the four source cards, the comparison grid and the record sheet.

Starter · create the need

Starter · create the need

Two accounts of the same arrival

Source one has shepherds in it and no wise men.

Source two has wise men in it and no shepherds.

A lot of people would say both, because that is how the story is usually told now.

The combined version is a fifth retelling, made later, and it is not on your sheet.

Noticing that is the whole skill: what you thought was the story is one telling of it.

  • 1 · read one source at a time.
  • 2 · list what is in it.
  • 3 · compare the lists.
  • 4 · say what only one has.

7 min
I Do · model

I Do · model

Building the grid one source at a time

I read source one and list what is in it: shepherds, a manger, an angel.

I do not compare yet. Comparing while reading makes you see what you expect.

Then source two: wise men, a star, gifts, a journey.

Now I put the two lists side by side and look for what appears once.

The star is in source two only. The shepherds are in source one only.

Then the harder question: what does source one emphasise by leaving the star out?

It puts ordinary working people at the centre of the arrival, and nobody grand.

Make the emphasis sentence the last thing, and say why it could not have come first.

I Do 2 · connect

I Do 2 · connect

Leaving out is a way of pointing

What a retelling leaves out shapes what a listener notices as much as what it puts in.

Take the wise men out and the arrival happens among people with no power at all.

Take the shepherds out and the arrival is recognised by learned travellers from elsewhere.

Neither telling says the other is false. Each one points somewhere.

So your sentence names what is left out and says what that makes the reader notice.

Write it about the source, not about whether the story is true.

Independent · evidence

Independent · evidence

Two details and one sentence

Work on your own. Read each source completely before comparing.

Record two details that appear in only one source, and name the source for each.

Then write one sentence on what a source emphasises by what it leaves out.

Write about the source's choices, not about whether the events happened.

If you finish early, name a detail the modern combined version adds to both.

  • Supported · one detail with its source, and the sentence said aloud.
  • Standard · two details with sources, and a written sentence on emphasis.
  • Stretch · as Standard, and name a detail the modern combined version adds.

Read the emphasis sentence to an adult
