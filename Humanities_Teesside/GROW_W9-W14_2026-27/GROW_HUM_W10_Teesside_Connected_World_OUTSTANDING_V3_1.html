Connected across the sea

Autumn 2 · Week 3 · 40 minutes

Learning intention

I can explain a connection between places using a source and a diagram.

Success criteria

  • Name the places and identify what connects them.
  • Use a named source for a claim.
  • Say what the source does not prove.

Use Next to begin. Ask for word help or your usual support when needed.

Arrival · 2 min

Find the two places

Find Teesport and Rotterdam in the diagram.

Teesport is in North East England. Rotterdam is in the Netherlands.

Use the labels and key. This drawing shows a connection; it cannot measure the journey.

Starter · 3 min

What crosses the sea?

Does every connection need a ship?

Goods can move by ship. Messages can travel through communication networks.

Both connect places. They move different things in different ways.

A youth group sends another group a digital drawing. What is being exchanged?

I do · 5 min

Source → route → claim

Use the operator's evidence precisely.

Source A supports a goods connection between the two ports.

It does not show every person's journey. A possible local effect is work handling cargo; that is an inference to investigate.

A · Port operator report

In February 2021, PD Ports described cargo links between Teesport and Rotterdam. It announced work with Rotterdam on a digital port-information system.

Paraphrase of PD Ports, 11 February 2021. The operator is describing and promoting its services.
We do · 6 min

Which source supports it?

Choose a source, then test a claim.

Switch between A, B and C.

Notice what each source changes in the diagram. A population figure does not show a travel route.

A · Port operator report

In February 2021, PD Ports described cargo links between Teesport and Rotterdam. It announced work with Rotterdam on a digital port-information system.

Paraphrase of PD Ports, 11 February 2021. The operator is describing and promoting its services.

B · Population snapshot

Middlesbrough Council reports that 12.29% of residents were born outside the UK in Census 2021. This is about 12 in every 100 residents.

Adapted from Middlesbrough Council's Census 2021 summary; page updated 19 December 2025. Rounded display is labelled approximate.

C · Imagined youth project

A Teesside youth group shares digital music recommendations with a Rotterdam youth group. Nobody is described as travelling.

Constructed classroom example. It is not evidence of a real partnership or any individual's life.
Paper or spoken route

Use A/B/C cards, a blank connection diagram and a 100-square grid. Draw goods/messages with different line styles. For B, annotate the dated proportion and explain why no individual travel route can be drawn.

I do · 4 min

Repair an overclaim

Does the evidence go that far?

Overclaim: “The port link proves everyone in Teesside goes to Rotterdam.”

Repair: “Source A describes a cargo link. It does not tell us where every resident travels.”

  1. Find the claim's biggest word: everyone.
  2. Look back at the source.
  3. Keep what it supports; remove what it cannot show.
We do · 5 min

Goods and messages can work together

Build two different chains.

In our imagined project, the groups send a digital design first. They later post a small finished model.

Name what moves in each chain and how. The activity stays fictional even when it is realistic.

D · Imagined design exchange

Teesside group → digital design → Rotterdam group. Later: Teesside group → posted model → Rotterdam group.

Constructed scenario for practising two connection types.
Independent · 12 min

Map a new connection

Explain this new project and compare it with A.

Use scenario E to draw and label one connection.

Then use the real operator card A for a second connection. Explain how they differ.

Add a possible effect and say which parts the evidence cannot prove.

E · New imagined project

A Teesside class emails a seed-growing diary to a class in Lille, France. The classes compare their plants. No plants or people are sent between the classes.

New constructed transfer scenario. Not a real exchange or a claim about all schools in either place.

Supported

Use E to label two places and what passes between them. Give a short reason for your category and point to one thing E does not say. Then identify the different connection in A.

____ connects to ____ by ____. The evidence is ____. It does not say ____.

Standard

Explain E's message connection and A's goods connection. Include places, medium/route, named evidence, one possible local effect and a limitation.

Two short chains, then one comparison: both…, but…. Label possible effects as inferences.

Stretch

Compare E and A, then evaluate whether B could explain either connection. Explain the difference between evidence of a population link and evidence of an individual route or motive.

This source can establish ____, but a different source would be needed for ____.

No name is needed. Save your written notes before closing or reloading.

Exit · 3 min

Claim, source, limit

Give one sentence someone can check.

Name your connection and source.

Add: “This does not prove…”

Choose one question for the next geography lesson.

Does B tell us why one particular resident moved?

Preparation

  • Display/print source cards, the connection diagram and optional 100-square grid.
  • Provide paper, pencil and two distinguishable line styles or colours.
  • Keep source A beside the independent task; it is evidence, not an answer key.

Care and safety

  • No home location, nationality, faith or family migration disclosure is required.
  • Do not ask pupils to contact a real overseas group. All exchange scenarios here are fictional.

Teacher response guidance

  • A: Teesport–Rotterdam goods/cargo connection. It cannot prove everyone's journey or feelings, an exact current sailing schedule, or equal benefit.
  • B: proportion of Middlesbrough residents born outside the UK at Census 2021. It does not identify an individual's route or motive. 12.29% is approximately 12 in 100.
  • C: information/messages in a fictional youth exchange.
  • D: design is information; posted model is goods. The parcel's full route is not specified.
  • E: Teesside–Lille message connection via email; no physical plants or people sent. Possible effect: pupils might learn from comparing growth. That effect is inferred, not measured.
  • Comparison: A is real dated operator evidence for cargo; E is a fictional educational message scenario. Both link places, through different kinds of exchange.
  • Exit: B cannot explain a particular person's reason for moving.

Evidence of learning

Look for two located places, a correctly named connection, evidence appropriate to the claim and an explicit limit. Check that the pupil distinguishes scenario reasoning from evidence of a real partnership. Do not score factual recall about individual residents.

Access and independence

Use short cards, large place labels, a key with text and line style, and read-aloud support. Pupils may draw, point, talk or use exact scribing. Fade category prompts before E. Reading demand is a design choice, not a certified age.

Arrival · Find the two places

Retrieve the meaning of a diagram key. Accept pointing or description. The drawing represents relationships and does not teach geographic position, scale or a precise sea route.

Starter · What crosses the sea?

The example is fictional rehearsal. Keep goods, people and information distinct, while allowing a real project to involve more than one connection.

I do · Source → route → claim

Model: 'A identifies a cargo connection, so I draw a goods link. Handling cargo may support local work, but this card gives no job total or worker's experience.' Distinguish an effect inference from a reported fact.

We do · Which source supports it?

Expected: A supports cargo connection; B supports a population proportion only; C supports reasoning within the imagined scenario. Do not infer faith, nationality or reason for migration from country of birth. The 100-square model is approximate, not 100 actual people.

I do · Repair an overclaim

The repair should narrow the claim, not deny the goods connection. Contrast 'some residents may travel' with 'the source establishes that they travel': possible is not proven.

We do · Goods and messages can work together

Expected: digital design is information; posted model is a physical good. Both connect the groups, but the scenario does not specify the parcel's full route, journey time or carbon footprint.

Independent · Map a new connection

Do not give the connection category before independent response. Look for information/messages, email medium and shared learning as a possible effect. Compare with A's cargo. Record fiction/real distinction; pupils should not cite E as evidence of a real school partnership.

Exit · Claim, source, limit

Use exit responses to decide whether the next lesson needs more work on diagram keys, real/constructed source labels or overclaims. Tell pupils what their responses changed in the teaching plan.

Misconceptions

A port route describes everyone's travel. Name what the operator actually reported: cargo. Population and individual travel need different evidence.

Country of birth reveals identity or religion. B only describes the recorded birthplace category. These are different questions.

A plausible fictional example proves a real event. Read the constructed label and say 'in this example' when reasoning.

The 2021 statistic describes today's population. Keep the Census 2021 date attached to the number.

Word help

connection: A link between people or places.

goods: Things people buy, sell or move.

port: A place where ships load and unload.

source: Where our information comes from.

limit: Something the evidence cannot tell us.
