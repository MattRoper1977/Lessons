3 min
Arrival · retrieve

What we already know about old and new

Last week we put labels on places. This week we look at how places change over time.

Then means a long time ago. Now means today. A picture can show either.

A clue is a small thing that tells you a big thing. Old cars in a picture are a clue.

You can point to a picture in the set. You do not have to bring one from home.

Places change. Shops change their signs. Roads get busier. Some things stay the same.

Two people can order the same pictures. Their clues can be different. Both are right.

Arrival decision: Pick the picture set, or pick a place you know that has changed. Both are full answers. You can change your mind later. Nobody will ask which one you picked.

Before we start, look at the six pictures on your table. They all show the same made-up street. Some are old. Some are new. There are cars, shops, a bus and people in each one. Everyone in this room can work with these pictures from start to end.

Pick up one picture now. Look at the cars. Look at the clothes. Say then or now, or point to the word. That is all you need to do to begin.

Offer the picture set first, not as a fallback. A learner who talks about their own old photographs is not prompted for more; note the clue they used, not the family. Wait; take the least prompt that works.

Starter · create the need

Why is a guess not enough?

Here are two answers about the same picture.

Answer A: “It is old.”

Answer B: “It is old, because the cars are square and the shop sign is painted.”

Answer B tells you more than Answer A. Answer A is a guess. Answer B is a guess with a clue. So someone who cannot see the picture still knows why it is old.

The need for this lesson is the clue. Anyone can say old or new. Today you will say the clue too. By the end you will be able to put pictures in order and say what told you.

Try it in your head: think of a phone from a long time ago. Now think of a phone today. What is different? That difference is a clue. It tells you which is older.

Some clues are big. A horse and cart is a big clue. Some clues are small. The shape of a lamp post is a small clue. Small clues count too.

Not everything changes. The church in the picture set looks the same in every picture. That is a clue as well. It tells you it is the same street.

Do not accept a bare "old" or "new" as complete and do not treat it as wrong. Name the gap: we have the guess, we still need the clue.

I Do · model

A street over time

The diagram above is a timeline. Time runs from left to right. The left end is long ago. The middle is when grown-ups were small. The right end is now.

Under each point there are clues. Long ago: a horse and cart, painted signs. In the middle: square cars, a phone box. Now: rounded cars, lit-up signs.

Notice what the timeline does not say. It does not say the old street was better. It does not say the new street is better. It only says which came first.

Watch me do it: I pick up a picture. I look for a clue. I see a horse and cart. Horses pulled carts long ago. So this picture goes at the left end. I find the clue first. Then I place the picture.

Now watch one more: a picture with square cars and a phone box. No horses. Not the newest cars either. It goes in the middle. Same steps. Clue first. Then the place on the line.

So the order on the line comes from the clues. Every picture needs its own clue said out loud. That is why the next diagram is about the clue itself.

Timeline means a line that shows what came first and what came next.

Read the timeline left to right, naming each point and its clues before any picture is placed. A learner who places a picture by the church rather than the cars has used a real clue; ask what it told them.

We Do · put them in order

Put three pictures in order together

Picture 1: a bus with a round front and lit-up shop signs.

Picture 2: a horse and cart outside the shop.

Picture 3: square cars and a red phone box.

Order first: put the three pictures in order on your table, oldest on the left, newest on the right. Everyone orders at the same time. Then we hold up the oldest one and check together.

Pictures 1 and 3 can be close. Some people will say the bus is newer. Some will say the cars are. When we check, we ask for the clue. We do not check the order on its own. A clue makes an order right.

After the check, one person from each table says the clue for their oldest picture. We are listening for something you can see in the picture. If two tables ordered differently and both gave a clue, both stay up.

Changing your order after you hear someone else's clue is allowed. It is a good sign. Move the picture and say what changed it.

Have every table order before any answer is taken, so nobody copies. An order changed after the check is a success, not a correction.

I Do 2 · connect

Clue, guess, check

The diagram above has three boxes: clue, then then or now, then check. The first box is what you can see. The middle box is your guess. The last box is a second clue that checks the guess.

Look at the picture. Clue: square cars. Guess: then. Check: there is a phone box too. That is a full chain. Two clues agree, so the guess is safe.

Here is one that is not complete: square cars, then then. It stops at the guess. It never checks. One clue can trick you. A new car can drive down an old street.

Fixing it together: add the check. Square cars, so then, and the shop sign is painted, not lit. The word and often starts the third box.

A chain with a check is an order you can trust. That is what your record will show: the clue, the guess, and the second clue.

Point to each box as you say it. A learner who gives only the guess has given one of three, and the diagram shows what is still to come.

We Do 2 · rank the clues

Rank the clues together

◆ Supported

Point to the clue card that shows the biggest change. Then choose the check card that matches.

▲ Standard

Say all three parts for the picture with the bus, in order.

★ Stretch

Give a second check for the same picture. Say which clue you trust more.

We rank the clues on the board first. Nobody writes yet. Everyone puts their best clue on a whiteboard. Then we show them all at once. Then we agree the order of clues, strongest first.

The picture for this is the one with the bus. The guess is done: now. Your job is the first box: which clue tells you most? Write one clue, not a describing word.

When we show, we will have lots of clues. We put the strongest at the top. We put the weakest at the bottom. They are not wrong. But a weak clue needs a check more than a strong one.

Then, together, we add the check and read the whole chain from left to right.

Take the clue from a learner who has not yet spoken today. Scribe exactly what is said; do not improve the wording.

Independent · evidence

Your record: then and now

Make one record. You may use the picture set, or a place you know that has changed, or both. All three are complete answers.

◆ Supported

Two pictures. Put them in order, then choose the clue card that fits. An adult may scribe your exact words, and the words stay yours. Point to the older one first, then the clue.

▲ Standard

Four pictures. Put them in order from oldest to newest. For each one, write the clue, your guess, and the check. Use a separate row for each picture so the parts stay in order.

★ Stretch

Six pictures in order. For one of them, say one thing that changed and one thing that stayed the same. A thing that stayed the same is still a finding.

Check yourself: are your pictures in order from oldest to newest?

Check yourself: does every picture have a clue you can see?

Check yourself: would someone who cannot see the picture get your clue?

Check yourself: did you add a check for at least one picture?

Finished early? Take one picture. Find a second clue. Then say which clue you would trust more, and why. Mark it.

If you are stuck on the check, say your first clue out loud and then add the word and. Whatever comes next is usually the second clue.

Here is a finished one: the picture with the horse and cart. Clue: a horse pulls the cart. Guess: then. Check: the signs are painted by hand. Three parts. One line. And someone who cannot see it gets why it is old.

You may not want to write about a real place. That is fine. The picture set is a full answer. Nobody will ask you why.

Your record is done when each picture has a clue and a guess. Read one of them out loud to yourself. Check it makes sense.

Nobody is asked to explain a choice to work from the picture set. Record the route used and any scribing on the evidence line, keeping adult support visible and separate from the learner's own words.

Exit · Audience and Influence

Audience receives; influence changes something

Read one chain to a partner or to an adult. The listener says back the clue they heard, in their own words.

Did the listener hear a different clue? Then change one word in your chain. Make it say what you meant. That change is your influence. It belongs to you, not to them.

Did the listener hear just what you meant? Write that down too. A chain that already works is worth noting. It needed no change.

Next week we look at what makes people the same and different. Keep your record. It will help you then.

Nothing goes outside this room unless you say so. Your record stays in your folder. You decide if it goes on the wall.

Close by naming one change a learner made after hearing their partner. That is the influence record, and it belongs to the learner.
