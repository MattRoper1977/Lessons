Arrival Task

Arrival Task – What we know about light

You never have to say what happens at your home. Every task works with the cards on the table. Nobody is asked what they believe or what they celebrate. Nothing you say leaves this room.
You can stop for a bit. You can point instead of speak. You can use the cards only. You can tell an adult what to write. Each one is a full way to do this lesson.
Festival is a special time when people do something together every year.

In winter it gets dark early. In summer it stays light late.

People use light to see. People also use light to show something.

A candle on a cake is not there to help you see. It is there to mark a day.

Lots of festivals in the world use light at the darkest time of year.

A festival happens at the same time each year. That is part of what makes it one.

You do not have to be part of a festival to learn what its lights are for.

Arrival decision: Pick a picture card from the table. You do not have to say why you picked it. You can change your mind later.

On your table there are six picture cards. Each one shows lights at a festival. Small oil lamps in a row. Candles in a window. Lanterns on a string. A fire in a field. Everyone in this room can work with these cards from start to end.

Look at one card now. Where are the lights? Point to them. That is all you need to do to begin.

Offer the cards first, not as a fallback. If a learner starts to describe their own celebration, thank them and return to the card. Wait; take the least prompt that works.

Starter

Starter: A guess and a checked guess

Here are two answers about the same picture of small lamps.

Answer A: “The lamps are there so people can see.”

Answer B: “The lamps are there so people can see. I checked the card. It says they mark light winning over dark.”

Answer B is stronger than Answer A, and not because it is longer. Answer A is a guess. Answer B is a guess that has been checked.

The need for this lesson is the checking. A guess is a good place to start. It only becomes an answer once you have looked.

Try it in your head: look at the lantern card. What do you think the lanterns are for? Hold that thought. Do not look at the card underneath yet.

A prediction you got wrong is still useful. It tells you exactly what to look for when you check.

Nobody in this room loses anything by predicting wrong. Writing it down before you look is the brave part.

Do not correct a prediction at this point and do not confirm one. Both close the checking step before it happens.

I Do

I Do: Predict, then check

The picture has three steps. First you predict, in writing, before you look. Then you check by turning the source card over. Then you say what changed.

The last step is the one people skip. Without it, a check is just reading. With it, you know what you learned.

You do not have to be wrong for the third box to be worth writing. Nothing changed is a real answer too.

Watch me do it: I look at the row of small lamps. I predict: they are for seeing in the dark. I write it down. Now I turn the card. The card says the lamps mark light winning over dark. I say what changed: I thought they were for seeing, and they are really for marking.

Now watch one where nothing changes: the candles in the window. I predict they show that somebody is home and welcome. I turn the card. The card says the same. So I write: nothing changed, and now I know why.

Both of those are finished pieces of work. One changed. One did not. Both went through all three boxes.

Predict means saying what you think before you look.
Insist the prediction is written before any card is turned. Model an unchanged prediction as well as a changed one, so nobody thinks being wrong is the goal.

We Do

We Do: Predict what the lights are for. Then check.

Card 1: lanterns on a string above a street. What are they for?

Card 2: a fire lit in a field at night. What is it for?

Card 3: a row of small oil lamps by a door. What are they for?

Predict first: write your prediction and hold it. Nobody turns a source card yet. Everyone commits at the same time, and then we hold up the predictions together before you check.

Evidence check

We will have different predictions for the same card. Good. When you check, you are not looking for who was right. You are looking for what the card actually says.

After the check, one person from each table says what changed. If nothing changed for them, they say that instead. Both count as a full answer.

Changing your mind after you read the card is the whole point. Cross out and write the new one underneath, so we can see both.

Hold the source cards face down until every prediction is written. A learner who reads early has not cheated; turn it back and take their prediction from memory.

I Do

I Do: Why light matters at that time of year

The picture has two circles. The left one is the dark time of year. The right one is what people do about it.

The arrow in the middle is the reason. When the nights come early, a light is worth more. So people gather and mark it.

That is why so many festivals of light land in the same part of the year. It is not a coincidence. It is the dark.

Saying the reason together: the nights are long, so a light stands out, so people put lights up and come together. Three steps, one sentence.

Your record will end with that reason in your own words.

Keep the reason at the level of dark and gathering. Do not extend it into belief; the outcome is about why light matters, and the source cards stop there too.

Predict the reason and say what a festival gives people that a lamp on its own does not.

We predict one reason together first. Nobody turns the last card yet. Everyone writes a reason on a whiteboard, then we hold up the boards at the same time.

The card for this one is the winter one. Your job is the reason. Why put lights up when it is already dark?

When we hold up, we will keep every reason that mentions the dark or the people. Reasons about the lamp itself we move to one side. They are not wrong, but they are about the object.

Then we turn the last card and check the whole reason together.

Take the reason from a learner who has not spoken yet today. Scribe exactly what is said; do not improve the wording.

Exit Ticket

Exit Ticket — Audience receives; influence changes something

Read one prediction and check to a partner or an adult. The listener says back what changed, in their own words.

Did the listener miss what changed? Then change one word in yours. That change is your influence. It belongs to you, not to them.

Did the listener get it first time? Write that down too. A record that already works is worth noting.

That is the last of these eight weeks. Keep your record; it goes in your folder with the others.

Nothing goes outside this room unless you say so. You decide if your work goes on the wall.

Close by naming one learner whose prediction changed after they read the card. That is the influence record, and it belongs to the learner.
