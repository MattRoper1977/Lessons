Arrival Task

Arrival Task – Retrieving what a source can carry

No learner is asked to disclose their own heritage, nationality, faith or family history. Every task can be completed entirely from the supplied sources; the enquiry concerns the sources, not the people in the room, and nothing said here leaves it.
You may pause, work alone, point rather than speak, use the printed route or direct an adult to scribe your exact words; each of these is a complete way to take part.
Provenance describes where a source came from: who produced it, when, for whom and for what purpose.

A source establishes only what can be pointed to on it; everything beyond that is either an inference the source supports or a claim it cannot carry.

Identity is layered rather than singular, and a source usually records one layer, such as country of birth, while remaining silent about the others.

Arrival decision: Work from the three supplied sources, or add a fourth from the approved pack; both are complete answers, and nobody will ask which you chose.

Offer the sources first rather than as a fallback. A learner who connects a source to their own family is not prompted further; record the provenance element they used. Wait, and take the least prompt that works.

Starter

Starter: Why does provenance change what the content means?

Consider two readings of the same recruitment poster.

Reading A: “The poster shows that Britain welcomed workers from the Caribbean in the 1950s.”

Reading B: “The poster shows that one transport employer, short of staff in the 1950s, chose to advertise for workers in the Caribbean; whether Britain as a whole welcomed them is a separate question the poster cannot answer.”

Reading B is more careful than Reading A because it keeps the claim the size of the provenance: one employer, one purpose, one audience. Reading A borrows the authority of the whole country from a source produced by a single company, which is the most common way an inference outgrows its evidence.

Do not accept a description of the picture as complete and do not treat it as wrong. Name the gap: the content has been read, the provenance has not yet been labelled.

I Do

I Do: Four provenance labels around one source

The diagram above places the census summary table in the centre with four provenance labels around it: origin, date, audience and purpose. The connecting lines indicate which part of the source supplies each label, and the caption at the bottom records that only two of the four are printed on the source itself.

Watch the method: I read the caption first and label origin and date, because those are printed. I then ask who would use a table like this and why it was compiled, and I label audience and purpose as inferences, marking them with a question mark until the annotation confirms them. Printed labels first, inferred labels second, and the difference recorded.

Inference is a conclusion reasoned from the source rather than read directly on it.
Read the diagram clockwise from origin, naming each label and whether it is printed or inferred before any learner labels a source. A learner who labels the census audience as "migrants" has inferred from content rather than provenance; ask which part of the source supports it.

We Do

We Do: Label the poster together

Element 1: the caption names a transport employer as the producer. Which provenance label does that supply?

Element 2: the poster was displayed in Caribbean towns in the 1950s. Which label does that supply, and is it printed or inferred?

Element 3: the wording invites readers to apply for skilled work in London. Which label does that supply?

Label first: write the provenance label for each of the three elements on your copy of the poster, marking each as printed or inferred; every table labels at the same time, and only then do we hold the copies up and compare.

Evidence check

Element 3 is where readings diverge, because the invitation to apply can be labelled as purpose or as audience depending on which question you asked of it; both are defensible if the annotation says which part of the wording supports the label. A label with a pointed finger is provenance work; a label alone is a guess.

Have every table label before any answer is taken so that nobody copies. A label revised after the comparison is a success rather than a correction.

I Do

I Do: Establishes, suggests, cannot establish

The diagram above sets out three boxes, establishes, suggests and cannot establish, and every annotation you write today must name the box its claim has earned; a claim that can be pointed to on the source belongs in the first, a claim argued from provenance in the second, and everything beyond the source in the third.

An annotation that fails this test reads: “The passenger list establishes that the Windrush generation felt British.” Nothing on a list of names, ages and occupations can carry a claim about feeling; the claim belongs in the third box, and moving it there is not a loss but an accurate record of the source's silence.

Repairing it together: rewrite the claim as “the passenger list cannot establish how the passengers understood their own identity, although the nationality law of the same year gave them the legal status of British subjects.” The claim is now the size of the evidence and points to a second source for the part the first cannot carry.

Point to each box as you name it. A learner who places a claim about feeling in the establishes box has made the most common annotation error; show the third box rather than marking the claim wrong.

WAGOLL — what a good one looks like

A finished row reads: the census table; origin printed, date printed, audience inferred, purpose inferred; it establishes that in 2011 roughly one usual resident in eight in England and Wales was born outside the UK, and it cannot establish how any of those residents described their identity. Provenance labelled, claim sized, silence recorded.
