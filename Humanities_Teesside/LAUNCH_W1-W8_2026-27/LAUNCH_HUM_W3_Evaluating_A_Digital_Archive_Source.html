Arrival Task

Arrival Task – Retrieving what an archive record is

No live connection is required and no learner researches their own family. Every task can be completed from the supplied offline catalogue extract, and nothing said here leaves the room.
You may pause, work alone, point rather than speak, use the printed route or direct an adult to scribe your exact words; each of these is a complete way to take part.
Catalogue record is the archive's description of a document, with fields such as reference, title, date and held-by, which is not the document itself.

An archive holds original documents; a catalogue record describes one of them in fixed fields, and a digitised copy reproduces it, so three different things can share one title.

A transcription is a typed copy made later by someone else, so an error in it is not an error in the original, and the catalogue usually says which you are reading.

Arrival decision: Work from the supplied catalogue extract, or add a second from the approved pack; nobody will ask which you chose.

Offer the extract first rather than as a fallback. A learner who mentions researching their own family in an archive is not prompted further; record the catalogue field they used. Wait, and take the least prompt that works.

Starter

Starter: Why does an evaluation need a field to point to?

Consider two evaluations of the same record.

Evaluation A: “The passenger list is a reliable source because it is in a national archive.”

Evaluation B: “The passenger list is reliable for the names, ages and stated occupations of those who sailed, because the description field shows it was compiled by the shipping line at the time of the voyage for official purposes; it is less useful for why they came, because the format field shows it records only what the form asked.”

Evaluation B is an evaluation and Evaluation A is a compliment, because B says what the record is reliable for and useful for, and points to the field that supports each claim, whereas A treats the building the document sits in as a guarantee of everything written on it.

Do not accept "it is reliable because it is official" as complete and do not treat it as wrong. Name the gap: the judgement is made, the field it rests on is not yet named.

I Do

I Do: Anatomy of a catalogue record

The diagram above lays out the seven fields of the catalogue record: reference, title, description, date, held by, format and access. Each field answers a different question, and an evaluation earns its judgement by pointing to the field that answers the question the judgement depends on.

Watch the method: I read the model evaluation's first sentence, which says the list was "created by the archive in 1948". I check the description field, which says the list was compiled by the shipping line, and the held-by field, which names the archive as custodian rather than creator. The sentence has confused the record with the document, which is the first error family on the checklist, so I mark it and write the correction beside it, naming both fields. Read the claim, find the field, name the error family, correct.

Custodian is the body that keeps a document safe and catalogues it, as distinct from the body that created it.
Read the diagram field by field, naming the question each answers, before any learner touches the model evaluation. A learner who treats the held-by field as the creator has made the first error family; ask what the description field says.

We Do

We Do: Spot the error in three sentences

Sentence 1: “The list is reliable because it was created by the national archive in 1948.”

Sentence 2: “Because the record was digitised in 2018, the information dates from 2018.”

Sentence 3: “The list is useful because it tells us why every passenger chose to come to Britain.”

Spot first: decide what is wrong with each sentence and write the error family and the catalogue field that exposes it on your whiteboard; every table commits at the same time, and only then do we hold the boards up and correct the sentences together.

Evidence check

Sentence 3 is where readings diverge, because the list records stated occupations, which some read as a reason for coming; the format field settles it, since the form asked for occupation and not for motive. A spotted error with a named field is evaluation; one without is a hunch.

Have every table commit before any answer is taken so that nobody copies. An error family revised after the comparison is a success rather than a correction.

I Do

I Do: Reliable for what, useful for what

The diagram above plots one record against four questions on two axes, reliable for and useful for, and the point of the grid is that the same document sits high on both axes for one question and low on both for another; reliability and usefulness are properties of a question-and-record pair, never of a record alone.

A model evaluation that ignores the grid reads: “This is an unreliable source because it does not explain why people came.” The list was never asked to explain motive, so its silence is not unreliability; the error confuses a low usefulness score on one question with a low reliability score on all questions.

Repairing it together: rewrite the sentence as “The list is reliable for who sailed but not useful for why they came, because the format field shows the form asked for occupation and not for motive,” which places the record correctly on both axes and names the field that fixes its position.

Point to each axis as you name it. A learner who declares a record unreliable because it is silent has made the third error family; show the grid rather than marking the judgement wrong.

WAGOLL — what a good one looks like

A finished row reads: “The list is reliable for who sailed, because the description field shows the shipping line compiled it at the time,” and beside it, “The list is not useful for why they came, because the format field shows the form asked for occupation, not motive.” Two claims, two fields, one record on both axes.
