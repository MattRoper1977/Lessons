3 min
Arrival · retrieve

Retrieving sequence, scale and significance

Sequence is fixed by the dates on the cards and is the same for every question; significance is a judgement that changes with the question and must be argued with locators.

The migration cards from the last three weeks belong on this line, among the wars, Acts and elections, as part of a wider story.

Arrival decision: Work from the supplied event cards, or add one dated event located in the approved pack; nobody will ask which.

Offer the cards first rather than as a fallback. A learner who places a card by family memory is not prompted further; record the date they used. Wait, and take the least prompt that works.

3 min
Starter · create the need

Why is the order not the same as the importance?

Consider two timelines built from the same cards.

Timeline A: the cards are placed in date order with equal gaps between them, and the largest card is the one the builder liked most.

Timeline B: the cards are placed in date order at scale, so that 1918 and 1928 sit close together while 1973 and 1979 sit close together, and the ranking for the enquiry question is written on a separate strip beneath the line with a locator for each reason.

Timeline B is an instrument and Timeline A is a decoration, because B keeps sequence and significance apart: the line carries only what the dates fix, and the strip beneath it carries only what the argument earns, so that either can be changed without disturbing the other.

Do not accept a date-ordered line with no ranking as complete and do not treat it as wrong. Name the gap: the sequence is built, the significance has not yet been argued.

4 min
I Do · model

A century on one line, to scale

The diagram above draws the century to scale, four millimetres to the year, from 1900 on the left to 2000 on the right. Six of the cards sit at their dates: the franchise Acts of 1918 and 1928, the two events of 1948, the 1962 Act on Commonwealth immigration, entry to the EEC in 1973 and the 1998 Belfast agreement.

Watch the method: I take the law against racial discrimination card, read its printed date, 1965, and measure twelve millimetres to the right of 1962 before placing it; I then read its locator aloud and say what came immediately before and after it on the line. Date, measure, place, locator, neighbours, and only then the next card; the ranking waits until the line is complete.

Franchise is the right to vote, and the Acts of 1918 and 1928 widened who held it.
To scale means that the distance along the line is proportional to the number of years, so gaps can be read as well as order.

Read the line left to right, naming each date and event before any card is placed. A learner who places cards at equal intervals has built a sequence but not a scale; ask how many years separate two neighbouring cards.

3 min
We Do · put them in order

Sequence three cards to scale together

Card A: 1965 · the first law against racial discrimination makes discrimination in public places unlawful.

Card B: 1979 · the first woman becomes prime minister of the UK.

Card C: 1945 · the second world war ends and a general election returns a Labour government.

Sequence first: place the three cards on your scaled line by their printed dates, then write the sequence as letters on your whiteboard with the years between each pair; every table commits at the same time, and only then do we compare.

The sequence has one right answer because every card is dated, but the gaps are where boards will differ; when we compare we ask for the two dates that fixed each gap, because a sequence with measured gaps is a timeline and a sequence without them is a list.

Have every table sequence before any answer is taken so that nobody copies. A gap re-measured after the comparison is a success rather than a correction.

3 min
I Do 2 · connect

Sequence fixed, significance argued

The diagram above separates the two things a timeline can carry: the sequence on the solid line, fixed by the dates and identical for every question, and the ranking on the dashed strips beneath it, which is rewritten whenever the enquiry question changes; the same four events appear in two different orders on the two strips while never moving on the line.

A ranking that fails this separation reads: “1948 is the most significant year because it comes in the middle of the century.” Position on the line is a fact about dates, not an argument about a question, so the sentence has borrowed the authority of the sequence to decorate a judgement it has not made.

Repairing it together: rewrite the sentence as “For the question of who lived in Britain, 1948 ranks first because the passenger list and the nationality law locate both the arrival and the right to settle in that year,” which argues from locators and names the question, leaving the line untouched.

Point to the line and then to each strip as you name them. A learner who argues significance from position on the line has made the most common timeline error; show the strips rather than marking the ranking wrong.

4 min
We Do 2 · rank for the question

Rank the events for one question together

We build one ranking on the board before anyone writes: each learner records a first-ranked card on a whiteboard with the locator that carries its reason, and only when every board is raised do we compare and agree what a reason that points looks like.

Take the first-ranked card from a learner who has not yet spoken today and scribe it exactly; do not improve the wording.

Your record: a timeline of twentieth-century Britain

Produce one timeline from the supplied event cards, or from those and one further dated event from the pack; both are complete answers.

★ Stretch

Complete the standard route, then re-rank the same cards for a second question from the supplied list and explain why the ranking changed while the sequence did not.

Check yourself: does every ranking reason carry a locator?

Check yourself: is the ranking written on the strip and not on the line?

A finished strip reads: for the question of who lived in Britain, first the 1948 Windrush arrival (locator), second the 1962 Act on Commonwealth immigration (locator), third the 1965 law against racial discrimination (locator); the line above is unchanged and the strip can be rewritten for the next question without moving a card.

Nobody is asked to justify working from the supplied cards alone. Record the route used and any scribing on the evidence line, keeping adult support visible and separate from the learner's own words.

Exit · Audience and Influence

Audience receives; influence changes something

Read your ranking strip to a partner or an adult, who repeats the question they heard it answer.

If the listener heard a different question from the one you meant, rewrite the strip heading so the question is unmistakable; the change is yours.

Close by naming one change a learner made after hearing their partner; that is the influence record and it belongs to the learner.
