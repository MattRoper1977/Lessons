Arrival Task

Arrival Task – Retrieving cause, order and evidence

No learner is asked about their own family's history or reasons for moving. Every task can be completed from the supplied cards and their locators, and nothing said here leaves the room.
You may pause, work alone, point rather than speak, use the printed route or direct an adult to scribe your exact words.
Trigger is the immediate event that turns existing pressures into action, such as an order to leave a country.

A cause is a factor that helps explain why an event happened, and a list of factors becomes an explanation only when the relationships between them are stated and evidenced.

Consequences fall on the people who moved and on the places they moved to, and a source usually records one of these more fully than the other.

Arrival decision: Work from the supplied factor cards, or add one located in the approved pack; nobody will ask which.

Offer the cards first rather than as a fallback. A learner who relates a factor to their own family's journey is not prompted further; record the category of cause they identified. Wait, and take the least prompt that works.

Starter

Starter: Why is a list of true facts not yet an explanation?

Consider two answers to the question of why people came to Britain from the Caribbean after 1948.

Answer A: “There had been a war. Britain needed workers. The nationality law was passed. A ship sailed.”

Answer B: “Post-war Britain faced a labour shortage, so employers and the government invited workers from Commonwealth countries; the nationality law of 1948 confirmed their right to come and settle; and one consequence was that hospitals and transport services were staffed in part by Caribbean workers who formed lasting communities in British cities.”

Answer B is an explanation and Answer A is not, although every sentence in A is true, because B states the relationships between the factors with words such as so, confirmed and one consequence was, and each of those joining words is a claim that must be carried by a locator.

Do not accept a list of true facts as complete and do not treat it as wrong. Name the gap: the facts are present, the relationships between them are not yet stated.

I Do

I Do: Three speeds of cause, one event, two kinds of consequence

The diagram above is a causal web rather than a chain: three causes of different speeds converge on one event, and two kinds of consequence diverge from it. The long-term cause is the post-war labour shortage, the short-term cause is the nationality law of 1948, and the trigger, drawn with a dashed border because it is the box most often asserted without evidence, is the advertisement of berths on a particular ship.

Watch the method: I take a factor card that says employers in Britain advertised for workers in Caribbean newspapers. I ask how long that condition had been building, and the answer is months rather than years or days, so I place it as a short-term cause; I then ask which event it helps explain, match it to 1948, and read its locator aloud. Speed first, then match, then locator, and only then the next card.

Locator is the printed reference that says where in the approved pack a sentence originates, so that any arrow can be checked.
Read the web from left to right, naming each speed of cause and both kinds of consequence before any card is sorted. A learner who sorts a consequence card as a cause has usually not asked whether it came before or after the event; ask for the date on the card.

We Do

We Do: Sort three cards. Match one to its event.

Card 1: in August 1972 the ruler of Uganda ordered people of Asian heritage to leave the country within ninety days.

Card 2: many of the families expelled from Uganda held British passports issued under earlier nationality law.

Card 3: during the 1970s shops and businesses founded by families from Uganda opened in towns including Leicester.

Sort first: sort the three cards in the frame on your table as long-term cause, short-term cause, trigger or consequence, then match each to the 1972 event card; every table sorts at the same time, and only then do we compare.

Evidence check

Card 2 is where readings diverge: the passports were issued years before 1972, which makes them long-term, yet they became decisive only when the expulsion order came, so both readings are defensible if the relationship between passports and trigger is stated. A sort with a stated relationship is an explanation; a sort without one is a guess.

Have every table sort before any answer is taken so that nobody copies. A card moved after the comparison is a success rather than a correction.

I Do

I Do: Weighing causes: push, pull and the scale of evidence

The diagram above sorts causes by direction, push factors arising in the place people left and pull factors arising in the place they reached, and beneath the two boxes it draws a scale on which the weight of any cause is measured not by how dramatic it sounds but by the number and quality of the locators that support it.

An explanation that fails the scale reads: “People came to Britain in 1948 because they wanted a better life.” The sentence may be true, but no card in the pack carries it, so it has no weight; the repair is to replace the unsupported motive with the located factors, which state what the evidence can carry.

Repairing it together: rewrite the sentence as “People came to Britain in 1948 because employers advertised work there and the nationality law confirmed their right to settle,” attaching the locator for each clause; the explanation now weighs exactly what the pack can support.

Point to each box and to the scale as you name them. A learner who explains a movement by an unlocated motive has made the most common causation error; show the scale rather than marking the sentence wrong.

Independent Work — Your record: cause and consequence of migration

Sort six cards into cause and consequence and match one cause to its event; an adult may scribe your exact words, which remain yours.

▲ Standard

Sort all cards by speed and direction, match each cause to its event, and write one explanation joining two causes to a consequence with a locator for every clause.

★ Stretch

Complete the standard route, then weigh which cause carried most force for one event, defending the judgement by its locators and naming one evidential limit.

Check yourself: does every joining word in your explanation have a locator beside it?

Check yourself: have you separated consequences for people from consequences for places?

Nobody is asked to justify working from the supplied cards alone. Record the route used and any scribing on the evidence line, keeping adult support visible and separate from the learner's own words.

⏸ Pens down — quick partner check!

Half way through: stop, swap with a partner, and check one thing on the list above together. Then carry on.

WAGOLL — what a good one looks like

A finished explanation reads: a post-war labour shortage (long-term, locator) led employers to advertise for workers in the Caribbean (short-term, locator), and the nationality law of 1948 confirmed the right to settle (locator), so that several hundred passengers arrived on the Windrush in June 1948, one consequence being that hospitals and transport services were staffed in part by Caribbean workers (locator). Four joins, four locators, one explanation.
