Lesson overview

Shared Values, Different Reasons

LAUNCH · Humanities · Week 4. Religious Education and Ethics. Shared Values, Different Reasons.

Objective: compare how two traditions on the approved source sheet mark the International Day of Peace, identify one value they share, and explain in a full paragraph why the reasons they give for it differ.

Success evidence: one shared value identified with a source reference from each tradition, and a paragraph explaining why the reasons differ.

All material comes from the approved source sheet. You report and compare what the sources say; you are not asked what you believe.

40 minutes. Offline.

Arrival · retrieve

Arrival · retrieve

Agreeing on the value is not agreeing on the reason

Two traditions can hold the same value and reach it by completely different routes.

At GCSE the marks are in the routes, not in the value.

Saying both believe in peace is true and scores almost nothing.

Saying why each one gets there, from its own sources, is the answer.

Today's sheet gives two traditions and a short source from each.

Do not survey the room's beliefs. The comparison is between the sources on the sheet.

You need the source sheet, the comparison grid, and the paragraph frame.

Starter · create the need

Starter · create the need

Two answers to the same question

Answer A: both religions believe in peace, so they both support the Day of Peace.

Answer B: both support it, but one grounds peace in a duty owed to God and the other in the consequences for the community, so their reasons work differently when the two conflict.

Both are accurate. One would be capped at the bottom band.

The difference is that the second answer says what the reasons are, and what follows from them.

An examiner is looking for that second move, and it is the only one worth practising.

  • 1 · name the shared value.
  • 2 · give each tradition's reason.
  • 3 · reference both.
  • 4 · say what follows from the difference.

I Do · model

I Do · model

Building the paragraph from the grid

I fill the grid first. Column one is the value, columns two and three are the two reasons.

Value: both sources say violence should be avoided where possible.

Reason one comes from a command in a text, quoted on source A, line three.

Reason two comes from an argument about harm, on source B, line two.

Now I write the paragraph, and the grid gives me its order.

The last sentence is the one that matters: a duty holds even when the outcome is bad, and a consequence-based reason does not.

That sentence could not be written without both references in front of me.

Write the last sentence last, and say why you could not have written it first. The order is the technique.

We Do · sequence the measures

We Do · everyone commits

Sort the reasons

Six reason cards are on your table. Sort them into duty-based and consequence-based.

It is commanded. It reduces suffering. It is what a good person does.

It keeps the community together. It is owed to God. It produces the best outcome.

Then match each card to the source on the sheet it could have come from.

When your table is done, hold up the one that could go in either group.

I Do 2 · connect

I Do 2 · connect

The test case does the work

Two reasons for the same value only show their difference when you push them.

So the paragraph ends with a case where they come apart.

A duty-based reason still forbids the act when the outcome would be good.

A consequence-based reason does not, and that is the whole difference in one sentence.

Choose your test case from the sheet, not from your imagination.

An invented case is not evidence and cannot be referenced.

We Do 2 · lab

Read the last sentence only

Work in twos. Read your partner only the final sentence of your paragraph.

They say which two reasons it must have been comparing.

If they cannot tell, the sentence is doing less work than you think.

Add the missing half and read it again.

Then swap over and do the same for theirs.

Independent · evidence

Independent · evidence

Grid, then paragraph

Work on your own. Fill the comparison grid before you write anything continuous.

Name the shared value, then each tradition's reason, each with a source reference.

Then write the paragraph, in the grid's order, ending on the test case.

Keep it to one paragraph. This is a practice at structure, not at length.

If you finish early, write the first line of the counter-argument you would answer next.

  • Supported · complete the grid and write two sentences from it.
  • Standard · complete the grid and write the full paragraph, ending on a test case.
  • Stretch · as Standard, and write the first line of the counter-argument you would answer next.

Exit · Audience and Influence

Exit · Audience and Influence

Read the last sentence to an adult

Read the final sentence of your paragraph to the adult at your table.

They read it back and name the two reasons they think it compares.

If they get one wrong, add the reference into the sentence and read it again.

Then choose what happens next: keep the paragraph, change the test case, or add the counter-argument.

Write the choice on the sheet with today's date before you leave.
