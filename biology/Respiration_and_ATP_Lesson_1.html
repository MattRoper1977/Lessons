Amylase.

In which part of the small intestine is digested food absorbed into the blood?

The ileum.

3. Villi (Recall)

Name two adaptations of villi that help absorption.

Any two: large surface area, microvilli, one cell thick, good blood supply, lacteals.

4. Lead-in: Energy

Once glucose has been absorbed into the blood, what do you think cells use it for?

To release energy (through respiration).

1. Enzymes (Recall)

Complete: Starch → ______ by amylase → ______ by maltase.

Maltose, then glucose.

2. Bile (Recall)

Explain the two roles of bile in digestion.

1) Neutralises stomach acid for alkaline conditions. 2) Emulsifies fats, increasing SA:V for lipase.

3. Absorption (Recall)

Explain why villi being one cell thick helps absorption.

Short diffusion pathway, so nutrients cross into the blood quickly.

4. Lead-in: Energy

Glucose is absorbed into the blood and carried to every cell. What chemical process do cells use to release the energy stored in glucose?

Respiration.

1. Enzymes (Recall)

Explain why the digestion of starch requires two separate enzymes (amylase and maltase).

Amylase breaks starch (polysaccharide) to maltose (disaccharide). Maltase breaks maltose to glucose (monosaccharide). Each enzyme is specific to one substrate.

2. Absorption (Recall)

Explain how the blood supply in a villus maintains a steep concentration gradient for absorption.

Blood constantly carries absorbed nutrients away from the ileum wall, keeping the concentration in the blood low relative to the gut, so diffusion continues rapidly.

3. Bile (Recall)

Explain how emulsification increases the rate of lipid digestion. Use the term SA:V.

Emulsification breaks large fat droplets into smaller ones, increasing SA:V. More surface area for lipase to bind to, so more fat digested per second.

4. Lead-in: Energy

Glucose contains chemical energy stored in its bonds. Suggest why cells need a controlled chemical process to release this energy rather than simply burning it.

Burning releases all energy as heat at once, which would damage cells. Respiration releases energy in small, controlled steps so cells can use it for specific processes without overheating.

I Do

I Do: Aerobic Respiration (Spec 2.34)

Key Idea: Respiration is the process by which all living organisms release energy from glucose to produce ATP.
0/2

The Word Equation

Glucose + Oxygen → Carbon Dioxide + Water + ATP

The Symbol Equation

C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + ATP

Where does it happen?

Mostly in the mitochondria – the "powerhouses" of the cell.

Cells that need lots of energy (e.g. muscle cells) have more mitochondria.

Key Facts to Remember

✅ Happens in ALL living cells (animals, plants, fungi, bacteria).

✅ Happens all the time – 24 hours a day.

✅ Aerobic respiration needs oxygen.

✅ Glucose comes from the food we digest and absorb.

✅ Oxygen comes from the air we breathe into our lungs.

The Journey So Far

Food → Digested → Absorbed → Respired in cells → ATP (energy)

The Mitochondria

🧠 Memory Trick: "Mighty Mitochondria Make ATP" – Mitochondria are the powerhouse of the cell. More mitochondria = more ATP = more energy!
Aspire: Why do muscle cells have MORE mitochondria than skin cells? Muscles need more ATP for contraction, so they need more mitochondria for more respiration.

Don't write: "Oxygen makes energy." or "Respiration makes energy."

Write: "Glucose and oxygen react to release energy as ATP."

Energy is released, not made — you can't create energy (Conservation of Energy). Always say ATP.

Talk to your partner: In your own words, what are the two things going IN and the three things coming OUT of respiration? Be ready to build it next.

I Do

I Do: ATP – The Energy Currency (Spec 2.35)

Key Idea: ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the molecule that provides energy for cells. Think of it as the body's rechargeable battery.
0/2

What is ATP?

Adenosine Triphosphate – a small molecule that stores and carries energy.

It is produced by respiration in the mitochondria.

When a cell needs energy, it breaks down ATP to release it.

ATP is the cell's rechargeable battery — respiration tops it up, cell work drains it.

⚠️ Respiration ≠ Breathing

Respiration = chemical reaction inside cells (makes ATP).

Breathing (ventilation) = moving air in/out of lungs.

Breathing delivers the oxygen that respiration needs, but they are different processes!

What does ATP provide energy for?

💪
Muscle contraction

Movement

🔄
Active transport

Moving molecules against gradient

🧬
Cell division

Growth & repair

🔧
Protein synthesis

Building new proteins

⚡
Nerve impulses

Sending signals

🌡️
Body temperature

Maintaining warmth

Aspire: A sperm cell is packed with mitochondria around its tail. Suggest why. The tail must beat constantly to swim, which needs a continuous supply of ATP. Mitochondria positioned right next to the tail respire to release that ATP exactly where it's used.
💡 Real-World Link: When you shiver in the cold, that's ATP powering muscle contractions to generate heat. When your heart beats, that's ATP. When you think, that's ATP powering nerve impulses. Every second, your body uses and regenerates about 10 million ATP molecules!

Examiners love this phrase: "Respiration releases energy from glucose to make ATP, which cells use for processes like..."

Three examples of ATP use = more marks: muscle contraction, active transport, protein synthesis, cell division.

Talk to your partner: Name three things in your body using ATP right now. Next we'll sort which processes need it and which don't.
