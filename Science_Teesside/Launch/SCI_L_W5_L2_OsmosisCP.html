I do · Worked example · 4 min

I do · a worked example

  1. Initial mass = 2.00 g; final mass = 2.20 g.
  2. Mass change = 2.20 − 2.00 = +0.20 g.
  3. Percentage change = 0.20 ÷ 2.00 × 100 = +10%.
  4. A positive change is consistent with net water entry; explain it using the membrane and the concentration difference.

Check a common mistake

A 0.2 g gain means different relative changes for a 1 g and a 4 g sample. Compare percentages rather than raw gains alone.

Before the reveal, predict the next step or explain why it follows.

Practical / observation route · method and recording

Adult-prepared equal potato cylinders, labelled sucrose solutions, labelled cups, balance, forceps, paper towel, timer and recording sheet. Use the local risk assessment; do not taste anything.

  1. Choose the route: use the supplied data, or measure prepared samples. The full soak takes about 30 minutes and is not an extra activity to squeeze into this lesson.
  2. For a full investigation, use equal-size cylinders from the same potato source. Label 0.0, 0.2, 0.4 and 0.6 mol dm⁻³ solutions. Keep volume, time and temperature the same.
  3. Blot each cylinder consistently. Measure and record its initial mass before it enters the solution.
  4. Submerge one piece in each solution. Start timing. A class can repeat each concentration with separate pieces.
  5. After the agreed time, remove pieces with forceps. Blot with the same method and record final mass.
  6. Calculate (final mass − initial mass) ÷ initial mass × 100. Keep the sign of the change.
  7. Compare the concentrations. Keep original readings and check any unexpected result rather than silently removing it.
  8. For prepared samples, use the recorded initial masses. State which measurements you personally made. Data analysis alone does not demonstrate that you carried out the practical.

No pupil cutting is needed. Adults prepare pieces and handle damaged glass. Wipe spills promptly. Report evidence from the route actually used.

Staff key · Shared work

Independent variable: Solution concentration.

Dependent variable: Percentage change in mass.

Control / consistent method: Immersion time; Tissue dimensions; Temperature; Blotting technique.

Worked example

  1. Initial mass = 2.00 g; final mass = 2.20 g.
  2. Mass change = 2.20 − 2.00 = +0.20 g.
  3. Percentage change = 0.20 ÷ 2.00 × 100 = +10%.
  4. A positive change is consistent with net water entry; explain it using the membrane and the concentration difference.

Evidence choices

A potato changes from 2.00 g to 2.30 g. What is the percentage change?
+15%: divide the 0.30 g gain by the 2.00 g start.

Which makes the comparison fairer?
Use the same time and blotting method for each concentration.

Changed: sucrose concentration. Measured: initial and final mass, used to calculate percentage change.

2. Calculate all four percentage changes.

0.0: +15%; 0.2: +5%; 0.4: −5%; 0.6: −15%.

A gain is consistent with water entering by osmosis from a more dilute external solution. A loss is consistent with water leaving into a more concentrated external solution.

Keep time and tissue dimensions, or other justified factors, the same. Blotting reduces variable surface solution on the tissue.

Staff answers · You do · Stretch

1. Why is final mass alone less useful than percentage change?

Initial masses may differ. Percentage change relates the change to each starting mass.

2. A 1.60 g piece finishes at 1.68 g. Compare its percentage change with the 0.2 row.

(1.68 − 1.60) ÷ 1.60 × 100 = +5%, the same relative gain.

3. Why should an individual mass change not be treated as perfect proof on its own?

Measurement and handling errors, surface liquid and tissue differences can also affect measured mass.

Use replicates at each concentration, consistently blot, or use finer concentration intervals; explain the corresponding improvement.
