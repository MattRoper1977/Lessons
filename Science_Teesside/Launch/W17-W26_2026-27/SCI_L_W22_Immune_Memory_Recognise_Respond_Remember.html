Shared evidence | Defence and memory cards

Models and teaching summaries, not personal medical guidance.

Barrier card

Skin limits entry. Airway mucus traps material and cilia move it towards the throat.

Chemical defence card

Lysozyme in tears can damage bacterial cell walls. Stomach acid kills many swallowed pathogens.

Recognition card

An antigen triggers a specific response. Some lymphocytes develop into antibody-secreting cells.

Antibody card

Antibodies are proteins with specific binding sites. Binding helps neutralise a target or marks it for other immune action.

Memory card

Some lymphocytes persist as memory cells. Later exposure to the same antigen can trigger a faster, larger response.

Vaccine card

Vaccines expose the immune system to antigens, or instructions for making them, to develop protection and memory without needing the full disease. Protection is not a guarantee.

Current stage

Objective

Explain how barriers and the specific immune response protect the body, including memory after immunisation.

Preparation

  • Print the shared evidence page, one chosen response route and exit page. Routes are alternatives, not a workload ladder.
  • Read the teacher answers before teaching. All numerical sample data in this lesson are constructed for learning.

Method precautions

  • Paper and screen tasks; no biological sampling, cultures, medicine handling or personal health disclosures.

Practical alternative

All evidence is included. Point, annotate, dictate or discuss quietly instead of writing or using a device.

Access and regulation

Supported: Same core scientific goal, with word bank, short prompts, read-aloud or pointing/dictation. Do not assign a route from diagnosis.

Standard: Use the supplied evidence to explain and justify an independent scientific decision.

Stretch: Optional challenge after secure core: evaluate a limitation and transfer the science. Higher-tier-only material, where present, is labelled explicitly.

Regulation: Preview the sequence. Offer solo or partner work, a pass and quiet response. Allow a brief reset and re-entry at the next numbered task; no compulsory disclosure, performance or speed competition.

Teacher answers

Retrieval

R1 disease-causing agent. R2 a barrier reduces pathogen entry. R3 cells and proteins are different; proteins are molecules made by cells.

W1–W3

W1 exposure triggers antibody response and memory formation; memory retained supports a faster later response to the same antigen. W2 no guaranteed binding to Y because its antigen structure differs; shape drawing is a simplified specificity model. W3 day 6 first 6, later 32, difference 26 arbitrary units; memory explanation, constructed-data limit.

Hinge

A false: protein versus cell. B true: specific memory supports faster secondary response. C false: recognition is specific. D false: antibiotics act against bacterial processes, not by generating specific immune memory.

S1 / M1

Skin physically reduces entry; mucus traps material and cilia move it towards throat; stomach hydrochloric acid kills many swallowed pathogens; lysozyme in tears can damage bacterial cell walls. M1 requires one physical and one chemical mechanism.

S2 / M2

Antigen: recognised molecular feature/target that triggers response. Antibody: specific binding protein made by specialised lymphocyte-derived cells. Memory lymphocyte: long-lived cell supporting a faster later response. Core answer need not name B cells/plasma cells.

S3

Day 4 first 2, later 20;20−2=18 arbitrary units more in later response.

S4 / M4

Memory lymphocytes form/are retained; later exposure to the same antigen can trigger faster response. Vaccination supplies antigen or instructions for making antigen, stimulating response and memory without requiring the full disease. Graph is constructed and immunity varies; no individual guarantee.

M3

Day 6 first 6, later 32; difference 26 arbitrary units. Same-antigen memory supports the faster/larger later response. This is evidence within a teaching model, not clinical proof.

T1

Specific binding sites need compatible antigen features. Different Y may not bind. Simple shapes omit molecular detail and the many other cell/protein interactions in immunity.

T2

Later reaches 20 at day 4. First does not reach 20 in the observed 0–10 day interval. Do not say it never reaches 20 or extrapolate beyond the supplied data.

T3

False inference. Antibody amount can fall while memory lymphocytes persist. Falling proteins are not evidence that every memory cell disappeared.

T4

Antibiotics act against susceptible bacteria and do not treat viral infection. Vaccination stimulates a specific immune response and memory; it is not an antibiotic. No clinical selection/dosing required.

Exit and assessment

E 1 accurate barrier+action. E 2 protein. E 3 retained memory lymphocytes support a faster specific response on later exposure to same antigen. L1 accepts a specific correction or evidence-backed confirmation. Secure core requires roles+same antigen+faster response; reteach with cell makes protein if roles are mixed.

Word help

barrier
A defence that reduces entry of pathogens.
antigen
A molecular feature recognised by the immune system that can trigger a specific response.
antibody
A protein that binds to a specific antigen and helps defend against it.
lymphocyte
A type of white blood cell involved in specific immune responses.
memory lymphocyte
A long-lived lymphocyte that supports a faster response to a previously encountered antigen.
immunisation
Stimulating protection against a disease, commonly by vaccination.
