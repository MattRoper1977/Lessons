A whale has finger bones. Why?

Learning intention

Interpret fossil, anatomical and DNA evidence to justify a claim about evolution and state a limitation.

Today: use evidence to write a museum caption.

You may explain by speaking, writing or annotating.

Success looks like

  • Identify what an evidence source actually shows.
  • Use two different evidence types to explain shared ancestry or change over time.
  • Read the DNA graph accurately and avoid claiming that one living species changed into another.

Pakicetus: about 50 million years ago, four walking legs. Ambulocetus: about 50–48 million years ago, large feet suited to movement in water. Dorudon: about 40–33 million years ago, flippers and very small hind legs. Approximate ages and broad features: Natural History Museum.

A human forelimb and a five-digit whale flipper share corresponding regions: one upper bone, two lower bones, wrist bones and digits. Their shapes and functions differ. This shared arrangement is called homologous.

Slide 1

TA Brief

Current stage

Objective

Interpret fossil, anatomical and DNA evidence to justify a claim about evolution and state a limitation.

Preparation

  • Print evidence page for each pupil or pair; select one response route after retrieval.
  • Keep the answers separate. Pre-teach Ma = million years ago and that a larger Ma value is older.
  • Explain that Q/A/B/C DNA values are invented practice data; real fossil ages and features are sourced summaries.

Method precautions

  • Paper and screen lesson; no live organisms, biological cultures or chemical work.

Practical alternative

All evidence is supplied for offline use. Discuss, point, annotate or dictate instead of cutting cards or writing extended prose.

Access and regulation

Supported: Same scientific objective, with chunked cards, word bank, read-aloud access and short evidence stems. Accept accurately explained oral or annotated responses.

Standard: Use two evidence sources and a reasoned explanation independently.

Stretch: Evaluate limitations, explain a mechanism and transfer to unfamiliar evidence.

Regulation: Offer solo or partner working and a quiet response route. Preview the sequence; allow a brief reset and re-entry at the next numbered task. No compulsory performance or timed competition.

Teacher answers

R1–R3 retrieval

R1 DNA; genes are sections of DNA. R2 populations across generations, not an individual changing during its lifetime. R3 50 Ma is older than 33 Ma; Ma means million years ago.

W1 museum label

Edit/reject the direct-ancestry claim. Observation: corresponding limb bones are present. Supported inference: shared ancestry, followed by modification of limb form and function. Humans are not presented as the ancestors of modern whales. Fossil relatives with different combinations of limb features support change through time.

W2 DNA label

Q and A differ at 2 of the 100 sampled positions, so even this sample is not identical. Their greater similarity supports a recent shared ancestor compared with Q/C, not that living A became Q. Whole genomes were not measured.

W3 combine evidence

Independent sources can agree on an explanation and compensate for limitations of a single source; fossil ages/features and corresponding anatomy offer different kinds of evidence. More evidence can also expose conflicts and prompt revision.

Hinge A–D

B is best: fewer differences in corresponding sampled DNA support closer relatedness. A wrongly identifies a living species as the direct ancestor. C wrongly treats larger difference as no ancestry. D ignores two differences and extends a small sample to entire genomes.

S1 / M1 fossil comparison

Pakicetus is older (about 50 Ma) and had walking legs; Dorudon is younger (about 40–33 Ma) with flippers and very small hind legs. Different limb combinations in dated relatives support evolutionary change through time. They do not on their own establish a direct ancestor chain.

S2 / M2 anatomical reasoning

Corresponding one-upper/two-lower bone regions, wrist and digits suggest inherited organisation from a common ancestor, with forms modified in descendant lineages. They are homologous even though function differs. Accept a labelled diagram plus an accurate explanation.

S3 / M3 graph

A is most similar to Q in the sample: 2 differences per 100 aligned positions, compared with 11 for B and 24 for C. Therefore Q/A is the closest supported pair involving Q. Do not generalise to identical entire genomes.

S4 / M4 completed panel and next evidence

Model: “Older whale relatives had walking limbs, while later relatives such as Dorudon had flippers and tiny hind limbs. The shared arrangement of mammalian forelimb bones supports shared ancestry with modification. Fossils are incomplete and these examples do not prove the exact direct ancestor sequence.” For M4 accept comparing more corresponding DNA regions, analysing more dated fossil skeletons, or adding independent anatomical evidence with a stated purpose.

T1 corrected claims

Claim 1 overstates completeness and direct descent: the fossil evidence supports change among whale relatives, but does not establish every ancestor or all branches. Claim 2 extends beyond a short sample and ignores two differences: Q/A are similar in those sampled positions; whole-genome identity is not shown.

T2 second DNA region

Yes. Q/A again has the fewest differences (4 rather than 14 or 25), so a second region supports the same closest relationship. The two regions are still samples; evolutionary rates can differ and wider comparative evidence is needed. Do not calculate an exact divergence date.

T3 next check

Accept a testable proposal, for example compare a third corresponding DNA region in Q/A/B/C; predict Q/A will be most similar; agreement raises confidence, disagreement requires examining sampling, alignment or the model. For fossils: seek more securely dated limb material and explain the expected pattern, allowing branches rather than a forced ladder.

T4 integrated caption

Example: “Dated whale relatives show changes in limb form, and corresponding mammalian forelimb bones support shared ancestry. Similarity in comparable DNA provides another line of evidence, although fossil gaps and DNA sampling limit exact reconstructions.” Must integrate at least two types with no claim of a living direct ancestor.

E1–E3 exit

E1 Q/A: 2 differences in 100 sampled aligned positions. E2 shared ancestry, followed by modification in descendant groups. E3 accept exact ancestors, an exact split date, complete fossil sequence, or whole genome similarity from 100 positions.

L1 and next step

Answers vary; expect a specific question and an observable revision, such as replacing “proves” with “supports” or adding the sampled quantity. Act on the selected next step; do not grade confidence as knowledge.

Word help

evolution
Change in inherited characteristics of populations over generations.
fossil
Preserved remains or traces of past organisms.
common ancestor
An ancestral population shared by two or more descendant groups.
homologous
Having a shared evolutionary origin, even when function differs.
inference
An explanation supported by evidence and reasoning.
limitation
A reason the evidence cannot answer every part of a question.

T3. Design the next check: choose a new DNA region, a new fossil or a new anatomical feature. State a prediction and how the result could change confidence.
