Enzyme Action: Preparing the pH Investigation

Learning objective

I can explain enzyme specificity and denaturation, then identify the variables and evidence needed to investigate the effect of pH on amylase safely.

Success looks like

  • Use enzyme, substrate, active site and specificity accurately in an explanation.
  • Explain how an unsuitable pH can change an active site's shape and reduce enzyme activity.
  • Identify pH as the independent variable, time to starch disappearance as the dependent variable, and suitable control variables.

We will observe, work together, then use the evidence ourselves.

Arrival task

Arrival task

Choose a route. Think first, then check the answers together.

1. Catalyst and substrate

An enzyme is a biological catalyst: it speeds a reaction without being used up. Choose: speeds up / always stops a reaction.

The fact is in the first sentence.

Answer: Speeds up a reaction without being used up. Enzyme action is introduced today; this is a supplied starting fact.

2. Substrate or comparison

Amylase breaks down starch. Which is its substrate here: starch, protein or lipid?

Substrate means the substance the enzyme acts on.

Answer: Starch. Amylase breaks starch into smaller sugars.

3. Controls or repeats

To test the effect of pH, should we change pH only or change pH and temperature together?

Keep other rate factors the same.

Answer: Change pH only, while keeping temperature and other relevant conditions the same.

4. Changed factor and endpoint

We change pH and measure a time. Which is the independent variable: pH or time?

Independent means the factor we deliberately change.

Answer: pH. Time is the measured outcome, or dependent variable.

1. Catalyst and substrate

Starting fact: an enzyme is a biological catalyst. A catalyst speeds a reaction without being used up. Describe its job.

Use both parts of the supplied definition.

Answer: It speeds up the reaction and is not used up, so it can act again.

2. Substrate or comparison

Starting fact: amylase breaks starch into smaller sugars. Name the substrate and the enzyme.

The substrate is what is acted on.

Answer: Substrate: starch. Enzyme: amylase.

3. Controls or repeats

Why keep temperature the same when testing the effect of pH on a reaction?

Temperature can also affect reaction speed.

Answer: So a change in time or rate is not confused with a temperature effect. It makes the comparison more valid for investigating pH.

4. Changed factor and endpoint

An investigation changes pH and times a reaction endpoint. Name the independent and dependent variables.

Changed factor versus measured outcome.

Answer: Independent: pH. Dependent: time to the defined reaction endpoint, measured in seconds.

1. Catalyst and substrate

Starting fact: catalysts speed reactions without being used up. How is the role of amylase different from the role of starch?

Amylase is the enzyme; starch is its substrate.

Answer: Amylase catalyses the reaction and can act again. Starch is the substrate converted into smaller sugar products.

2. Substrate or comparison

Two trials use different pH values AND different amylase concentrations. Why is this a problem?

Can an effect be attributed to pH alone?

Answer: Both factors could change the rate, so a difference cannot be attributed to pH alone. Keep amylase concentration constant.

3. Controls or repeats

Why repeat a reaction-time measurement at the same pH?

Think about variation and a doubtful result.

Answer: Repeats show variation, help identify doubtful runs and allow a more reliable summary of the time at that pH. They do not remove a systematic error by themselves.

4. Changed factor and endpoint

Why must everyone agree what counts as the reaction endpoint before starting a timed comparison?

The stopping rule is part of the measurement.

Answer: Otherwise different stopping rules may create apparent time differences. A shared observable endpoint makes the comparison more consistent.

Starter · what can iodine tell us?

Blue-black

Orange-brown remains

Starch is not detected in the sampled drop.

These are separate iodine tests on a spotting tile. The colours are supported by words; colour alone is not the answer.

Which observation shows starch is still present? Which might help us decide when the reaction has reached an endpoint?

Separate the observed colour from what it tells you about starch.

Define an observable endpoint; avoid claiming what the test does not detect.

I do · connect pH to the measure

pH can affect an enzyme’s active site and activity.

At an unsuitable pH, binding and catalysis may be less effective.

Denaturation is a change in an enzyme’s three-dimensional structure that disrupts its active site and function.

We will investigate which tested pH gives the shortest endpoint time.

A complementary active site can bind this substrate.

The model does not give a universal optimum pH. Reduced activity alone does not prove permanent denaturation.

Which observation is our endpoint?

pH measures acidity or alkalinity. For matched starting amounts and the same endpoint, shorter time means a higher relative rate.

Stretch

1. Predict a testable pH–activity pattern. Explain specificity and reuse without claiming that reduced activity proves permanent denaturation.

Help with this question

Connect mechanism to a measurable prediction. A shape model illustrates a possibility; it does not establish the pH optimum.

2. Specify the IV, operational DV and four controls. Justify two controls against competing explanations.

Help with this question

Operational means another person can apply the same stopping rule. Temperature and concentration could alter activity independently of pH.

3. Order method cards A–F; explain why iodine goes into separate test drops and why a consistent start matters.

Help with this question

Timing must start consistently with mixing. The detector must not become a second changed condition.

4. Distinguish the no-active-amylase control from a control variable. What would an unexpected orange-brown result make you check?

Help with this question

Use expected starch presence to reason backwards. An unexpected control questions validity before it supports a mechanism.

5. Illustrative samples: blue-black at 90 s, orange-brown at 120 s, sampled every 30 s. State the recorded time, the interval containing the threshold crossing, and one precision improvement. For another run with no endpoint by 300 s, choose a truthful table entry.

Help with this question

The first negative starch sample is an upper sampling bound. Choose headings pH / trial / time or no endpoint by… / observation; explain what your improvement addresses.

Exit

Exit · mechanism, measure, control

Use your book OR one ticket. Show your explanation with the supplied evidence.

Plan: investigate how pH affects amylase acting on starch. Iodine turns blue-black when starch is detected; it remains orange-brown when starch is not detected. Today is preparation for the later investigation; no reaction results have been collected.

1. Why can amylase act on starch and then act again? Use the model or complete: ‘Starch fits the ___. The enzyme is not ___.’

Help with this question

Word bank: active site · used up · product. Choose the words that explain binding and reuse.

Answer guidance: Starch fits the complementary active site. The enzyme is not used up, so it can catalyse another reaction. Accept correct labelled pointing with explanation or exact scribing.

2. For the pH test, name what we change, what we time and one thing we keep the same.

Help with this question

Use the headings Change / Time until… / Keep the same. Use the detector key to finish the timing rule.

Answer guidance: Change pH. Time until a sample leaves iodine orange-brown, in seconds. Keep a relevant factor such as temperature or enzyme/starch volume or concentration the same.

1. Explain why amylase acts on starch and can be used again.

Help with this question

Link complementary fit at the active site to specificity; then distinguish enzyme from substrate.

Answer guidance: The active site has a complementary fit with the starch substrate, enabling catalysis. Products are released and the enzyme is not used up, so it can act again.

2. Name the IV, define the timed DV with units, and name one control with a reason.

Help with this question

The DV needs an observable endpoint. Explain how changing another rate factor would affect a comparison of pH.

Answer guidance: IV: pH. DV: time / s until a sample leaves iodine orange-brown. For example, keep temperature constant because it also affects enzyme activity and could confuse the effect of pH.

1. Explain enzyme specificity and reuse. Predict how an unsuitable pH could affect this mechanism.

Help with this question

Link active-site shape, binding and product release. Do not assume that every reduction in activity proves permanent denaturation.

Answer guidance: A complementary active site enables substrate binding and catalysis; products leave and the enzyme remains available. Unsuitable pH can alter binding or catalysis, sometimes through structural change, reducing activity. Reduced activity alone does not establish permanent denaturation.

2. Specify the IV, endpoint-time DV with units, and one justified control for a valid pH comparison.

Help with this question

Make the stopping rule observable and show how the chosen control prevents a competing explanation.

Answer guidance: IV: mixture pH. DV: time / s to the first sampled drop leaving iodine orange-brown. A justified matched factor such as temperature, enzyme/starch concentration or sampling interval reduces a competing cause of time differences.

Word help

enzyme

A biological catalyst that speeds a reaction without being used up.

substrate

The substance an enzyme acts on.

active site

The region where the substrate binds and the reaction is catalysed.

specificity

An enzyme acts on particular substrates because of its complementary active site.

endpoint

The agreed observable point at which timing is recorded.

control variable

A factor kept the same to make the comparison valid.

denaturation

A change in an enzyme’s three-dimensional structure that disrupts the active site and its function. Unsuitable pH or high temperature can cause this.

Teacher answers · W8L1

Shared activities

Model: 1 amylase enzyme; 2 starch substrate; 3 active site; 4 smaller sugar products. The enzyme is not used up. Method order F, B, D, A, C, E, allowing F/B swap. Blue-black detects starch; the first orange-brown remaining sample gives the endpoint. No-active-amylase control expected blue-black.

Supported arrival

  • 1. Speeds up a reaction without being used up. Enzyme action is introduced today; this is a supplied starting fact.
  • 2. Starch. Amylase breaks starch into smaller sugars.
  • 3. Change pH only, while keeping temperature and other relevant conditions the same.
  • 4. pH. Time is the measured outcome, or dependent variable.

Supported independent task

  • 1. Amylase: enzyme; starch: substrate; not used up.
  • 2. Change pH; measure endpoint time; keep temperature the same because it also affects activity.
  • 3. F, B, D, A, C, E. F and B may swap if complete before mixing.
  • 4. Endpoint: orange-brown remains. No-active-amylase comparison: blue-black, because starch should remain detectable.
  • 5. Use the supplied four headings. Record “no endpoint by 300 s”; do not invent zero time or zero rate.

Supported exit

  • 1. Starch fits the complementary active site. The enzyme is not used up, so it can catalyse another reaction. Accept correct labelled pointing with explanation or exact scribing.
  • 2. Change pH. Time until a sample leaves iodine orange-brown, in seconds. Keep a relevant factor such as temperature or enzyme/starch volume or concentration the same.

Standard arrival

  • 1. It speeds up the reaction and is not used up, so it can act again.
  • 2. Substrate: starch. Enzyme: amylase.
  • 3. So a change in time or rate is not confused with a temperature effect. It makes the comparison more valid for investigating pH.
  • 4. Independent: pH. Dependent: time to the defined reaction endpoint, measured in seconds.

Standard independent task

  • 1. pH may affect activity, giving different endpoint times; the fastest tested condition is to be found. Products leave and amylase is not used up.
  • 2. IV pH; DV time / s to the first sampled drop leaving iodine orange-brown. Controls include temperature, concentrations, volumes and sampling conditions. Temperature also affects activity.
  • 3. F, B, D, A, C, E (F/B may swap). Separate iodine drops avoid changing the main reaction mixture with the detector.
  • 4. A control variable is kept the same; a control sample is an additional comparison. Without active amylase starch should remain detectable, giving blue-black.
  • 5. Record “no endpoint by 300 s”; leave numerical time/rate uncalculated for that run. 1/time compares matched starting amounts and endpoints; it is not product mass per second.

Standard exit

  • 1. The active site has a complementary fit with the starch substrate, enabling catalysis. Products are released and the enzyme is not used up, so it can act again.
  • 2. IV: pH. DV: time / s until a sample leaves iodine orange-brown. For example, keep temperature constant because it also affects enzyme activity and could confuse the effect of pH.

Stretch arrival

  • 1. Amylase catalyses the reaction and can act again. Starch is the substrate converted into smaller sugar products.
  • 2. Both factors could change the rate, so a difference cannot be attributed to pH alone. Keep amylase concentration constant.
  • 3. Repeats show variation, help identify doubtful runs and allow a more reliable summary of the time at that pH. They do not remove a systematic error by themselves.
  • 4. Otherwise different stopping rules may create apparent time differences. A shared observable endpoint makes the comparison more consistent.

Stretch independent task

  • 1. pH may alter binding/catalysis, changing endpoint time. A complementary active site binds starch; products leave and the catalyst is not used up. Slower activity alone cannot establish permanent denaturation.
  • 2. IV mixture pH; DV time / s to first orange-brown sample. Match temperature, enzyme/starch concentration and volume, total volume, start/mixing and sampling conditions. Link at least two to plausible rate/timing confounds.
  • 3. F, B, D, A, C, E, allowing F/B swap. Separate iodine avoids altering the reaction; a consistent mixing/timer start prevents an artificial time difference.
  • 4. A variable is matched; the control sample lacks active enzyme. Blue-black expected. If orange-brown, check starting starch, reagent function, sample identity and contamination before interpreting enzyme action.
  • 5. Record 120 s; threshold crossed after 90 and by 120 s, not exactly 105 s. A shorter feasible fixed sampling interval reduces interval uncertainty. Record “no endpoint by 300 s”; no invented numerical rate.

Stretch exit

  • 1. A complementary active site enables substrate binding and catalysis; products leave and the enzyme remains available. Unsuitable pH can alter binding or catalysis, sometimes through structural change, reducing activity. Reduced activity alone does not establish permanent denaturation.
  • 2. IV: mixture pH. DV: time / s to the first sampled drop leaving iodine orange-brown. A justified matched factor such as temperature, enzyme/starch concentration or sampling interval reduces a competing cause of time differences.
