LAUNCH · GCSE BIOLOGY FOUNDATION · W11 · LESSON 3 · 5 MINUTES

Watch the model

In a simulated study, did more people improve in the treatment group than in the comparison group?

Worked explanation · reveal after thinking

The 20-person groups and all outcomes are simulated for arithmetic and evaluation. Improvement and adverse events may occur in the same person. They are not mutually exclusive outcomes. Do not diagnose, recommend treatment, ask for family histories or require pupils to disclose a personal ethical view.

32-second silent captioned explainer · optional

No sound or autoplay. Use instead of part of the modelling time, not as an extra activity.

Full transcript

Ask a bounded question. In a simulated study, did more people improve in the treatment group than in the comparison group?

Read the denominator. Twelve of twenty improved in the treatment group. Six of twenty improved in the comparison group.

Check possible harm. Four of twenty had an adverse event in the treatment group; one of twenty in the comparison group.

Keep limits visible. The groups are small and follow-up lasts eight weeks. These invented data do not establish long-term safety or a cure.

Work through one example

Worked example

Improve the sentence: “The cells worked in a dish, so the treatment must be safe.”

Step 1

Keep the observation: cells showed a useful feature in a dish.

Step 2

Remove the unsupported jump to safety in people.

Step 3

Add the gap: human benefits and harms still need study.

Step 4

Conclude: the finding may justify further research, not a safety guarantee.

Ethics reading and contrasting considerations

Embryonic stem-cell research can involve using early embryos; views differ about their moral status and the acceptability of using them for possible health benefits. Informed consent, fairness and careful evidence matter. Adult stem cells avoid that particular embryo-source concern but usually have more limited potential. You may explain contrasting arguments without stating a personal belief.

Worked explanation · reveal after thinking

The simulated treatment group improved more often (60% versus 30%), suggesting possible benefit. However, adverse events were also more common (20% versus 5%). Small groups and short follow-up leave uncertainty. A larger, well-controlled study with informed consent and longer follow-up would be needed before broader claims.

Knowledge organiser

LAUNCH · W11 · Lesson 3 · Shared resource

Learning objective

Construct a balanced discussion using evidence, ethical considerations and a qualified conclusion.

Key words

Evidence — Observations or measurements used to support a claim.

Comparison group — A group used to help judge a treatment effect.

Benefit — A helpful outcome.

Risk — The possibility of harm.

Uncertainty — Something the evidence cannot yet establish.

Ethical judgement — A reasoned view about what ought to be done.

Remember

• Stem cell transplants are established for some blood conditions.

• A result in a dish is not proof of a safe treatment in people.

• Benefits, risks and ethical views are different kinds of consideration.

• Use group sizes, a comparison and follow-up before judging a claim.

Success looks like

• Include a supported benefit and a risk.

• Distinguish scientific evidence from ethical reasoning.

• Reach a qualified judgement and refine one overclaim.

Route answers · Standard

LAUNCH · W11 · Lesson 3 · Standard

Arrival

1. Benefit: Treatment group.

2. Risk: Treatment group.

3. Ethics: An ethical question.

4. Discuss: No.

Independent / application

1. Plan: Benefit: 60 versus 30%; risk: 20 versus 5%; uncertainty: size/follow-up.

2. Discuss: Accept an accurate benefit and risk, a clear limitation and a proportionate conclusion; do not mark agreement with the teacher.

3. Refine: For example: “It cures people” becomes “The simulated group had more improvement, but this does not establish a cure”.

Two-check exit

1. Build: A supported benefit, a risk or limitation, and a qualified conclusion.

2. Conclude: For example: a possible improvement benefit, 60% versus 30%, but small groups and short follow-up leave uncertainty.

Responsive feedback within the science

During We do, the lesson teacher is the named audience. Offer one optional request: clearer labels, fewer cards, reading help, adult-directed handling or more thinking time. Explain what can change, try it and check its effect. Passing or asking privately is valid. No compulsory form or written conversion of speech. Do not infer school marking codes from clicks, saved answers or a completed box.

Using routes and evidence

Supported, Standard and Stretch are alternative scaffolds, not fixed pupil labels. Scribe exact words; distinguish access support from content prompting. Use current school policy separately; automated checks do not demonstrate participation or award evidence.
