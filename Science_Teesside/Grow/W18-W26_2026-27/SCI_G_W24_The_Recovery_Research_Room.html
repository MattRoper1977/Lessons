Slide 1

TA Brief

Current stage

Objective

Use supplied sources and repeated sample data to present a cautious explanation of heart-rate recovery.

Preparation

  • Make clear that every number is constructed sample data, not a pupil or actual volunteer record.
  • No physical trial or online research is required. Print page 1 and the selected route.

Method precautions

  • No exercise challenge, pulse measurement or comparison of pupils’ bodies.
  • Do not interpret the fictional rates as diagnosis, fitness rankings or age-specific normal limits.
  • If adapting to a later real practical, the school must plan it separately for individual access and relevant health needs. This pack supplies a complete paper route.

Practical alternative

Analyse supplied constructed records and paraphrased primary-source summaries. Present as a paper research briefing; no actual trial is needed.

Access and regulation

Supported: Read one trial; choose a relevant source and use a briefing frame.

Standard: Compare repeats and present claim/evidence/explanation/limit.

Stretch: Calculate selected means and evaluate a causal overclaim and test design.

Regulation: Offer quiet pointing, private writing, drawing or adult scribing. Pass-and-return for public questions. Use fictional cases; no personal medical disclosure or comparison of bodies. Assign response routes from current evidence, not fixed pupil labels.

Teacher answers

R1–R3

R1: blood. R2: working muscles need more energy released in respiration, usually requiring more oxygen delivery. R3: 96 beats per minute is a measurement with units.

W1–W3

W1: all trials fall towards 72: A 120→80, B 116→76 and C 124→84 beats per minute from immediately after activity to two minutes later. Final values remain above 72. W2: source A, NHLBI, directly explains heart-rate response and oxygen demand; B provides general context; C lacks attribution, method and supporting evidence. W3: make a bounded claim about sample trials, not a health ranking. A, B and C are repeats for one fictional person.

Hinge A–C

A is incorrect: trial labels are not people and rates cannot rank health. B is correct: each trial shows a fall during recovery. C is incorrect: 80, 76 and 84 are not equal to 72.

S1–S5

S1: three repeat trials. S2: 120 to 80 beats per minute. S3: no, 80 is still above 72. S4: oxygen. S5 example: In this sample trial, heart rate fell during recovery. Trial A fell from 120 to 80 beats per minute over two minutes. These constructed records cannot diagnose or prove overall health.

N1–N4

N1: appropriate pairs from any two trials, showing a common fall but not a return to 72. N2: 120 − 96 = 24 beats per minute. N3: source A, NHLBI; the advert has no author, method or supporting evidence and overclaims what one value can show. N4: bounded claim, accurate values and units, an oxygen-delivery explanation credited to NHLBI, and a limit such as constructed data, one fictional person, few repeats or limited recovery times.

T1–T4

T1: immediate mean = (120 + 116 + 124) ÷ 3 = 120 beats per minute; one-minute mean = (96 + 92 + 100) ÷ 3 = 96 beats per minute. T2: the mean falls by 24 beats per minute. This does not establish a universal response, especially from constructed data for one person. T3: any two controls among activity, duration, room, recovery posture and measurement timing or method; each reduces an avoidable comparison difference. A useful improvement is more trials to explore variation, longer follow-up, or a clearer standardised measurement procedure. T4: report three sample trials for one fictional person, an accurate recovery pattern, numerical evidence, explanation and a limit.

E1–E4 / reflection

E1: accurate paired values and units. E2: NHLBI for the heart-rate and oxygen-delivery explanation; NHS is acceptable for broad activity context if relevance is clear. E3: no clinical diagnosis, health ranking, claim of real measurement or universal conclusion. E4: one genuine control with an explanation of its comparison purpose. Reflection accepts a precise question for the next paper dataset.

Assessment and re-teach

Secure: uses accurate values and units, identifies a relevant source, explains a heart-rate response and states an appropriate limit. If a health ranking appears, circle the trial labels and ask how many people are represented. If a pattern lacks numbers, require one paired comparison. If a mean is confused with a total, model total divided by number of trials.

Word help

heart rate
The number of heartbeats per minute.
recovery
The period after an activity while body responses move back towards a resting state.
repeat
Another trial using the same planned method.
variation
Differences between measurements or trials.
mean
The total of the values divided by the number of values.
limitation
A reason to restrict how far a conclusion can reach.
