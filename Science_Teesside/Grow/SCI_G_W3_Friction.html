Opening

GROW · SCIENCE · WEEK 3 · LESSON A · 2 MINUTES

Friction: Friend and Enemy

Learning objective

I can explain friction and compare surface-test evidence.

Success looks like

  • I can identify the touching surfaces and the direction of friction on a sliding object.
  • I can explain when friction helps or hinders an intended job.
  • I can compare repeated results and qualify my conclusion.

One force, different jobs. Explore grip and unwanted rubbing, then investigate a sliding block using real measurements or clearly labelled teaching data.

Two separate lessons

A · Explore and explain · 40 minutes

B · Friction: Test the Surfaces · 40 minutes

Stop after Lesson A. Lesson B has its own start and exit.

Point, speak, sign, move cards, draw, write or direct an adult. Choose one route; no extra participation form.

Standard · 4 questions

2. Force arrow

Help beside the question
Track the contact.

Answer guidance

3. Useful force

Name the touching parts in a rim brake.

Help beside the question

4. Compare fairly

Help beside the question

Stretch · 4 questions

1. Pair matters

Why does “roughest surface wins” need checking?

Help beside the question

Answer guidance

Answer: Appearance alone does not determine the actual contact resistance.

2. Helpful or not

When is rubbing in an engine unhelpful?

Help beside the question
State the intended job.

Answer guidance

Answer: When smooth movement is wanted; rubbing can cause unwanted heating and wear.

3. Arrow precision

Why say friction ON the block in this example?

Help beside the question
Identify the object and relative slide.

Answer guidance

Answer: The direction depends on the object and the contact motion being described.

4. Evidence limit

Does one stopping distance measure friction force directly?

Help beside the question
What is the unit being recorded?

Answer guidance

Answer: No. Distance in cm is indirect evidence under controlled conditions.

Worked explanation · reveal after thinking

Friction opposes relative sliding or its tendency at a contact; it need not act opposite the overall travel of every object. The arrow here is the friction ON a block sliding right across a fixed surface. Smooth surfaces can have friction. Results depend on the actual pair and conditions, not a universal roughest-to-smoothest ranking. Shorter stopping distance is evidence of greater resistance in this controlled model, not a direct force measurement.

Brake pad grips a rim to slow a wheel.: Resisting the pad–rim sliding helps the intended stopping job.

Shoe sole grips the ground to avoid slipping.: Friction at the shoe–ground contact can help prevent slipping.

Hand grips a door handle to turn it.: Grip lets the hand turn the handle rather than slide over it.

Moving engine parts rub when smooth movement is wanted.: Unwanted friction can heat and wear the parts.

Box rubs on carpet when easy sliding is wanted.: Friction resists the intended sliding of the box.

A dry axle rubs when free turning is wanted.: The contact can resist the desired movement.

Two pieces of rubber touch. No job is stated.: The intended job and motion are needed to judge helpful or unhelpful.

Investigate / workshop

GROW · SCIENCE · WEEK 3 · LESSON B · 20 MINUTES

Investigate, test and explain

Adult secures a low ramp, the same small sliding block, level surface samples and a tray or padded stop. Release without a push; keep hands out of the run. No oil, powders, rubbing skin or pupils sliding on floors. Directing or analysing the supplied data is an equal route.

4 min: use the arrival to identify the surface pair and test plan. Choose tester, observer, recorder or data-analysis route.
20 min: release the same block onto each of three level samples. Aim for two trials each; measure from the same ramp-foot mark in centimetres. Record a non-slide honestly.
2 min: compare the repeats and check one control or measurement concern.
6 min: retry a doubtful run if possible; keep both original and retry. Conclude only what the evidence supports and clear equipment.

Key words

friction — A contact force that resists relative sliding or its tendency.

surface pair — The two surfaces in contact.

control — A relevant factor kept the same in a comparison.

trial — One run of a test.

evidence — Observations or data used to support an explanation.

Remember

  • Friction acts along a contact and can resist sliding even on smooth surfaces.
  • Grip at a brake can help stop a wheel; rubbing in moving parts can cause unwanted heating and wear.
  • Compare the same block, release, ramp and measuring zero when changing a surface.
  • The supplied numbers are invented teaching data, not measurements made by a pupil.

Success looks like

  • I can explain when friction helps or hinders an intended job.
  • I can compare repeated results and qualify my conclusion.

Does one stopping distance measure friction force directly?

No. Distance in cm is indirect evidence under controlled conditions.

Staff · feedback and access

Audience and response

The lesson teacher is the named adult audience. Hear or read the pupil’s own science response. A click, downloaded file or completed box is not evidence that a request was heard.

Respond within the existing task

Offer reading help, fewer cards at once, clearer labels, an adult-directed model or additional thinking time. Say what can and cannot change, then check whether the adjustment helped.

No additional pupil artefact

A response can be spoken, pointed, signed, drawn or written. Do not require a separate Lundy record, signature or written conversion of an oral response.

An optional pupil-owned note such as “What I said, and what it changed” may be used in existing work. It is not a requirement. If scribing, keep the pupil’s own words; the adult is an audience, not a verifier.

Keep assessment honest

Use current school recording practice and unit criteria separately. This pack does not certify an award. Do not infer a policy code from automated checking or a download.
