Muscles Work in Pairs

Learning objective

I can explain how a pair of muscles pulls bones to bend and straighten an arm.

Success looks like

  • Identify biceps and triceps on the model.
  • Show which muscle contracts for each movement.
  • Use a model to explain why two pulling actions are needed.

Meet the arm pair, control the model and explain the swap. Start from what you notice today; no earlier work is needed.

Two separate lessons

A · Explore and explain · 40 minutes

B · Model Arm Evidence Lab · 40 minutes

Stop after Lesson A. Lesson B has its own start and exit.

Point, say, sign, move a card, draw or write. You can direct an adult. Each way can show your science.

Stretch · 4 questions

1. Work together

Why does a skeleton alone not explain how an arm moves?

Help beside the question
Consider what provides a force.

Answer guidance

Answer: Muscles provide forces that pull on the bones.

2. Joint or muscle?

What does the elbow joint allow that a solid straight rod would not?

Help beside the question
Compare a hinged model with a rigid stick.

Answer guidance

Answer: The parts can turn relative to each other, bending or straightening the model.

3. Test a string

Why can a loose string pull a card but not push it back?

Help beside the question
Imagine trying to push with slack string.

Answer guidance

Answer: A flexible string goes slack or bends when pushed; it transmits tension when pulled.

4. Make a prediction

A string bends the model arm. What extra arrangement could return it?

Help beside the question
Think about a pull acting on the other side of the joint.

Answer guidance

Answer: An opposing pull can return the arm. Test it rather than treating the prediction as proof.

00–08 seconds · Two actions at one joint
Bones meet at the elbow joint. Muscles pull on bones to help move them.

08–16 seconds · Bend: biceps pulls
In our bending model, biceps contracts and shortens as it pulls. Triceps relaxes and can lengthen.

16–24 seconds · Straighten: triceps pulls
To actively straighten the model, the roles swap. Triceps contracts; biceps relaxes.

24–32 seconds · A model has limits
Two opposed pulls make two movements. A string transfers a person’s pull; it is not living muscle.

Claim explanations · reveal after sorting

Muscles pull: In this elbow model, an active muscle pulls on a bone.

Muscles push bones: Muscles generate a pull, not a pushing action on a bone.

Biceps contracts to bend: Biceps has the bending role in our model.

Triceps contracts to straighten: The triceps pull straightens the model.

The bone moves itself: The bone is moved by forces from muscles.

The relaxed partner pushes: Relaxing is not pushing. The opposing muscle supplies the active pull.

The elbow is a joint: The joint is where the bones meet and change angle.

The string is living muscle: The person supplies force through the string; it is only a model.

TA Brief

Current stage

Current stage:

Preparation and access

Use the diagram or a card model, not a pupil’s body. An adult prepares holes and secures split pins. Pull loose strings gently; do not use stretched elastic. No one needs to move, touch or show their own arm.

Scientific care

This is a simplified elbow model. Biceps helps bend the elbow; triceps helps straighten it. In these movements the active muscle shortens as it pulls, while its partner relaxes and can lengthen. Real contractions do not always shorten a muscle, and several muscles can work together or stabilise a joint. Do not teach that every muscle acts in just one isolated pair, or that a relaxed muscle pushes.

Responsive feedback within teaching

One optional help prompt sits inside each lesson. The lesson teacher hears the request, explains what can change, tries it and checks the effect. Passing or asking privately is valid. No compulsory participation task or new pupil form.

Sources, scope and safety

Teacher only · Local classroom review edition

Scientific guardrails

This is a simplified elbow model. Biceps helps bend the elbow; triceps helps straighten it. In these movements the active muscle shortens as it pulls, while its partner relaxes and can lengthen. Real contractions do not always shorten a muscle, and several muscles can work together or stabilise a joint. Do not teach that every muscle acts in just one isolated pair, or that a relaxed muscle pushes.

Access and safety

Use the diagram or a card model, not a pupil’s body. An adult prepares holes and secures split pins. Pull loose strings gently; do not use stretched elastic. No one needs to move, touch or show their own arm.

Independent work and feedback

Teacher only · A response is evidence, not a required format.

Lesson A · Supported

Point to biceps at the front of the upper-arm model and triceps at the back. Demonstrate or direct a bend. Complete “biceps contracts”.

Lesson A · Standard

Locate both muscles and the elbow pivot. Bending: biceps contracts, triceps relaxes. Straightening: triceps contracts, biceps relaxes. The opposing pull returns the model.

Lesson A · Stretch

The relaxed muscle does not push; the opposing active muscle pulls. The strings model applied tension and movement, not living contraction. An accurate limitation matters more than technical vocabulary.

Lesson B · Evidence to notice

Two tested actions, roles that swap and one corrected label or reason. For an observation route, accept direction to an adult or a pointed choice on the model. Do not require fine-motor construction.

Read the cause, then choose support

Vocabulary barrier: read a word and point. Unclear evidence: isolate one clue. Sequence confusion: slow the model or change. Motor/access barrier: accept adult direction. Ready to deepen: ask about a model limitation.

Worked feedback

“You named the pair. Now show the contracting muscle for a straighten.” The learner may answer through speech, sign, pointing or writing. Use the response to choose what happens next.
