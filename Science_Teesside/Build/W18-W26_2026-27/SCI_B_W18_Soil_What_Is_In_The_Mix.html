Soil: what is in the mix?

Learning intention

Use evidence to show that soil contains rock material and organic matter.

You are the evidence team.

What is hiding in a handful of soil?

Success looks like

  • Identify one piece of mineral material and one piece of organic material.
  • Support a choice with something observed or stated on an evidence card.
  • Explain that soil is a mixture; air and water can occupy spaces between solids.

Slide 1

TA Brief

Current stage

Objective

Use evidence to show that soil contains rock material and organic matter.

Preparation

  • Choose one pupil response route; all routes assess the same core science.
  • Print the six evidence cards and keep teacher answers separate.
  • For hands-on observation, screen samples for sharp objects, animal waste and heavy dust; use a known clean teaching source.
  • A second adult may pre-label two observable pieces. Do not depend on finding recognisable remains in every soil.

Method precautions

  • No tasting, smelling closely or blowing soil; keep hands away from faces and wash hands afterwards.
  • Keep samples in trays and use tools; offer sealed-tray or paper observation to pupils avoiding touch.
  • Use plastic containers; an adult pours demonstration water slowly in a spill tray and wipes spills promptly.
  • Do not add living animals to the activity or collect unknown soil from contaminated sites.

Practical alternative

Use the six printed evidence cards. Their described observations provide all information needed for every task. A bubble model substitutes for the optional demonstration; pupils label it as model evidence.

Access and regulation

Supported: Read two cards aloud at a time. Pupil points, matches or dictates one evidence reason. Use the supported response page, with mineral/organic word bank.

Standard: Classify evidence independently and write a two-part explanation that includes mineral and organic components.

Stretch: Evaluate uncertain evidence and improve a rock-only model; explain one limit of the model.

Regulation: Invite seated observation or paper evidence. Give predictable roles: observer, card mover, reporter. Reporting may be private; no timed competition or compulsory touching.

Teacher answers

Retrieval 1–3

1 Yes, a fallen leaf came from a living plant. 2 Accept hardness, colour, texture or another suitable property; not “all rocks are heavy/hard”. 3 A lens magnifies small visible details.

We do W1 / cards A–C

A mineral: quartz is identified as a mineral from rocks. B organic: recognised fallen-leaf tissue/vein. C organic: plant-stem fibres. Colour is not the deciding clue.

We do W2 / cards D–F

D remains uncertain; dark colour alone is insufficient. E supports air/gas occupying pores before water displaced it. F supports water in that damp sample. Neither E nor F tells us the percentages of each component.

Hinge

Second option is correct. Rock-only misses organic matter and pore contents. Dark colour does not show every grain is a dead plant. Reteach with the two-source diagram and cards A/B if needed.

S1–S5

S1 mineral material. S2 organic matter. S3 it came from a plant stem / has recognisable plant fibres. S4 mineral material and organic matter. S5 a space between particles containing air or water; accept a labelled sketch.

T1

A mineral—quartz origin. B organic—leaf vein/plant origin. C organic—stem/fibres. D uncertain—only colour known. E air in spaces—bubbles escape as water enters. F water—damp sample leaves water mark.

T2–T3

T2 soil also contains organic matter from living things and pores that can contain air/water. Core credit requires organic matter alongside mineral material. T3 colour is not sufficient; we need information about its material/origin or further examination. Do not demand an unavailable laboratory test name.

X1–X4

X1 include mineral grains and organic fragments, with labelled gaps. X2 air/water goes in gaps, not inside every solid particle. X3 dark minerals and organic pieces can both be dark. X4 model helps explain a mixture or pore spaces; it does not show actual sizes, proportions, organisms, chemistry or long formation processes. Accept any accurate limitation.

Exit E1–E3

E1 quartz grain, rock fragment or mineral particle. E2 leaf/stem remains or another valid example. E3 air or water. Do not demand both if the prompt is singular.

R1–R2

Reflective answers vary. Accept an evidence-related revision, e.g. “I thought soil was only rock; leaf fibres changed my mind.” R2 should be a relevant question or observation; no fixed right preference.

Optional observation

Actual results vary. Record visible observations without inventing identifiable organic fragments. “Cannot tell” is scientifically valid for an unidentifiable particle; pair it with card evidence so the core objective can still be assessed.
