Sugar Evidence: Read the Label

Learning objective

I can compare sugar values using the same label basis and order four supplied food labels from least to most sugar.

Success looks like

  • I can find the sugar number, unit and label basis.
  • I can compare values only when the unit and basis match.
  • I can order four labels and explain that sugar is one part of nutrition evidence.

We will observe, work together, then use the evidence ourselves.

Arrival task

BUILD · SCIENCE · Week 8 · Sugar evidence

Arrival task

Choose a route. Think first, then check the answers together.

New to this, returning or revisiting? Use the clues beside each question. Point, say, sign or ask an adult to read the words. The label question is a first idea.

1. Food chain

Plant → caterpillar → bird. Which is the plant that starts this chain?

A producer makes its own food. Here, it is the plant.

Answer: The plant is the producer. The arrows show food and energy passing to the next living thing.

2. Body jobs

Food helps us have energy and grow. Choose: energy only / growth only / both.

There are two jobs in the question.

Answer: Both. Nutrients support energy needs, growth and repair.

3. A varied pattern

A balanced eating pattern includes a range of foods. Choose: one food always / a range of foods.

Balanced means varied over time.

Answer: A range of foods. One food does not have to supply everything.

4. Label clue

Teaching label: 'of which sugars: 4 g per 100 g'. Point to the sugar number.

Find the number just before the first g.

Answer: 4. The complete sugar value is 4 g per 100 g.

1. Read the arrow

Plant → caterpillar → bird. What does the arrow from plant to caterpillar show?

Think about food and energy transfer.

Answer: The caterpillar gets food and energy by eating the plant. The arrow points in the direction of transfer.

2. Recall body jobs

Name two jobs that nutrients from food support.

Think about energy, growth or repair.

Answer: Any two: supplying energy, growth, repairing tissues. Accept other correct nutrient functions.

3. Explain variety

Why does a balanced eating pattern include a range of foods?

Different foods supply different nutrients.

Answer: Different foods provide different nutrients and amounts. A varied pattern helps meet different needs; no single food must do every job.

4. Read all three

A label says 'of which sugars: 4 g per 100 g'. Give the number, unit and basis.

Number = how much; unit = what measure; basis = in how much food.

Answer: Number: 4. Unit: g (grams). Basis: per 100 g of food.

1. Use food-chain evidence

Plant → caterpillar → bird. Explain why the arrows are not pointing towards what each animal hunts or eats.

Follow the transfer of food and energy.

Answer: The arrows show food and energy moving from the organism eaten to the consumer, not the direction the consumer travels to find food.

2. Separate the claims

'Food supplies energy' and 'This person eats badly'. Which is a statement about a body job?

Keep the science about nutrients and evidence.

Answer: 'Food supplies energy' describes a body job. The second judges a person and is not supported by that nutrient fact.

3. Limit a judgement

Can one food's sugar value describe a person's whole eating pattern? Explain.

Consider the other foods and information missing.

Answer: No. One sugar value does not show all foods, nutrients, amounts or the pattern over time. Keep conclusions within the evidence.

4. Spot the mismatch

Can 8 g sugar per serving be compared directly with 8 g per 100 g? What is missing?

A serving might not be 100 g.

Answer: No, unless the serving is known to be 100 g. We need its mass and must put both values on the same basis.

▶ Watch: how to read and compare sugar labels · 42 seconds

Silent, with on-screen captions. Pause or replay any part.

Read the transcript
  1. Find “of which sugars”, rather than total carbohydrate.
  2. Read the number, unit and basis: 4 g sugars per 100 g of food.
  3. A has 4 g and B has 9 g sugars per 100 g. A has less sugar when equal food amounts are compared.
  4. E has 3 g sugars per 25 g serving. Its food amount differs.
  5. Four 25 g servings make 100 g. Four lots of 3 g give 12 g sugars per 100 g.
  6. The figures compare sugar only. They cannot identify the best whole food for everyone.

Working space

Order A–D from least to most sugar per 100 g. Annotate one number, unit and basis.

Supported

1. Use these fictional teaching cards. All give sugar in g per 100 g: A — 4 g; B — 9 g; C — 14 g; D — 23 g.

Help with this question

Use a paper strip to track one card at a time. Say or point to the letter, the sugar number and ‘g per 100 g’ together.

2. Put the four cards in order from LEAST sugar per 100 g to MOST. Point, move paper cards or direct an adult.

Help with this question

Compare two numbers first. Place the smaller one towards LEAST, then find where each remaining card belongs.

3. Complete: Label ___ has the least sugar per 100 g. Label ___ has the most.

Help with this question

Look at the two ends of your own order. Use those card letters to fill the gaps.

4. Choose the claim we can support: 'A has less sugar per 100 g than D' / 'A is the healthiest food for everyone'.

Help with this question

Ask: does this sentence describe the sugar evidence, or claim to know everything about the food?

5. Finish or point: These sugar numbers show ONE PART OF NUTRITION / THE WHOLE EATING PATTERN. Keep “g per 100 g” with each number.

Help with this question

Look at what was measured. Think about whether these cards show every nutrient and everything a person eats.

Standard

1. Read these fictional teaching labels. Each is PER 100 g. A: carbohydrate 22 g; of which sugars 4 g. B: carbohydrate 67 g; of which sugars 9 g. C: carbohydrate 58 g; of which sugars 14 g. D: carbohydrate 72 g; of which sugars 23 g.

Help with this question

Track the ‘of which sugars’ row. Keep the carbohydrate and sugar rows separate.

2. Make a results row for each label. Copy the SUGAR value with its unit and basis; do not copy total carbohydrate.

Help with this question

Use the headings Label | Sugar / g per 100 g. Check each copied value against its nutrient name.

3. Order A–D from least to most sugar per 100 g.

Help with this question

Check adjacent pairs: is each value no larger than the next? Recheck that all four use the same basis.

4. Write or say one comparison: 'Label ___ has ___ g sugar per 100 g. This is less/more than ___.'

Help with this question

Build the comparison from two complete readings. Include which label you are comparing it with.

5. Explain one thing these sugar numbers cannot tell us about the whole food or eating pattern.

Help with this question

Choose one missing piece of information. Explain why that missing information limits a wider judgement.

Stretch

1. Fictional labels, all PER 100 g: A — carbohydrate 22 g, sugars 4 g; B — carbohydrate 67 g, sugars 9 g; C — carbohydrate 58 g, sugars 14 g; D — carbohydrate 72 g, sugars 23 g.

Help with this question

Identify the nutrient and reference amount before selecting numerical evidence.

2. Extract the four sugar values and order them. Keep the unit and basis.

Help with this question

Audit the completed order against the source labels; a correct number copied from the wrong row is still the wrong evidence.

3. Extra label E says 'sugars 3 g per 25 g serving'. Someone puts E before A because 3 is less than 4. Audit this decision.

Help with this question

Compare the reference quantities before comparing the two bare numbers.

4. Put E on a per-100 g basis before comparing. How many 25 g servings make 100 g? Then place E among A–D.

Help with this question

Find the scale factor from 25 g of food to 100 g of food. Apply the same factor to the sugar amount.

5. Write one supported comparison from A–D and repair this claim: 'The lowest-sugar card proves the best food for everybody.'

Help with this question

Separate a justified numerical comparison from a wider judgement. State exactly what the evidence establishes and what it leaves unknown.

Responsive feedback · policy and practical use

Policy source: Feedback & Marking Policy 2025/2026 (Pilot), Issue 1, May 2026; the 16-page EFL version. The classroom examples below are practical choices, not extra policy rules.

Use feedback within the science

Make time and space to respond, including co-regulation, a familiar adult and permission to refuse. Give useful feedback. Let the pupil respond in an accessible way. Genuinely read, hear or watch that response, and use it to adapt the next explanation, question, scaffold or task. Check the re-attempt when useful. A chosen button or completed ticket alone does not demonstrate Audience or Influence.

The small prompt in the sorting activity is optional. It is not a scheduled stage, a required form or a condition for moving on. Normal pupil work and re-attempts can provide the response; you can give further feedback wherever it is needed. The policy does not impose a one-next-step-per-day limit.

There is no additional pupil loop form or signature in this lesson. That is a design choice to keep this lesson manageable, not a ban on school evidence routines. Extended writing and assessment may need one or two precise next steps and appropriate response time; do not turn this short exit task into an extended writing exercise.

Answers

Shared activities

A 4 < B 9 < C 14 < D 23 g per 100 g. The carbohydrate row is not the sugar row. E cannot be ordered against the raw A–D numbers until converted: 3 × 4 = 12 g per 100 g, between B and C. Core success does not require that conversion. Sugar alone cannot decide whole-food health.

Supported task

  • Order: A — 4 g per 100 g; B — 9 g per 100 g; C — 14 g per 100 g; D — 23 g per 100 g.
  • Least: A. Most: D.
  • Supported claim: A has less sugar per 100 g than D. The sugar data alone do not establish a healthiest food for everyone.
  • ONE PART OF NUTRITION. These numbers describe sugar, not the whole food or eating pattern. Accept pointing, signing, speech or exact scribing without changing the pupil’s scientific meaning.

Standard task

  • Sugar rows: A 4 g per 100 g; B 9 g per 100 g; C 14 g per 100 g; D 23 g per 100 g. Values 22, 67, 58 and 72 are total carbohydrate, not the requested sugar values.
  • Order A, B, C, D: 4 < 9 < 14 < 23, all in g per 100 g.
  • Example: 'B has 9 g sugar per 100 g. This is less than C, which has 14 g per 100 g.' Accept any correct comparison that keeps the same basis.
  • Valid limits include: other nutrient amounts are not all supplied; actual portion eaten is unknown; the person's needs and wider eating pattern are unknown. Sugar alone cannot identify a universally healthiest food.

Stretch task

  • A 4 < B 9 < C 14 < D 23, all g sugar per 100 g. Total carbohydrate is not the requested nutrient value.
  • E cannot be placed by comparing 3 with 4 directly: its basis is per 25 g serving, while A–D are per 100 g.
  • Four 25 g servings make 100 g. E: 3 × 4 = 12 g sugar per 100 g. The full order is A 4, B 9, E 12, C 14, D 23, all g per 100 g. Accept supported doubling 3 → 6 → 12 with the serving masses 25 → 50 → 100.
  • Example supported comparison: 'A has 19 g less sugar per 100 g than D.' Simpler correct less/more comparisons are also sufficient.
  • Repair: the lowest value identifies the least sugar per 100 g among these labels. It does not prove the best food for everyone; a broader judgement needs more nutrition and context information. No judgement about pupils' own food choices is required.

Sugar Evidence: Read the Label

Shared activities

A 4 < B 9 < C 14 < D 23 g per 100 g. The carbohydrate row is not the sugar row. E cannot be ordered against the raw A–D numbers until converted: 3 × 4 = 12 g per 100 g, between B and C. Core success does not require that conversion. Sugar alone cannot decide whole-food health.

Supported task

  • Order: A — 4 g per 100 g; B — 9 g per 100 g; C — 14 g per 100 g; D — 23 g per 100 g.
  • Least: A. Most: D.
  • Supported claim: A has less sugar per 100 g than D. The sugar data alone do not establish a healthiest food for everyone.
  • ONE PART OF NUTRITION. These numbers describe sugar, not the whole food or eating pattern. Accept pointing, signing, speech or exact scribing without changing the pupil’s scientific meaning.

Standard task

  • Sugar rows: A 4 g per 100 g; B 9 g per 100 g; C 14 g per 100 g; D 23 g per 100 g. Values 22, 67, 58 and 72 are total carbohydrate, not the requested sugar values.
  • Order A, B, C, D: 4 < 9 < 14 < 23, all in g per 100 g.
  • Example: 'B has 9 g sugar per 100 g. This is less than C, which has 14 g per 100 g.' Accept any correct comparison that keeps the same basis.
  • Valid limits include: other nutrient amounts are not all supplied; actual portion eaten is unknown; the person's needs and wider eating pattern are unknown. Sugar alone cannot identify a universally healthiest food.

Stretch task

  • A 4 < B 9 < C 14 < D 23, all g sugar per 100 g. Total carbohydrate is not the requested nutrient value.
  • E cannot be placed by comparing 3 with 4 directly: its basis is per 25 g serving, while A–D are per 100 g.
  • Four 25 g servings make 100 g. E: 3 × 4 = 12 g sugar per 100 g. The full order is A 4, B 9, E 12, C 14, D 23, all g per 100 g. Accept supported doubling 3 → 6 → 12 with the serving masses 25 → 50 → 100.
  • Example supported comparison: 'A has 19 g less sugar per 100 g than D.' Simpler correct less/more comparisons are also sufficient.
  • Repair: the lowest value identifies the least sugar per 100 g among these labels. It does not prove the best food for everyone; a broader judgement needs more nutrition and context information. No judgement about pupils' own food choices is required.

Exit ticket · answer guidance

Supported

  1. B: 9 g sugars per 100 g, less than C at 14 g. Pointing to the complete B value is enough.
  2. The amount of sugar. These figures do not describe everything about the food.

Standard

  1. 9 g; less. The basis is per 100 g for both labels.
  2. Sugar. Accept an equivalent spoken completion.

Stretch

  1. 12 g sugars per 100 g; more sugar than B at 9 g. Four 25 g servings make 100 g, so 3 × 4 = 12.
  2. Other nutrient amounts. Also accept a relevant own example, such as actual portion, other nutrients, individual needs or the wider eating pattern. The label font is not nutrition evidence.
