BUILD · SCIENCE · WEEK 5 · LESSON A · 2 MINUTES

What a Body Needs

Learning objective

I can use food evidence to explain how different nutrients help a body work.

Success looks like

  • Name energy, growth and repair as body needs.
  • Link a food to a nutrient and its job.
  • Explain why variety matters and why one food can have several jobs.

Investigate the jobs of food and water. Use made-up cases and supplied cards; no one needs to describe what they eat.

Two separate lessons

A · Explore and explain · 40 minutes

B · Food-Jobs Evidence Mission · 40 minutes

Stop after Lesson A. Lesson B has its own start and exit.

Point, say, sign, move a card, draw or write. You can direct an adult. Each way can show your science.

Watch the model, then explain

Think aloud: food → nutrient → job

“Beans contain protein, which supports growth and repair. They also contain carbohydrate and fibre. I should not force beans into only one job.”

Start with energy. The other body jobs matter too.

Water is essential

It helps transport substances and control temperature. Plain water supplies no food energy.

The wider picture

Some fat is essential and provides energy. Vitamins and minerals have many body roles. Fibre helps digestion.

These are selected jobs, not complete nutrient lists or a personal food plan.

Supported · three ways to show the idea

1. Choose

Choose bread or potato to show one energy source.

Look for starch / carbohydrate on the cards.

2. Match

Choose beans or lentils and point to the growth-and-repair job.

Protein is the nutrient clue.

3. Explain

Complete: “Different foods can give a body different ___.”

Choose nutrients / names. Speak, point or use a word card.

Standard · three ways to show the idea

1. Link

Choose two foods. Link each food to one nutrient and a job.

Food → nutrient → body job.

2. Add

The model starts with bread and water. Suggest one food to widen the nutrient mix.

Beans or lentils add protein; fruit or vegetables add other nutrients and fibre.

3. Explain

Why is a nutrient reason stronger than “I like it” for this task?

We are checking food jobs, not preferences.

Stretch · three ways to show the idea

1. Connect

Show two different body jobs supported by beans or lentils.

Name the relevant nutrient for each job.

2. Compare

Compare two additions to the bread-and-water model.

More than one supported choice may work.

3. Evaluate

Explain why adding more bread is not the same as adding variety.

More of one food does not create a wider range of food types.

Worked explanation · reveal after thinking

Bread or potato supplies starch for energy. Beans or lentils supplies protein for growth and repair. Adding beans plus a carrot gives more types of food, but does not prove a complete diet.

TA Brief

Current stage

Current stage:

Preparation and access

Use paper cards only. No tasting, feeding animals or personal diet records. Do not discuss pupils’ weight, calories or body size. These fictional cases explore food jobs; they are not complete meals or advice for a real person.

Scientific care

Foods contain mixtures of nutrients, so one food can support several jobs. Carbohydrate and fat provide energy; protein supports growth and repair and can also provide energy. Vitamins and minerals support many body processes, and fibre supports digestion. Water is essential but provides no food energy. The six-food comparison is deliberately limited, not a complete balanced diet. Amounts and needs vary; do not prescribe portions or make claims about an individual pupil’s needs.

Responsive feedback within teaching

One optional help prompt sits inside each lesson. The lesson teacher hears the request, explains what can change, tries it and checks the effect. Passing or asking privately is valid. No compulsory participation task or new pupil form.

Policy language: VF · WS · I · NS+ · // · ? · R · E. R needs Voice + Audience + a meaningful next step + pupil response. Do not infer it from a click, download or written form. Use current school recording practice and existing science evidence.

Sources, scope and safety

Teacher only · Local classroom review edition

Scientific guardrails

Foods contain mixtures of nutrients, so one food can support several jobs. Carbohydrate and fat provide energy; protein supports growth and repair and can also provide energy. Vitamins and minerals support many body processes, and fibre supports digestion. Water is essential but provides no food energy. The six-food comparison is deliberately limited, not a complete balanced diet. Amounts and needs vary; do not prescribe portions or make claims about an individual pupil’s needs.

Access and safety

Use paper cards only. No tasting, feeding animals or personal diet records. Do not discuss pupils’ weight, calories or body size. These fictional cases explore food jobs; they are not complete meals or advice for a real person.

BUILD Science · Sugar Evidence: Read the Label · Supported

Your task

1. Use these fictional teaching cards. All give sugar in g per 100 g: A — 4 g; B — 9 g; C — 14 g; D — 23 g.

Help with this question

Use a paper strip to track one card at a time. Say or point to the letter, the sugar number and ‘g per 100 g’ together.

2. Put the four cards in order from LEAST sugar per 100 g to MOST. Point, move paper cards or direct an adult.

Help with this question

Compare two numbers first. Place the smaller one towards LEAST, then find where each remaining card belongs.

3. Complete: Label ___ has the least sugar per 100 g. Label ___ has the most.

Help with this question

Look at the two ends of your own order. Use those card letters to fill the gaps.

4. Choose the claim we can support: 'A has less sugar per 100 g than D' / 'A is the healthiest food for everyone'.

Help with this question

Ask: does this sentence describe the sugar evidence, or claim to know everything about the food?

5. Finish or point: These sugar numbers show ONE PART OF NUTRITION / THE WHOLE EATING PATTERN. Keep “g per 100 g” with each number.

Help with this question

Look at what was measured. Think about whether these cards show every nutrient and everything a person eats.

BUILD Science · Sugar Evidence: Read the Label · Standard

Your task

1. Read these fictional teaching labels. Each is PER 100 g. A: carbohydrate 22 g; of which sugars 4 g. B: carbohydrate 67 g; of which sugars 9 g. C: carbohydrate 58 g; of which sugars 14 g. D: carbohydrate 72 g; of which sugars 23 g.

Help with this question

Track the ‘of which sugars’ row. Keep the carbohydrate and sugar rows separate.

2. Make a results row for each label. Copy the SUGAR value with its unit and basis; do not copy total carbohydrate.

Help with this question

Use the headings Label | Sugar / g per 100 g. Check each copied value against its nutrient name.

3. Order A–D from least to most sugar per 100 g.

Help with this question

Check adjacent pairs: is each value no larger than the next? Recheck that all four use the same basis.

4. Write or say one comparison: 'Label ___ has ___ g sugar per 100 g. This is less/more than ___.'

Help with this question

Build the comparison from two complete readings. Include which label you are comparing it with.

5. Explain one thing these sugar numbers cannot tell us about the whole food or eating pattern.

Help with this question

Choose one missing piece of information. Explain why that missing information limits a wider judgement.

BUILD Science · Sugar Evidence: Read the Label · Stretch

Your task

1. Fictional labels, all PER 100 g: A — carbohydrate 22 g, sugars 4 g; B — carbohydrate 67 g, sugars 9 g; C — carbohydrate 58 g, sugars 14 g; D — carbohydrate 72 g, sugars 23 g.

Help with this question

Identify the nutrient and reference amount before selecting numerical evidence.

2. Extract the four sugar values and order them. Keep the unit and basis.

Help with this question

Audit the completed order against the source labels; a correct number copied from the wrong row is still the wrong evidence.

3. Extra label E says 'sugars 3 g per 25 g serving'. Someone puts E before A because 3 is less than 4. Audit this decision.

Help with this question

Compare the reference quantities before comparing the two bare numbers.

4. Put E on a per-100 g basis before comparing. How many 25 g servings make 100 g? Then place E among A–D.

Help with this question

Find the scale factor from 25 g of food to 100 g of food. Apply the same factor to the sugar amount.

5. Write one supported comparison from A–D and repair this claim: 'The lowest-sugar card proves the best food for everybody.'

Help with this question

Separate a justified numerical comparison from a wider judgement. State exactly what the evidence establishes and what it leaves unknown.

Independent work and feedback

Teacher only · A response is evidence, not a required format.

Lesson A · Supported

Bread or potato provides starchy carbohydrate for energy. Beans or lentils contain protein for growth and repair. Different foods provide different nutrient mixtures.

Lesson A · Standard

Accept any correct food → nutrient → job chain. Beans add protein; carrot or apple adds other nutrients and fibre. A preference alone does not identify a nutrient job.

Lesson A · Stretch

Beans or lentils: protein → growth and repair; carbohydrate → energy; fibre → digestion. Several additions can be justified. Extra bread changes amount, not the range of food types.

Lesson B · Evidence to notice

Case 1 retains bread and water and widens the food-card evidence. Case 2 retains rice and vegetables and swaps the protein source. A beans-to-lentils swap preserves the model’s protein role. The checking tool does not assess portions or dietary completeness.

Read the cause, then choose support

Vocabulary barrier: read a word and point. Unclear evidence: isolate one clue. Sequence confusion: slow the model or change. Motor/access barrier: accept adult direction. Ready to deepen: ask about a model limitation.

Worked feedback

“You chose beans. Now point to the nutrient that explains repair.” The learner may answer through speech, sign, pointing or writing. Use the response to choose what happens next.
