BUILD · SCIENCE · WEEK 3 · LESSON A · 2 MINUTES

Backbones and No Backbones

Learning objective

I can use body evidence to sort animals with and without a backbone.

Success looks like

  • Find the backbone on a body diagram.
  • Sort animals using the backbone rule.
  • Explain one way an animal without a backbone is supported.

We will look inside a body model, sort animals together, then use the evidence ourselves.

One useful question

Does it have a backbone?

Size, colour and number of legs do not decide.

New to this, returning or revisiting? We start with what you notice today. You do not need earlier work.

Two separate 40-minute lessons: A · Find and sort. B · Evidence lab. Stop after A or start B when it is time.

Arrival

BUILD · SCIENCE · WEEK 3 · LESSON A · 5 MINUTES

Four clues to start

Choose a route. Think first, then check together. Point, say, sign, move a card, draw or write. An adult can read or scribe. Each way can show your science.

Supported · 4 questions

1. Living things

Which is an animal: mouse or oak tree?

Help beside the question
An animal is a living thing, but not a plant.

Answer guidance

Answer: Mouse. An oak tree is a plant.

2. Body support

A skeleton helps support a body. Choose: support / change its colour.

Help beside the question
Support means helping something keep its shape.

Answer guidance

Answer: Support. A skeleton also helps protect parts of the body and allows movement.

3. Body clue

A body diagram shows a line of joined bones along the back. Point to the backbone.

Help beside the question
Use the supplied backbone picture. Follow the joined parts.

Answer guidance

Answer: The joined line along the back is the backbone. Accept pointing or tracing.

4. First idea

A fish has a backbone. Choose: vertebrate / invertebrate.

Help beside the question
Vertebrate means an animal with a backbone.

Answer guidance

Answer: Vertebrate. The backbone is the relevant evidence.

Standard · 4 questions

1. Animal or plant?

Name the animal in this pair: mouse / oak tree.

Help beside the question
Both are living things; only one is an animal.

Answer guidance

Answer: Mouse.

2. Support

Give one job of a skeleton.

Help beside the question
Think about support, protection or movement.

Answer guidance

Answer: Support, protection or helping movement. Accept one correct job.

3. Find the evidence

On the supplied diagram, locate the backbone. What do you notice about its parts?

Help beside the question
Look along the back, not at the ribs.

Answer guidance

Answer: It runs along the back and has many joined parts, not one long bone.

4. First idea

What makes an animal a vertebrate?

Help beside the question
Use the body feature, not its size.

Answer guidance

Answer: It has a backbone.

Stretch · 4 questions

1. Choose a useful group

Why can mouse and oak tree be called living things, but not both be called animals?

Help beside the question
A useful group uses a shared feature.

Answer guidance

Answer: Both are living things, but the oak tree is a plant and the mouse is an animal.

2. Body support

Give two jobs that an internal skeleton can do.

Help beside the question
Support, protection and movement are different jobs.

Answer guidance

Answer: Any two: support the body, protect organs, help movement.

3. Use a diagram

What does the backbone diagram show that an outside photograph might not?

Help beside the question
Separate what is inside from what you can see outside.

Answer guidance

Answer: The internal supporting parts, including the joined backbone. An outside view may not reveal them.

4. Test the claim

“All small animals have no backbone.” Is size a reliable test?

Help beside the question
A mouse is small and has a backbone.

Answer guidance

Answer: No. A mouse is a counterexample. Use backbone evidence, not size.

Supported · 4 questions

1. The rule

An animal with a backbone is a vertebrate / invertebrate.

Help beside the question
Vertebrate = has a backbone.

Answer guidance

Answer: Vertebrate.

2. Read a fact

Body fact: a goldfish has a backbone. Which group?

Help beside the question
Use the body fact, not where it lives.

Answer guidance

Answer: Vertebrate.

3. Another support

A beetle has a hard outer case. Is this a backbone: yes / no?

Help beside the question
A backbone is inside, along the back.

Answer guidance

Answer: No. A beetle has an exoskeleton and no backbone.

4. Choose evidence

Which helps us check: a body diagram / the animal’s size?

Help beside the question
Look for information about its inside support.

Answer guidance

Answer: A clear body diagram. Size is not the test.

Standard · 4 questions

1. Recall or read

What feature separates vertebrates from invertebrates?

Help beside the question
The key word is backbone.

Answer guidance

Answer: Having a backbone, or not having one.

2. Read a fact

A bat has wings and a backbone. Which fact decides its group?

Help beside the question
Only one fact answers our classification question.

Answer guidance

Answer: Its backbone makes it a vertebrate.

3. Body support

Name one support an animal without a backbone can have.

Help beside the question
Think about a hard outside case, or fluid and muscles.

Answer guidance

Answer: A hard outer case (exoskeleton), or fluid working with muscles. A shell is also a valid protective/supporting structure.

4. Check a claim

“No legs means no backbone.” Give a reason to check that claim.

Help beside the question
A snake has no legs but has a backbone.

Answer guidance

Answer: A snake is a counterexample. Leg number does not decide the group.

Stretch · 4 questions

1. State the rule

Explain the difference between vertebrate and invertebrate without using size.

Help beside the question
Say whether a backbone is present.

Answer guidance

Answer: A vertebrate has a backbone; an invertebrate does not.

2. Useful evidence

A bat and a beetle both have wings. Does this put them in the same backbone group?

Help beside the question
Bat: backbone inside. Beetle: hard outer case, no backbone.

Answer guidance

Answer: No. Wings are not the rule. The bat is a vertebrate; the beetle is an invertebrate.

3. Avoid a shortcut

Why does “no backbone” not mean “no support”?

Help beside the question
Use an example of a different support system.

Answer guidance

Answer: Support can come from a hard outer case, or from fluid working with muscles.

4. Know the limit

An outside picture does not show a backbone. Is that enough evidence that there is none?

Help beside the question
Not visible is different from not present.

Answer guidance

Answer: No. Check a reliable body diagram or body fact before deciding.

Knowledge organiser

Keep this beside your science work. It is not an answer sheet.

Learning objective

I can use body evidence to sort animals with and without a backbone.

Our two groups

Vertebrate

An animal with a backbone.

Invertebrate

An animal without a backbone.

Use evidence

Use a clear body diagram or a reliable body fact. An outside picture may not show what is inside.

Not a test: size, colour, number of legs or where an animal lives.

The backbone

The backbone has joined parts along the back. In bony animals these parts are small bones called vertebrae.

Some vertebrates, such as sharks, have a skeleton made of cartilage rather than bone.

No backbone does not mean no support

Exoskeleton: a hard outer case that supports and protects.

Fluid support: muscles work against fluid inside a soft body.

Success looks like

• Find the backbone on a body diagram.
• Sort animals using the backbone rule.
• Explain one way an animal without a backbone is supported.

Useful words: backbone · vertebrate · invertebrate · skeleton · support · evidence
Explain: “The animal belongs here because the body evidence shows ___.”

Word help

Backbone
Joined supporting parts along the back, inside an animal. The parts are called vertebrae.
Vertebrate
An animal with a backbone.
Invertebrate
An animal without a backbone.
Skeleton
A framework that supports a body, protects parts and helps movement. It can include bone or cartilage.
Exoskeleton
A hard outer case that supports and protects a body.
Fluid support
Support from fluid inside, working with muscles.
Evidence
Information that helps us decide or explain. Use a reliable body diagram or body fact.

Starting point and access

Use today’s responses, not assumptions about cover work. New: show and read the rule. Revisiting: ask for the body clue. Offer pointing, oral response, drawing, sorting or scribing. Do not make pupils touch a spine or handle an animal.

Responsive feedback, not a separate pupil task

One optional help prompt is placed inside Lesson A’s We Do and Lesson B’s evidence review. The lesson teacher hears the request, explains what can change, tries it and checks its effect. Passing or asking privately is valid. No participation form is required.

School policy language: VF · WS · I · NS+ · // · ? · R · E. A response is not inferred from a click, download or completed form. The R gate needs Voice, Audience, a meaningful next-step prompt and the pupil’s response. Use existing work and the school’s current recording practice.

Scientific guardrails

Do not use size, wings, legs, habitat or a hard outside as proof. An outside picture cannot establish that a backbone is absent. Some vertebrates have cartilage rather than bony skeletons; all pictures here are simplified teaching models.

Staff notes and sources

Scope, scientific limitations and provenance

Scientific care

The backbone rule is the age-appropriate organising idea. Avoid “all vertebrates have bones”: sharks have cartilaginous skeletons. Avoid “all invertebrates have shells”: support systems vary. A tortoise’s hard shell does not make it an invertebrate. Do not infer absence from an outside picture.

Assessment and evidence

This lesson supports observation of grouping and explanation. It is not, by itself, a claim of completed AQA UAS or Pearson accreditation. Apply the exact current unit criteria separately where relevant. Keep any photograph to the work, following school consent and data practice.

BUILD Science · Sugar Evidence: Read the Label · Supported

Your task

1. Use these fictional teaching cards. All give sugar in g per 100 g: A — 4 g; B — 9 g; C — 14 g; D — 23 g.

Help with this question

Use a paper strip to track one card at a time. Say or point to the letter, the sugar number and ‘g per 100 g’ together.

2. Put the four cards in order from LEAST sugar per 100 g to MOST. Point, move paper cards or direct an adult.

Help with this question

Compare two numbers first. Place the smaller one towards LEAST, then find where each remaining card belongs.

3. Complete: Label ___ has the least sugar per 100 g. Label ___ has the most.

Help with this question

Look at the two ends of your own order. Use those card letters to fill the gaps.

4. Choose the claim we can support: 'A has less sugar per 100 g than D' / 'A is the healthiest food for everyone'.

Help with this question

Ask: does this sentence describe the sugar evidence, or claim to know everything about the food?

5. Finish or point: These sugar numbers show ONE PART OF NUTRITION / THE WHOLE EATING PATTERN. Keep “g per 100 g” with each number.

Help with this question

Look at what was measured. Think about whether these cards show every nutrient and everything a person eats.

BUILD Science · Sugar Evidence: Read the Label · Standard

Your task

1. Read these fictional teaching labels. Each is PER 100 g. A: carbohydrate 22 g; of which sugars 4 g. B: carbohydrate 67 g; of which sugars 9 g. C: carbohydrate 58 g; of which sugars 14 g. D: carbohydrate 72 g; of which sugars 23 g.

Help with this question

Track the ‘of which sugars’ row. Keep the carbohydrate and sugar rows separate.

2. Make a results row for each label. Copy the SUGAR value with its unit and basis; do not copy total carbohydrate.

Help with this question

Use the headings Label | Sugar / g per 100 g. Check each copied value against its nutrient name.

3. Order A–D from least to most sugar per 100 g.

Help with this question

Check adjacent pairs: is each value no larger than the next? Recheck that all four use the same basis.

4. Write or say one comparison: 'Label ___ has ___ g sugar per 100 g. This is less/more than ___.'

Help with this question

Build the comparison from two complete readings. Include which label you are comparing it with.

5. Explain one thing these sugar numbers cannot tell us about the whole food or eating pattern.

Help with this question

Choose one missing piece of information. Explain why that missing information limits a wider judgement.

BUILD Science · Sugar Evidence: Read the Label · Stretch

Your task

1. Fictional labels, all PER 100 g: A — carbohydrate 22 g, sugars 4 g; B — carbohydrate 67 g, sugars 9 g; C — carbohydrate 58 g, sugars 14 g; D — carbohydrate 72 g, sugars 23 g.

Help with this question

Identify the nutrient and reference amount before selecting numerical evidence.

2. Extract the four sugar values and order them. Keep the unit and basis.

Help with this question

Audit the completed order against the source labels; a correct number copied from the wrong row is still the wrong evidence.

3. Extra label E says 'sugars 3 g per 25 g serving'. Someone puts E before A because 3 is less than 4. Audit this decision.

Help with this question

Compare the reference quantities before comparing the two bare numbers.

4. Put E on a per-100 g basis before comparing. How many 25 g servings make 100 g? Then place E among A–D.

Help with this question

Find the scale factor from 25 g of food to 100 g of food. Apply the same factor to the sugar amount.

5. Write one supported comparison from A–D and repair this claim: 'The lowest-sugar card proves the best food for everybody.'

Help with this question

Separate a justified numerical comparison from a wider judgement. State exactly what the evidence establishes and what it leaves unknown.

Science activity answers

Teacher only · Explanations are models, not compulsory scripts.

Lesson A · Eight-card sort

Vertebrates: fish, bird, frog, lizard, mouse.
Invertebrates: snail, crab, earthworm.

Worked explanations

“The fish is a vertebrate because its body evidence shows a backbone.”

“The crab is an invertebrate because it has no backbone. Its hard outer case supports and protects its body.”

Lesson A · Independent route guidance

Supported: accept locating the joined backbone, sorting one card from its fact and completing the explanation by pointing or speaking.

Standard: check that the label reaches the backbone, not a rib. Crab: hard outside case. Earthworm: fluid and muscles.

Stretch: mouse challenges the size claim. Crab and earthworm both lack a backbone, but have different support systems.

Review claim

“No legs means no backbone” is false. A snake has no legs but has a backbone. The earthworm has no legs and no backbone: leg number cannot separate the groups.

Mystery model

Animal: crab. Clue 1: I have no backbone. Clue 2: My body is supported and protected by a hard outer case. These identify the group/support, but may fit more than one animal; a further identifying clue can be added when needed.
