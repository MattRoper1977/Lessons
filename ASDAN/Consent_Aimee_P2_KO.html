Key vocabulary

WordWhat it means
BystanderSomeone who is present and sees what is happening.
Bystander effectThe more people present, the less likely any one person acts.
Active bystanderSomeone who safely steps in, distracts, or gets help.
The 5 D'sDirect, Distract, Delegate, Document, Delay — safe ways to help.
Diffusion of responsibilityEveryone assumes someone else will deal with it, so no one does.
Writer's purposeThe reason a writer chooses to include something in a story.
AssumptionA fixed idea about something that may turn out to be wrong.
ReportingTelling someone in authority what has happened.
EmpathyUnderstanding and sharing how someone else feels.
ConsentFreely, willingly agreeing — freely given, informed, enthusiastic.

The storyline in four steps

  1. On the bus, a man behaves sexually towards Aimee without her consent. Other passengers are nearby.
  2. No one steps in. Aimee freezes, then plays it down afterwards.
  3. She tells Maeve, who takes it seriously; her friends rally round and support her.
  4. Later the impact shows — Aimee is too frightened to get on a bus.

Revision prose

Key distinction: a passive bystander stays out of it; an active bystander does something safe. Helping never has to mean confronting anyone — the 5 D's are all safe options.

Memory hook: the five D's — Direct, Distract, Delegate, Document, Delay.

Common mark-losers: blaming the bystanders instead of explaining the bystander effect; saying only "the writer wanted to raise awareness" without saying of what or why.

Self-quiz: cover this and say — (1) what the bystander effect is, (2) two of the 5 D's, (3) one reason the writer told this story.

Key messages & support

Remember

  • Consent must be freely given, informed and enthusiastic.
  • Bystanders can help safely — doing something small beats doing nothing.
  • However someone reacts, it's never their fault.
