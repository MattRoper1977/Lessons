Week 1 · 40 minutes

Strengths with evidence

Open lesson slideshow

Objective

Identify strengths from evidence, recognise useful feedback and plan a small supported next step.

  • I can link a strength to something a person does.
  • I can separate useful feedback from a hurtful judgement.
  • I can choose a next step and the support it needs.

Preparation

  • Read the fictional evidence cards and prepare two-choice strength prompts.
  • Identify a private help route and offer a neutral alternative to self-description.
  • Decide one accessible change pupils can influence for the next lesson.

Materials

  • Pupil learning record
  • Printed strength and feedback cards
  • Pens or drawing tools; optional communication device

40-minute plan

TimeActivity
3 minWelcome, participation choices and private help route
5 minModel Jay’s strength and evidence
6 minCharacter strength sort
5 minUseful feedback and safe response modelling
12 minThree pupil tasks with chosen support
4 minDiscuss next steps using model answers
3 minAccess suggestion and exit check
2 minReset, collect work privately and transition

Vocabulary

  • strength: A skill or quality someone can use and develop.
  • evidence: An example that supports an idea.
  • feedback: Information that can help someone review their work.
  • next step: A small action to try next.

Model the task

Say: “I can see Jay changed the base. That is evidence. I will choose problem solving, rather than guessing that Jay is good at everything.” Then think aloud through one small next step with support.

Support

Read one character card at a time. Offer two strength words, then ask pupils to point to the evidence. Scribe exactly what the pupil communicates. A fictional profile is a complete response.

Stretch

Invite two valid interpretations of the same action and ask which is better supported. Evaluate whether the next step could be observed without judging confidence or personality.

Fallback

Work orally from the three fully written character examples. Adult scribes one evidence sentence and one next-step plan; no cards or personal photographs are required.

Safety and sensitive discussion

Avoid public strength rankings, compulsory partner compliments or retelling hurtful experiences. Keep any personal example private. For a disclosure, use the response protocol in Week 5 and the school safeguarding procedure.

What to check

Look for an action linked to a strength, a distinction between specific feedback and put-downs, and one achievable supported step. Record the communication support used without treating it as lower attainment.

Common misconceptions

  • A strength is not only a prize-winning talent.
  • Support does not cancel achievement.
  • Persistence can include stopping, resting and changing the approach.
  • A harmful comment is not automatically useful feedback.

Week 2 · 40 minutes

Feelings, clues and asking for support

Open lesson slideshow

Objective

Use feeling vocabulary, separate clues from certainty and choose a clear way to ask for support.

  • I can suggest more than one possible feeling from a situation.
  • I can explain that a clue does not prove a feeling.
  • I can use words or another communication route to ask for support.

Preparation

  • Prepare feeling cards; remove any expectation of a named mood display.
  • Plan feedback on the Week 1 access suggestion.
  • Confirm a current in-class help signal and how a pupil can reach an adult privately.

Materials

  • Pupil record
  • Feeling, clue and request cards
  • Pens, drawing materials or agreed communication aids

40-minute plan

TimeActivity
3 minWelcome and close previous access feedback
5 minTeach precise feeling words
6 minExplore Ari: clues and uncertainty
5 minModel feeling, action and need
12 minComplete the three tasks
4 minCompare respectful help messages
3 minExit check and private help reminder
2 minReset and transition

Vocabulary

  • feeling: An emotional experience, such as worry or excitement.
  • clue: Something we notice that may help us understand.
  • uncertain: Not sure yet.
  • support: Help that responds to someone’s needs.

Model the task

Think aloud: “I know the file failed. I do not know Noor’s feeling. Frustrated is possible, and disappointed could fit too. I can ask whether Noor would like help.” Model pointing to a help card without saying a feeling.

Support

Use two feeling choices and read each scenario separately. Accept pointing, drawing, signing or scribing. Reduce writing while retaining the distinction between clue and certainty.

Stretch

Compare two plausible interpretations of a scenario. Rewrite a certainty claim using “might” and a respectful question; explain why the change matters.

Fallback

Teach from the fictional scenarios orally. Pupils select from words read aloud and an adult records one sentence; omit any body map or performance activity.

Safety and sensitive discussion

Do not request personal body sensations, diagnoses, recent incidents or a public mood rating. If a pupil reports feeling unwell, respond through school procedures rather than assuming anxiety. Use Week 5 disclosure guidance for a safeguarding concern.

What to check

Assess whether pupils use a plausible feeling word, keep uncertainty about another person’s feelings, and communicate a support need. Do not assess the mood they report or how quickly it changes.

Common misconceptions

  • A face or body clue does not prove an emotion.
  • Feeling words do not always make a feeling disappear.
  • A person may feel more than one emotion or not know the word.
  • Support is allowed at any point; it is not earned by coping alone first.

Slide 10: A clue is not proof

Check distinction between observation and interpretation. Collect no public mood data; end with the agreed private help route.

Booklet answers

1 · More than one possible feeling

2 · Sort what the words tell us

Worried = feeling; shutting book = action; request for first step = support need. Helpful response: show one step and ask whether the person wants to try it together.

3 · A message that can be understood

A: “Please show me the new room.” B: “I feel unwell. I need an adult now.” C: “I keep feeling worried. Can we talk somewhere private?” An agreed help symbol is also valid.

Week 3 · 40 minutes

Belonging and having a say

Open lesson slideshow

Objective

Recognise inclusive group practices, distinguish disagreement from harm and apply the four elements of meaningful participation.

  • I can suggest a practical way to make a group more inclusive.
  • I can separate an everyday disagreement from behaviour needing adult help.
  • I can explain how a pupil view reaches someone who can respond.

Preparation

  • Read the club cards and four Lundy definitions.
  • Plan a real small class access decision within your authority and a time to report back.
  • Use fictional groups so nobody has to disclose family structure, religion, friendships or online memberships.

Materials

  • Learning record
  • Club and participation cards
  • Pens or communication tools

40-minute plan

TimeActivity
3 minWelcome and fictional-group option
5 minMakers Club barrier discussion
6 minInclusion and disagreement/harm sort
7 minTeach and model four participation elements
10 minComplete the three learning tasks
4 minCompare proposal quality and choices
3 minExit and genuine access suggestion
2 minReset and transition

Vocabulary

  • belonging: Feeling accepted and included in a group.
  • diversity: Differences among people, experiences and ways of living.
  • inclusion: Making it possible for people to participate.
  • influence: Views receive serious consideration and a response.

Model the task

Think aloud through Casey’s proposal: “The leader can change meetings, so that is the audience. A response explaining a trial provides influence. I still need to check whether the written board is accessible.”

Support

Read one card at a time; match Space, Voice, Audience and Influence to four printed actions. Accept pointing or scribing and focus on one practical access barrier.

Stretch

Critique a club where every member can write a suggestion but the leader never reads the board. Explain which elements are missing and propose a realistic decision-and-feedback process.

Fallback

Use the complete club story as an oral task and have pupils point to the four definitions. The learning works without personal maps, photographs or live online communities.

Safety and sensitive discussion

Avoid making any pupil a spokesperson for a group or identity. Do not stage exclusion or require reconciliation. Address discriminatory language through school policy. Use Week 5 disclosure guidance if personal harm is disclosed.

What to check

Look for a barrier linked to a useful access change, a safe response to harm, and a proposal including all four participation elements. Do not grade pupils’ group memberships or willingness to speak publicly.

Common misconceptions

  • Belonging is not earned by disclosing personal information.
  • Fairness may require different supports.
  • Rights do not depend on perfect behaviour.
  • A vote does not by itself show all four participation elements.
  • Harmful exclusion is not a balanced disagreement to mediate.

Week 4 · 40 minutes

Regulation: finding helpful support

Open lesson slideshow

Objective

Compare regulation options, include adult and environmental support, and make a flexible plan without treating calm behaviour as the goal.

  • I can choose support for a fictional situation and explain why it may help.
  • I can explain how an adult or environment could change.
  • I can review a plan and ask for more help when needed.

Preparation

  • Check individual support plans and agreed safe movement/quiet-space arrangements.
  • Prepare optional low-demand strategy choices; no breathing exercise is compulsory.
  • Decide how pupils can request a change or stop, and give the Week 3 decision feedback.

Materials

  • Learning record
  • Support menu and scenario cards
  • Paper and pencils; optional agreed focus tools

40-minute plan

TimeActivity
3 minWelcome and feedback on access suggestion
5 minTeach support and shared responsibility
5 minAnalyse noisy-room scenario
6 minModel menu; optional observe/try/paper comparison
12 minThree pupil tasks and flexible plan
4 minReview an unhelpful trial
3 minExit check and help reminder
2 minReset and supported transition

Vocabulary

  • regulation: Support for managing energy, feelings and attention.
  • strategy: An action someone may try.
  • co-regulation: Support from another person to help someone manage.
  • review: Check what helped and decide what to change.

Model the task

Model Rowan’s case using: barrier, person’s preference, adult change, request route and review. Say: “This did not help enough. That tells us to change the support.” Avoid performing a public emotional regulation demonstration with a pupil.

Support

Offer two options relevant to the fictional barrier and two adult actions. Read them aloud; accept pointing. Keep any personal response private and treat observing as full participation.

Stretch

Compare a strategy focused on the individual with a change to the environment. Explain why a plan should include the person’s preference and a review rather than a fixed “calm down” requirement.

Fallback

Use the printed trial reports entirely on paper. Pupils recommend support for characters; there is no need for equipment, movement, breathing exercises or personal ratings.

Safety and sensitive discussion

Follow each pupil’s agreed plan. Do not use touch, breath-holding, closed-eye demands or forced participation. A pupil can stop immediately. Respond to reported illness through school procedures. For safety concerns, use the Week 5 protocol.

What to check

Assess the match between need and support, an adult/environment action, and a flexible review. Do not assess emotional disclosure, stillness or whether a pupil reports feeling calmer.

Common misconceptions

  • Regulation is not the same as compliance, silence or a cheerful face.
  • Different people may need different supports.
  • An unhelpful strategy does not mean someone failed.
  • An adult may need to change the environment, not only offer a tool.
  • A strategy does not replace health, safeguarding or professional support.

Week 5 · 40 minutes

Trusted help and safe choices

Open lesson slideshow

Objective

Recognise trustworthy behaviour, practise accessible help-seeking and identify suitable support routes and their limits.

  • I can describe behaviours that make an adult a safer person to approach.
  • I can make a clear help request using a chosen communication route.
  • I can choose another route if help is delayed and know when urgent action is needed.

Preparation

  • Confirm the actual safeguarding lead, deputy, pastoral contact and available private help route before the lesson.
  • Read the source service guidance and the school’s current disclosure/reporting procedure.
  • Use fictional adults on public resources; offer confidential support to a pupil unable to identify anyone they trust.

Materials

  • Learning record
  • Support route and response cards
  • Blank paper or agreed help symbol; no personal devices/messages required

40-minute plan

TimeActivity
3 minWelcome, fictional option and local help route
5 minTrustworthy behaviour and warning signs
5 minModel a private request
6 minService matching and confidentiality limits
12 minComplete the three tasks
4 minRehearse first and backup routes using fictional notes
3 minExit and identify real available school help
2 minReset and private follow-up opportunity

Vocabulary

  • trusted adult: An adult whose behaviour supports safety and takes concerns seriously.
  • support network: People and services someone can approach for help.
  • private: Shared with care, rather than with everyone.
  • urgent: Needing attention straight away.

Model the task

Use a fictional note: “I need help with a message.” Model an adult acknowledging the request, checking whether help is needed now and explaining the next person involved. Demonstrate a backup route without suggesting the pupil caused an adult’s failure to respond.

Support

Read two response cards at a time. Offer an agreed help symbol and model an adult response. Accept a fictional network consisting of school roles and one backup; do not require personal names.

Stretch

Explain why “tell someone” is incomplete without an accessible request and an available listener. Distinguish Childline support, GP health care, YoungMinds information, CEOP reporting and emergency response.

Fallback

Use only the printed fictional response/service cards. Adult scribes a first request and backup route. If a pupil needs support now, pause the task and arrange it using school procedures.

Safety and sensitive discussion

If a child discloses harm: listen without pressing for detail; take them seriously and say it is not their fault. Do not promise secrecy or investigate. Explain who must be informed, record their words accurately and report promptly to the designated safeguarding lead through school procedures. If immediate danger exists, get emergency help. Do not confront the person alleged to have caused harm. Keep personal network details off displays and websites.

What to check

Check recognition of supportive versus pressuring behaviour, appropriate service matches and a workable backup route. Assess the fictional reasoning; do not mark a pupil on how many trusted people they can name or whether they disclose a concern.

Common misconceptions

  • A familiar person or authority title does not guarantee safe behaviour.
  • Help-seeking can feel uncomfortable even with a safe adult.
  • A child does not need five named adults to qualify for support.
  • Not all services offer counselling, confidentiality or emergency response in the same way.
  • CEOP is not a general bullying, hacking or fake-account reporting service.

Week 6 · 40 minutes

Kindness, fairness and boundaries

Open lesson slideshow

Objective

Recognise respectful kindness, distinguish prejudice from discriminatory treatment, and choose safe responses that respect boundaries.

  • I can explain why a kind offer must leave room for “no”.
  • I can identify an unfair judgement and an unfair action in fictional examples.
  • I can propose a safe response to exclusion without taking responsibility for solving harm alone.

Preparation

  • Check the class needs and school response to discriminatory incidents.
  • Prepare brief fictional cards without slurs or requests to describe real incidents.
  • Remind pupils of a private route to an available adult.

Materials

  • Learning record
  • Offer, fairness and boundary cards
  • Pens, drawing materials or communication aids

40-minute plan

TimeActivity
3 minWelcome and voluntary fictional approach
5 minModel offer, answer and respect
5 minSort actions and boundaries
6 minTeach prejudice/treatment and model design-club response
12 minComplete the three tasks
4 minCompare practical fair responses
3 minExit and private help route
2 minReset and transition

Vocabulary

  • kindness: Considerate words or actions that respect another person.
  • boundary: A limit someone sets about what they want or accept.
  • prejudice: An unfair judgement about people based on assumptions about their identity or group.
  • discrimination: Unfair treatment linked to who someone is or is assumed to be.

Model the task

Model the full exchange: offer, wait, accept the answer. Then contrast a kind sentence with an adult changing an exclusionary practice. Say: “This pupil does not have to become friends with someone to receive fair access.”

Support

Offer two response cards at a time and use a non-verbal “no” option. Scribe one explanation of a boundary and one adult-help step. All tasks can remain fictional.

Stretch

Explain why “be nice” may be insufficient when a group’s routine excludes someone. Propose an access change, a responsible adult and a way to check whether the person’s view influenced the response.

Fallback

Use the four actions from Task 1 orally and one brief design-club example. An adult records the chosen safe action and reason; no live kindness mission or peer feedback is required.

Safety and sensitive discussion

Do not enact prejudice, assign pupils as victims, repeat slurs or invite discriminatory debate. Address real disclosures and incidents through school policy and Week 5 guidance. No forced apology, reconciliation or physical contact forms part of this lesson.

What to check

Look for respect for a no, a distinction between judgement and unfair treatment, and a safe response with adult responsibility. Do not reward how many favours, compliments or social contacts a pupil completes.

Common misconceptions

  • A kind offer does not oblige another person to agree or repay it.
  • Respect does not require touch, forgiveness, friendship or staying near harm.
  • Prejudice is a judgement; discriminatory treatment is an action or practice.
  • Identical resources do not always give equal access.
  • A harmed pupil is not responsible for educating or rehabilitating the person who harmed them.

Model answer: No. Respect does not require touch, forgiveness or friendship. Safety and boundaries matter.

Week 7 · 40 minutes

All About Me: a private celebration of learning

Open lesson slideshow

Objective

Bring together the six taught themes using evidence, review a safe next step and choose what, if anything, to share.

  • I can use examples to explain the six themes we have studied.
  • I can improve a response using feedback or support.
  • I can choose a next step and explain what help would make it possible.

Preparation

  • Provide the complete Morgan profile so no personal evidence or previous work is needed.
  • Prepare a private collection method and genuine options for sharing or keeping work private.
  • Decide when the class will receive feedback on their suggested teaching changes.

Materials

  • Learning record
  • Six Morgan evidence cards
  • Word banks from this unit; pens or communication aids

40-minute plan

TimeActivity
3 minWelcome and choice of fictional/private profile
4 minRetrieve and repair three claims
5 minRead Morgan profile and model one evidence panel
16 minThree tasks: about 4 minutes recall, 9 minutes panels, 3 minutes review/target
5 minRead back selected answers and give specific feedback
3 minOptional celebration choice and pupil view
2 minExit next step and help reminder
2 minCollect privately, reset and transition

Vocabulary

  • reflection: Thinking about learning and what may help next.
  • evidence: An example supporting a claim.
  • choice: An option someone is free to select or decline.
  • progress: A change in understanding or skill, which can include using support.

Model the task

Model one panel using claim, evidence and next action. “Morgan asked for a diagram and changed one part: that supports problem solving. The next step could be explaining the change with labels.” Then improve “Morgan is nervous” to “Morgan might be nervous or excited; we should ask.”

Support

Read one Morgan card and offer two matching theme/phrase options. Accept a pointed evidence line with a spoken or scribed idea. Reduce handwriting rather than reducing access to the six themes.

Stretch

Explain why a plausible answer can still be too certain about feelings. Improve a plan that gives the pupil all responsibility, or trace a suggestion through all four Lundy elements.

Fallback

Use the six character cards orally with an adult recording brief responses. Choose one next step together. The lesson needs no photos, prior work, public speech, online account or personal family/support-network information.

Safety and sensitive discussion

Keep personal information private; do not upload or display real support contacts, health details or family information. A pupil can change their sharing choice. If work suggests harm, use the Week 5 disclosure protocol and school procedures rather than treating it as assessment evidence.

What to check

Formative review: six panels, up to 2 points each for a local 12-point teaching check. Award 1 for an accurate theme-specific idea and 1 for relevant evidence or a safe practical next action. Do not award the second point for generic praise alone. Accept speaking, pointing, drawing and support equally. Use the resource exemplars; revisit safety misconceptions regardless of total. Record one learning next step. This is not a standardised or accredited assessment.

Common misconceptions

  • A fictional answer can demonstrate the same understanding as a personal one.
  • Progress need not mean no longer feeling worried or needing support.
  • Sharing, display and applause are not conditions of celebration.
  • Source headings do not prove topics were taught; review only the six themes in this pack.
