An artist uses line and colour

Monday 18 January 2027 · P5 · 40 minutes

Weekly outcome: Find out about an artist who expresses feelings.

I can find out how an artist uses line and colour.

Today I can

  • I can name the artist or point to his name.
  • I can find two facts in the short description.
  • I can try my own idea with shapes.

In your book or arts log. You can point, speak, sign, draw or write. An adult can record your confirmed words.

Theme: Resilience & Change. You can use an invented idea, work quietly and share privately.

artist

A person who makes art.

line

A long mark.

shape

A space with an edge, such as a circle.

arrange

Put things where you want them.

Arrival

Arrival

0–3 minutes

Today we find out about one real artist.

You can point, speak, sign or pass.

Our own art can use a made-up feeling.

What to say

An artist makes art. Today the artist is Henri Matisse. Say the name slowly, or point to it while I read it. We will use a short description, not a copy of his artwork. You may use an invented feeling for your own response and share privately.

What good looks like

The pupil can indicate the artist's name or recognise it when read aloud. No personal disclosure or public performance is required.

Materials-free fallback

Read the name aloud and let the pupil confirm it with an agreed gesture. No print, image or art materials are needed.

Check and response

Naming, pointing to or confirming the spoken name is acceptable. A pupil does not need to spell the name from memory.

Starter

Starter

3–6 minutes

Watch a curved air line, then a sharp air line.

What do you notice?

Could each line suggest a feeling? You may have your own view.

What to say

I make a slow curve in the air, then a sharp zigzag. Tell me one thing you notice before guessing a feeling. A curve might suggest calm to me. You might read it differently. We do not know a maker's private mood from one line.

What good looks like

The pupil notices a visible feature and distinguishes it from an interpretation, with support if needed.

Materials-free fallback

Describe the two lines aloud or follow the pupil's directions. A pupil can watch without making a movement.

Check and response

The curve bends smoothly; the zigzag changes direction sharply. A feeling linked to either feature is a possible reading, not a fixed rule.

I do

I do

6–12 minutes

Listen to the short artist description.

Find two facts: cut shapes and moved shapes.

Watch the adult try two new layouts.

These class tests are our own work.

Artist description to read aloud

Henri Matisse

Cut-outs · Later work

Henri Matisse made art with line, colour and cut paper. He used line and colour to show feelings. He cut shapes from painted paper. He moved the shapes to try a new layout. We can read his art in our own way. We do not know his mood from it.

Notice: What did he cut? What could he move?

How could moving your own shapes change the way your piece feels?

He cut shapes from painted paper and moved the shapes to try a layout. In my own art, I could move shapes apart to suggest calm, or put them close to suggest energy. Those are my own readings. They do not tell us what he felt.

Other readings can be valid. Point to a detail and say why.

No artwork by this artist is shown. We use a description in our own words. A personal reading starts “This suggests ... to me”.

Which feature could fit your own idea?

What to say

Read the artist card aloud in short parts. Matisse used line and colour to express feelings. In his cut-paper work he cut painted paper and could change the layout. Those are facts from the description. I now use my own three torn shapes. I place them close, then move them apart. I think the wider space feels calm. That last idea is my reading. These shapes and this design are an original class example, not a copy of his art.

What good looks like

The adult identifies two accurate details and models the difference between a fact and a personal interpretation. The original class sample uses a general method while retaining its own design.

Materials-free fallback

Read the description aloud. Use three finger positions close together, then apart, or describe those positions. A pupil may direct the adult's hand positions.

Check and response

Ask: 'Which is a fact from the card: he moved cut shapes, or everyone must feel calm?' Expected: he moved cut shapes. If unclear, reread that sentence and point to the original class shapes.

We do

We do

12–18 minutes

Help place three class shapes.

Try them close, then far apart.

Say what changed. Choose a layout for a made-up feeling.

Finish: This suggests ___ to me.

B. Ask the maker. If a pupil selects A, model two different readings of the same blue shape. Parallel check: 'Could a blue shape show joy to its maker?' Expected: yes, if that is their intended meaning. Move on when the pupil can identify a feature and allow another reading.

Other reasons can be valid. Show the feature that fits your idea.

What to say

You direct our own class sample. We keep the shapes the same and change their spacing. What do you notice? Which layout could fit your invented feeling? Hinge: A pupil says all blue shapes must mean sad. Do we A accept one fixed meaning, or B ask the maker for their meaning and reason?

What good looks like

The pupil compares two layouts and gives or confirms a reason linked to spacing. The pupil recognises that a colour does not have one compulsory emotional meaning.

Materials-free fallback

Direct three adult finger positions or listen to two spoken layouts. Choose verbally or with an agreed gesture.

Check and response

B. Ask the maker. If a pupil selects A, model two different readings of the same blue shape. Parallel check: 'Could a blue shape show joy to its maker?' Expected: yes, if that is their intended meaning. Move on when the pupil can identify a feature and allow another reading.

Create

Create

18–32 minutes

In your book or arts log, name the artist.

Keep two facts from the description.

Make or draw three shapes of your own.

Try two layouts. Choose one for a made-up feeling.

Keep your tests and your own reason.

Supported

Listen to the artist card with an adult.

Point to or say cut shapes and moved shapes.

Try three own shapes close, then apart.

Choose a layout. Tell or show your reason.

Independent

Name the artist and find two facts.

Make two new trials with your own shapes.

Choose a layout for a made-up feeling.

Add the facts, tests and reason to your arts log.

Aspire

Complete the two trials and artist facts.

Try one new gap or line in your own design.

Say how it changes your reading.

Keep your own view and allow a different view.

Choose one route within the same 14 minutes. Every route keeps your own choice and arts-log evidence.

What to say

Choose drawing, torn paper or a silent route. For torn paper, make three simple shapes from scrap. Tearing is enough; no cutting tool is needed. Try the shapes close together and farther apart before fixing anything. For drawing, make two tiny versions on scrap. For a sound response, try three quiet taps close in time, then with wider pauses; this changes space in time in your own art form. Keep sound below talking volume and stop on my raised hand. The pupil decides the feeling and layout. Do not recreate an existing artwork. An adult may handle materials under the pupil's direction.

What good looks like

The pupil retains two real artist details, makes two original trials and chooses a layout or sequence with a reason. A simple test is enough today. Staff distinguish the artist facts, the pupil's view and the adult's observation.

Materials-free fallback

Use or direct three silent hand positions close, then apart. The pupil names or confirms the artist and two spoken facts. The adult records the actual sequence and confirmed pupil reason when a recording method is available.

Check and response

Example only: Matisse cut painted paper and moved shapes. I tried three own shapes close and apart. I chose apart because it feels calm to me. This is the pupil's possible method link, not a claim about the artist's mood.

Review

Review

32–37 minutes

Tell an adult one fact about the artist.

Show your two tests and chosen layout.

Say: I chose ___ because ___.

Tidy and keep your work for next time.

Teacher model only: Matisse cut painted paper and moved shapes. I used the idea of moving my own shapes. I chose wide gaps because they suggest calm to me.

Teacher model only: I kept the same three shapes and changed their spacing. Someone else may read my piece in a different way. The artist's private mood is not known from our description.

Teacher examples only. Keep your own work, words and choices as evidence.

What to say

Ask for an artist fact first, then the pupil's own view. A listener describes one feature before offering a different interpretation. The maker can keep their choice. Sharing may be private. Use the last two minutes to cap glue, collect scraps and store loose shapes and tests flat in each pupil's own tray.

What good looks like

The pupil separates an accurate artist detail from a personal interpretation. The trials and chosen layout are retained honestly for lesson two.

Materials-free fallback

The pupil directs a repeat of their silent layout. The adult repeats one fact and asks the pupil to confirm or correct it, then records the pupil's own choice.

Check and response

A useful artist fact is that he moved cut shapes to test a layout. A personal view is that a pupil's wide gaps seem calm. Both belong in the log with their different purposes clear.

What to say

Keep the actual artist facts and your own work separate in the log. I can scribe your words, read them back and let you correct them. Use today's real date and record the help used. Keep your own tests so you can make a short response next time.

What good looks like

A dated entry gives or confirms the artist's name, two facts, a pupil-made trial and a chosen feature with a reason. No adult example is prefilled as pupil evidence.

Materials-free fallback

Use an oral log. The adult confirms the name, facts and choice with the pupil, then makes a dated witness note at the next recording opportunity.

Check and response

Teacher guidance

Preparation

For four pupils: four books or arts logs, four pencils and eight small test sheets.

Paper route: twelve ready-torn scrap shapes plus spare scrap paper, four mats and two glue sticks. Keep shapes loose until a layout is chosen. No cutting tool is required.

Drawing route: four sets of familiar drawing tools. Optional paint uses five brushes, four mats, two stable water pots, a small shared amount of washable paint and cloths.

Adult model: three original torn shapes and two different arrangements. Label them as original class examples. Do not display or copy an actual artist's work.

Quiet sound route: fingertips only, below talking volume. Materials-free route: silent hand positions or pupil-directed adult movement. Keep four labelled storage spaces for actual trials.

Teaching approach

Read the artist description or model a small original plan. Describe a feature, a choice and a reason. Keep each pupil's own choice and reason. You do not need to paint or perform expertly. Judge intention and explanation. Use the least help needed and record the support actually used. Scribe the pupil's own words, read them back and allow correction. Plan in any language.

Sound stays below talking volume. Use a raised open hand to stop. Offer the silent option before sound, seated movement and a private audience. No personal disclosure is required. Use familiar non-toxic materials and local procedures. Keep wet materials away from electrical items.

Prompt ladder

  1. Point to the instruction or place.
  2. Ask a short question.
  3. Rephrase the instruction.
  4. Provide a scaffold or choice.
  5. Model the first step only, then return the task.

Common misconceptions

An artist's work tells us exactly how they felt A description can give facts about a method. A feeling suggested to a viewer is an interpretation, not proof of a private mood.

To learn from an artist we must copy their picture Use a general method, such as moving shapes, then make an original design with the pupil's own shapes and arrangement.

A colour always means one feeling Ask the maker for their intended meaning and a reason. Different personal and cultural readings are valid.

This lesson contributes to the Explore record. A whole award decision needs the full portfolio. Any optional next level evidence is additional.

A calm pause

Put tools down. Choose a quiet position. You can watch or use silent movement when you return.

Ask an adult for the agreed quiet space or return cue. You do not need to explain a personal feeling to the group.
