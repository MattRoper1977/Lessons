Two ideas for resilience

Arrival

Arrival

0–3 minutes

Choose visual art, sound or silent movement.

Use a made-up idea. You may share privately.

What to say

We are planning an original artwork about resilience. It can show a pause, a new route or help. It does not need a story from your life. Choose visual art, a quiet sound idea or a silent movement idea. You can plan by speaking, drawing, signing or directing me.

What good looks like

The pupil chooses or indicates a form and understands that today's work is planning. An invented or abstract idea is accepted. Quiet and private routes are available.

Materials-free fallback

Pupil speaks or directs an adult to describe a four-part sequence. Stillness, silent movement or an adult-scribed plan are valid.

Check and response

There is no preferred art form. The plan should show an intention through a feature or sequence.

Starter

Starter

3–6 minutes

Think of a seed after wind, a path round a block, or a pattern that returns.

Which might show a way forward? Give a reason.

What to say

A seed could bend then grow in a new direction. A path could find a way around a block. A pattern could stop for a gap, then return. These are made-up possibilities, not fixed meanings. Which one gives you an idea, and why? You may choose another idea.

What good looks like

The pupil links a possible subject or abstract feature to finding a way forward. The adult accepts a reasoned different interpretation and does not infer a private feeling.

Materials-free fallback

Read the choices aloud. Pupil chooses a spoken option or directs one small gesture. No image or materials are needed.

Check and response

For example: A path around a block could show finding another way. A returning pattern could show starting again after a pause.

I do

I do

6–12 minutes

Watch two genuinely different rough plans.

See a picture plan and a short sequence plan.

A rough plan is enough today.

One minute teacher demonstration

0 to 15 seconds Say: I can plan a made-up idea. Show a small seed that bends, then grows another way. Label: change direction.

15 to 30 seconds Show a separate path turning around a block. Say: This is a different subject and layout. Label: find another route.

30 to 45 seconds Show four silent planned parts: repeat, pause, change, return. Say: A sequence note is a plan too.

45 to 60 seconds Point to one option. Say: I choose this because it fits my idea and I can make it. I keep the other plan to show my choice.

Which feature could fit your own idea?

What to say

My first rough idea shows a small seed bending, then a tall curve growing another way. My second shows a path turning around a block. They have different subjects and layouts. For sound or movement, I could plan four parts: repeat, pause, change, return. I label what the feature is meant to suggest. These are adult plans, not finished artwork or your evidence.

What good looks like

The adult models two distinct options and a short label linking a feature to resilience. A small sketch, spoken sequence or four-part notation is enough. Pupils see that a plan need not be neat.

Materials-free fallback

Describe both ideas aloud or demonstrate the short sequence with small silent gestures. An adult can draw or note the pupil's directed layout.

Check and response

Check: Are the plans different only because one is blue? No. A new subject, layout or sequence creates a real choice. If unclear, compare a seed plan with a path plan and identify one difference.

We do

We do

12–18 minutes

Compare two class plans.

Say what is different and what each could suggest.

Which one can be made with simple tools or no tools?

Check your plan

Which pair gives a real choice of ideas?

Choose, then explain your reason.

What to say

Let us compare an invented seed plan and a returning-pattern plan. What is different? What could each suggest? Hinge: which pair gives a real choice? A the same layout in two colours, B a seed changing direction and a path around a block, or C two copies of the same plan. Explain your choice.

What good looks like

The pupil chooses B and names a difference in subject or arrangement. They can explain a possible resilience link and notice one practical constraint. The adult models one comparison, then returns the choice.

Materials-free fallback

Read the options aloud. Pupil may point, sign, speak or direct the adult to outline the two plans in the air.

Check and response

If A or C is chosen, name the repeated layout and model the different subject. Parallel check: Does a four-part pause-and-return sequence offer a different idea from a rising seed drawing? Yes. Continue when the pupil identifies one meaningful difference.

What to say

Plan two different ideas. Use small thumbnails for a picture, or a short ordered note for sound or movement. Keep the work rough and small. Label the feature that could show resilience. Choose one idea because it fits your intention and can be made with the access, time and materials available. This is a planning session, not the full making.

What good looks like

Two genuine options are retained, each with an intention or resilience link. The pupil selects one for a specific reason. A directed or scribed plan preserves the pupil's decisions. No finished artwork is claimed.

Materials-free fallback

Pupil describes two original short sequences or directs an adult to show them. Adult keeps ordered notes and the exact confirmed choice and reason when a recording surface is available.

Check and response

A useful reason links intention and feasibility: I choose the returning pattern because the pause shows a restart and I can make it with silent actions. A third plan is optional if it helps a decision.

Optional stretch what to say

Compare both plans. Note a strength and a limit of each. Think about time, tools or access. Explain why your choice fits the idea. Say what you will test in the detailed plan.

Optional stretch what good looks like

The pupil weighs intention against a practical constraint, compares real options and gives a reasoned choice without claiming completed making.

Optional stretch materials-free fallback

Adult asks the same prompts orally and retains ordered idea notes with the pupil's confirmed comparison and reason.

Review

Review

32–37 minutes

Show your two plans to an adult or partner.

Ask what they notice. You choose what to keep.

Keep both plans and your reason.

Teacher model only: My ideas were a seed changing direction and a path around a block. I chose the path because the turn could show finding another way. I can plan it with a few clear lines.

Teacher model only: I planned a steady rhythm and a pause-and-return rhythm. I chose the second because the pause could show a fresh start. I still need to plan how the piece will end.

Teacher examples only. Keep your own work, words and choices as evidence.

What to say

The listener says one feature they notice, then asks what the maker intends. The maker can keep the choice or revise the reason after considering feedback. You may share with only me. Use the final two minutes to put tools away and store both rough plans for the next lesson.

What good looks like

A pupil-owned decision remains clear after feedback. Both options and the selection reason are retained. Private sharing and an unchanged choice are valid.

Materials-free fallback

Adult reads both sequence notes back. Pupil confirms the choice or changes it. Note feedback separately from the pupil's decision.

Check and response

Useful feedback: I notice the path turns around the block. The maker can explain why that matters. Do not require an audience to agree with the interpretation.

Date the real planning work. Record the two ideas, your chosen plan, the feature you want to use and your reason. Add one thing the detailed plan will need next time. You can speak, sign, sketch or direct a scribe, and I will read your words back.

Teacher guidance

Preparation

For five pupils: five books or arts logs, five pencils and ten small planning spaces for two rough ideas each. Use a third space only if useful.

Optional visual route: six drawing tools including the adult model, five blank layout areas and a small set of colour tools for planning labels. No paint, cutting or fixing is required this week.

Sound or movement plans: five four-part sequence spaces, or five spoken/witness records. Fingertips and seated actions are enough for a brief planning demonstration.

Adult examples: two or three labelled original rough plans, one selected plan and one materials-and-time plan. These are teacher models, not pupil evidence.

For lesson two retain each pupil's chosen rough plan, reason and any feedback. No recording device or specialist art kit is required.

Teaching approach

Read the artist description or model a small original plan. Describe a feature, a choice and a reason. Keep each pupil's own choice and reason. You do not need to paint or perform expertly. Judge intention and explanation. Use the least help needed and record the support actually used. Scribe the pupil's own words, read them back and allow correction. Plan in any language.

Sound stays below talking volume. Use a raised open hand to stop. Offer the silent option before sound, seated movement and a private audience. No personal disclosure is required. Use familiar non-toxic materials and local procedures. Keep wet materials away from electrical items.

Prompt ladder

  1. Point to the instruction or place.
  2. Ask a short question.
  3. Rephrase the instruction.
  4. Provide a scaffold or choice.
  5. Model the first step only, then return the task.

Common misconceptions

Resilience means never pausing or asking for help. A pause, support or a different route can be part of finding a way forward. Use invented or abstract ideas, not personal hardship.

Two ideas must be almost the same design. Change the main subject, arrangement or sequence so the plans offer a real choice. Changing only a colour may not make a genuinely different idea.

A plan is a finished artwork. Keep rough sketches, labels or short sequence notes as plans. The full making happens later. Do not claim completed artwork this week.

A more complex idea is always better. Choose an idea that communicates an intention and can be made with the time, space, materials and access available. A small original piece can work well.

This lesson contributes to the Explore record. A whole award decision needs the full portfolio. Any optional next level evidence is additional.

A calm pause

Put tools down. Choose a quiet position. You can watch or use silent movement when you return.

Ask an adult for the agreed quiet space or return cue. You do not need to explain a personal feeling to the group.
