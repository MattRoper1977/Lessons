A first response to change

Wednesday 6 January 2027 · P5 · 40 minutes

Weekly outcome: Explore how artists express resilience and change.

I can develop my first response, test a change and record how an artist's idea helped me.

Today I can

  • I make or direct an original response to resilience or change.
  • I test one adjustment and describe its effect.
  • I add evidence and a personal view to my arts log.

In your book or arts log. You can point, speak, sign, draw or write. An adult can record your confirmed words.

Theme: Resilience & Change. You can use an invented idea, work quietly and share privately.

repeat

Do something again.

change

Make something different.

method

A way to do something.

response

Your own art made after exploring an idea.

resilience

Finding a way forward. This can include a pause, help or a new method.

abstract

Using colour, shape or pattern without showing a realistic scene.

Arrival

Arrival

0–3 minutes

Recall your first response. You may keep the art form or change your method.

What to say

Last time you tried an idea. You may keep that form or change your method. Let us check the quiet option and stop signal before we begin.

What good looks like

The pupil recognises a previous choice or chooses a manageable restart.

Materials-free fallback

Adult reads the ordered note. Pupil repeats or directs the previous gesture or spoken sequence.

Starter

Starter

3–6 minutes

Recall one detail about Matisse and one about Thomas. Listen to the cards again if needed.

Ready

Watch or direct a small move, a repeat, a larger move and a pause. The adult models the movement. No sound or personal story is needed.

What to say

Which artist tested arrangements of cut shapes? Which artist used repeated coloured marks? You can listen to the cards again.

What good looks like

The pupil identifies an artist feature, with rereading if needed.

Materials-free fallback

Use the spoken cards and two verbal choices. Point to a numbered choice or answer orally.

I do

I do

6–12 minutes

Watch one change to the original seed example. What changed? Why?

Artist descriptions to read aloud

Henri Matisse

Cut-outs · Later career

Henri Matisse made paintings and later developed cut-outs as artworks in their own right. Assistants painted paper. He cut shapes and directed their arrangement. The shapes could move while he tested an idea. This gives us a way to discuss changing a method and revising a design.

Notice: What could Matisse change before the shapes were fixed?

How might moving a shape help an artist keep an idea going?

Possible response

He could change the positions or arrangement of the cut shapes. The artist can try a different position without starting everything again. I see this as one possible example of adapting.

Alma Thomas

The Eclipse · 1970

Alma Thomas developed an abstract painting style using repeated coloured marks. Nature and space helped her find ideas. In The Eclipse, made in 1970, a dark blue centre sits within rings of colour. The centre is off to one side. This arrangement can suggest a moment of movement and change.

Notice: Which two details does the description give us?

How could repeating marks help us show a change?

Possible response

A dark blue centre and surrounding rings of colour. The centre is off to one side. We could keep repeating a mark and gradually change its size, spacing or direction. This would be our own response, rather than a copy of the painting.

We use descriptions. A personal interpretation starts “This suggests ... to me”.

Which feature could fit your own idea?

What to say

My first marks all point sideways. I want growth to be clearer, so I change the last marks to point up. I keep the first part. This is my interpretation of resilience. The artists helped me think about a method, but this design is my own.

What good looks like

The pupil notices a specific before-and-after change and hears a reason linked to the idea.

Materials-free fallback

Use the same seated gesture sequence, changing only its final direction. Describe it if movement is uncomfortable.

Check and response

Did I change a feature for a reason, or only add more? Expected: changed the final direction to suggest growth.

We do

We do

12–18 minutes

Try two versions of one feature. Ask what your partner notices.

Hinge: must you use feedback, ignore it, or consider it and choose for your purpose?

Feedback and your purpose

A listener says, “The slow ending feels steady to me.” What should the maker do?

Choose, then explain your reason.

What to say

Let us try both versions. Tell me what you notice before suggesting a change. You can say 'The second version seems ... because ...'. The artist chooses what to keep. A listener says, 'The slow ending feels steady to me.' Must the artist A use that opinion, B ignore it, or C consider it and choose a change for their own purpose? Explain.

What good looks like

The pupil compares two versions and hears feedback linked to a visible or audible feature. They retain control of the choice. The pupil selects C and explains that feedback helps a decision but does not remove their choice.

Materials-free fallback

Perform two quiet or silent versions. The adult describes observed pace, spacing or direction without judging personal feelings. Choose from the spoken options and say or indicate one feature to test.

Check and response

If the pupil selects A or B without a reason, model two different interpretations and reread the purpose. Parallel hinge: 'Two people read blue differently. Can the artist keep blue and explain their intention?' Expected: yes. Move on when the pupil chooses a feature for a reason.

Create

18–32 minutes

Develop your own response. Test one change. Choose the version to keep.

Use a quiet or silent route if you prefer. You may pause or ask for help.

Aspire

Use two features in your piece. Say how they work together. How might someone else read your art? Keep or change a feature. Say why. The next level task is optional.

What to say

Make the response manageable. Use repetition or rearrangement in your own way. Keep the first version in a note or memory cue, test one change, then decide what to keep.

What good looks like

The pupil creates or directs a short original response and makes one purposeful adjustment. Art form may change when it improves access or suits the idea.

Materials-free fallback

Use a silent gesture, short drama sequence, original sound pattern or spoken piece. Adult observes before and after and records the exact ordered actions and pupil's reason.

Optional stretch what to say

Set one skill goal. Keep a note of your first try. Practise that skill. Try your piece again. Compare the two versions. Say how an artist's method helped. What feedback did you use? Choose your next practice step.

Optional stretch what good looks like

The pupil identifies how practising one skill changed the response and evaluates the change against their own intention rather than simply saying it is better.

Optional stretch materials-free fallback

Adult observes both live versions and records the ordered actions with the pupil's explanation. Audio, image and written pupil work are not required.

Review

Review

32–37 minutes

Explain the artist link and your own choices. Record your change and its effect in the arts log.

Thomas's repeated marks helped me think about repeating a gesture. My movement begins with small curved hands and gradually rises. I tried a sudden jump at the end, then a slow rise. I kept the slow rise because it suggests steady growth to me. Someone else may read it differently. Next I will practise the pauses.

Teacher examples only. Keep your own work, words and choices as evidence.

What to say

What idea did you borrow from an artist's method? What did you make yourself? Which change helped, and how do you know? You can explain that different viewers may read it differently.

What good looks like

The pupil identifies a genuine artist link, describes their own choice and evaluates a specific adjustment.

Materials-free fallback

Adult records a brief dated oral log. Separate the pupil's stated intention from the adult's observation.

What did you learn about making art through change? Choose one next step, such as more practice, a different material or asking for feedback. You can be satisfied with a small response today.

Teacher guidance

Preparation

For five pupils: five books or arts logs, five pencils and ten small test sheets if available.

Optional visual route: five sheets, six brushes including the adult model, a small amount of familiar non-toxic paint, five mats, three stable water pots and cloths.

Optional collage route: ten scrap sheets and three glue sticks. Tear paper instead of cutting; no pins.

Movement, original words or quiet finger taps need no art materials. A pupil may direct the adult.

Keep the original work or a dated observation note and the pupil's confirmed words for lesson two.

Teaching approach

Model a small choice, two trials and a reason. You do not need to paint or perform expertly. Judge intention and explanation. Use the least help needed and record the support actually used. Scribe the pupil's own words, read them back and allow correction. Plan in any language.

Sound stays below talking volume. Use a raised open hand to stop. Offer the silent option before sound, seated movement and a private audience. No personal disclosure is required. Use familiar non-toxic materials and local procedures. Keep wet materials away from electrical items.

Prompt ladder

  1. Point to the instruction or place.
  2. Ask a short question.
  3. Rephrase the instruction.
  4. Provide a scaffold or choice.
  5. Model the first step only, then return the task.

Common misconceptions

Everyone reads a colour or sound in the same way. Colours and sounds can suggest different feelings to different people. A reasoned personal reading is valid.

Resilience means doing the same thing until it works. An artist can change a method, take a break or ask for help. A purposeful adjustment counts.

A response must copy the artist's work. Borrow an idea such as repetition or rearrangement, then make your own subject and design. Do not recreate an existing composition.

We know an artist's feelings just by describing a work. Separate verified information from interpretation. Say 'This suggests ... to me' rather than claiming to know a private feeling.

This lesson contributes to the Explore record. A whole award decision needs the full portfolio. Any optional next level evidence is additional.

A calm pause

Put tools down. Choose a quiet position. You can watch or use silent movement when you return.

Ask an adult for the agreed quiet space or return cue. You do not need to explain a personal feeling to the group.
