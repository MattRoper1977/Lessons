Artists find different ways

Monday 4 January 2027 · P6 · 40 minutes

Weekly outcome: Explore how artists express resilience and change.

Explore two descriptions of artists' work, test an artistic idea and plan a first response.

Today I can

  • I identify a detail in a description of an artist's work.
  • I test repetition or a change in my chosen art form.
  • I explain one choice for my own response.

In your book or arts log. You can point, speak, sign, draw or write. An adult can record your confirmed words.

Theme: Resilience & Change. You can use an invented idea, work quietly and share privately.

Starter

Starter

3–6 minutes

Repeat a small action. Change its size, pace or direction. What changed?

Ready

Watch or direct a small move, a repeat, a larger move and a pause. The adult models the movement. No sound or personal story is needed.

What to say

What stayed the same? What changed? Repetition means doing something again. We can change pace, size, direction or space.

What good looks like

The pupil notices one change or makes a simple repeat-and-change pattern.

Materials-free fallback

Use silent finger movements or describe 'small, small, larger'.

I do

I do

6–12 minutes

Listen to the artist descriptions. Find one detail in each. Watch the original seed example.

Artist descriptions to read aloud

Henri Matisse

Cut-outs · Later career

Henri Matisse made paintings and later developed cut-outs as artworks in their own right. Assistants painted paper. He cut shapes and directed their arrangement. The shapes could move while he tested an idea. This gives us a way to discuss changing a method and revising a design.

Notice: What could Matisse change before the shapes were fixed?

How might moving a shape help an artist keep an idea going?

Possible response

He could change the positions or arrangement of the cut shapes. The artist can try a different position without starting everything again. I see this as one possible example of adapting.

Alma Thomas

The Eclipse · 1970

Alma Thomas developed an abstract painting style using repeated coloured marks. Nature and space helped her find ideas. In The Eclipse, made in 1970, a dark blue centre sits within rings of colour. The centre is off to one side. This arrangement can suggest a moment of movement and change.

Notice: Which two details does the description give us?

How could repeating marks help us show a change?

Possible response

A dark blue centre and surrounding rings of colour. The centre is off to one side. We could keep repeating a mark and gradually change its size, spacing or direction. This would be our own response, rather than a copy of the painting.

We use descriptions. A personal interpretation starts “This suggests ... to me”.

Which feature could fit your own idea?

What to say

The description tells me that Matisse moved cut shapes while trying ideas. I think this method could help an artist adapt. That second statement is my interpretation. Thomas's work uses repeated marks. My seed will use repetition in a different design.

What good looks like

The pupil can point to or repeat a verified detail and hears the difference between a fact and a personal reading.

Materials-free fallback

Read both cards aloud and model a silent repeat-and-change gesture. No image, internet or art materials are needed.

Check and response

Which is a detail from the description: 'Thomas used rings of colour' or 'Everyone must feel happy'? Expected: rings of colour.

We do

We do

12–18 minutes

Try two versions with a partner or adult. Which version fits your idea? Say why.

Hinge: an idea is unclear. Should the artist copy, change one feature and test, or give up?

One purposeful change

An idea is unclear. Which action helps the artist test it?

Choose, then explain your reason.

What to say

What can move or change? Try version one, pause, then try version two. Which version suggests your chosen idea, and why? You can disagree politely. An artist wants to keep the idea of growth but the first attempt is unclear. Should they A copy every shape from a famous work, B change one feature and try again, or C stop because art must be perfect? Explain your choice.

What good looks like

The pupil tests two versions and gives a reason linked to a feature, such as wider spacing, slower pace or an upward direction. The pupil selects B and links an adjustment to the idea. The teacher rereads and models one change if the pupil selects A or C.

Materials-free fallback

Direct the adult's hand positions, or perform two seated gestures. Say the two versions if movement is uncomfortable. Choose A, B or C verbally or by pointing. Describe the first and last part of a planned gesture or sound pattern.

Check and response

If the pupil cannot explain B, model changing only spacing, then use a parallel hinge: 'A quiet sound idea feels too busy. Would fewer taps and a pause help test the idea?' Continue independently when the pupil identifies a purposeful change.

What to say

Use your own idea. Show something continuing through a change. You may use colour and shape, an original sound pattern, a short movement or your own words. Stop after a small first version.

What good looks like

The pupil makes or directs a first attempt with a clear repeated feature and a deliberate change. The work need not be neat or finished.

Materials-free fallback

Create and repeat a short silent gesture sequence, or speak an original description. Adult records the action sequence and the pupil's stated choice.

Optional stretch what to say

Use both artist cards. Note one fact about each artist. Say which method interests you. Give your reason. Set one skill to practise. You can draw, speak or write.

Optional stretch what good looks like

The pupil distinguishes description from personal view, compares rearrangement with repetition and explains a choice for future practice.

Optional stretch materials-free fallback

Adult asks the same prompts and records a dated note using the pupil's own words.

Review

Review

32–37 minutes

Share privately or with a partner. Explain one artist link. Add your choice to the arts log.

Matisse could move shapes to test a design. I moved my curved marks so the seed seemed to grow upwards. I kept the spaces small at first, then made them wider. Next time I will test fewer marks so the change is clearer.

Teacher examples only. Keep your own work, words and choices as evidence.

What to say

Which artist's method or idea helped you? What did you choose for your own work? What might you test next time? You can say 'This suggests ... to me'.

What good looks like

The log links an artist detail to a pupil choice and identifies one next test. The adult distinguishes the pupil's words from a staff observation.

Materials-free fallback

Give an oral log. Adult retains a dated note of the artist detail, pupil choice and observed first attempt. If recording is unavailable, repeat the pattern with the adult and note it later.

Teacher guidance

Preparation

For five pupils: five books or arts logs, five pencils and ten small test sheets if available.

Optional visual route: five sheets, six brushes including the adult model, a small amount of familiar non-toxic paint, five mats, three stable water pots and cloths.

Optional collage route: ten scrap sheets and three glue sticks. Tear paper instead of cutting; no pins.

Movement, original words or quiet finger taps need no art materials. A pupil may direct the adult.

Keep the original work or a dated observation note and the pupil's confirmed words for lesson two.

Teaching approach

Model a small choice, two trials and a reason. You do not need to paint or perform expertly. Judge intention and explanation. Use the least help needed and record the support actually used. Scribe the pupil's own words, read them back and allow correction. Plan in any language.

Sound stays below talking volume. Use a raised open hand to stop. Offer the silent option before sound, seated movement and a private audience. No personal disclosure is required. Use familiar non-toxic materials and local procedures. Keep wet materials away from electrical items.

Prompt ladder

  1. Point to the instruction or place.
  2. Ask a short question.
  3. Rephrase the instruction.
  4. Provide a scaffold or choice.
  5. Model the first step only, then return the task.

Common misconceptions

Everyone reads a colour or sound in the same way. Colours and sounds can suggest different feelings to different people. A reasoned personal reading is valid.

Resilience means doing the same thing until it works. An artist can change a method, take a break or ask for help. A purposeful adjustment counts.

A response must copy the artist's work. Borrow an idea such as repetition or rearrangement, then make your own subject and design. Do not recreate an existing composition.

We know an artist's feelings just by describing a work. Separate verified information from interpretation. Say 'This suggests ... to me' rather than claiming to know a private feeling.

This lesson contributes to the Explore record. A whole award decision needs the full portfolio. Any optional next level evidence is additional.

A calm pause

Put tools down. Choose a quiet position. You can watch or use silent movement when you return.

Ask an adult for the agreed quiet space or return cue. You do not need to explain a personal feeling to the group.
