One skill to practise

Monday 11 January 2027 · P6 · 40 minutes

Weekly outcome: Develop a skill and record it in my arts log.

I can choose one technique, keep a first try and practise it.

Today I can

  • I can choose one technique to practise.
  • I can keep a real before test and set one goal.
  • I can record what I tried and what I noticed.

In your book or arts log. You can point, speak, sign, draw or write. An adult can record your confirmed words.

Theme: Resilience & Change. You can use an invented idea, work quietly and share privately.

skill

The space or time between two things.

Keeping a similar pace.

Arrival

Arrival

0–3 minutes

Choose four marks or four quiet or silent actions.

Keep this choice for both lessons. You may share privately.

What to say

Today you will choose one small technique to practise across both lessons. You could place four similar marks with even gaps, or make four quiet taps or silent finger actions at a steady pace. Pick a comfortable version. You may direct me and share privately. Before the first test, agree a neutral cue such as a slow count, or no cue. Keep the same cue and support for both tests.

What good looks like

The pupil chooses or confirms one form and technique. Staff note the choice before any teaching of the method. The quiet option and stop signal are clear.

Materials-free fallback

Use four small silent finger actions or let the pupil direct the adult's actions. If movement is not comfortable, the pupil may direct an original four-part spoken sequence.

Check and response

There is no best form. The important choice is one manageable technique that can be used in both periods.

Starter

Starter

3–6 minutes

Make your first test before the adult teaches the skill.

Use four marks or four actions. Keep a note of this first try.

What to say

This is your before test. Make four marks or four actions in your chosen way. I will not show a method yet. We will keep what really happens. If I help with materials, I will note that help. It is fine if this first try already works well. Use the cue agreed before this test, or no cue if that was the choice. I will not coach the target feature yet.

What good looks like

A real four-part baseline is retained before modelling. For marks, keep the actual row and describe its gaps. For actions, record the ordered pattern and whether the three intervals seemed steady or varied. Do not judge a pupil's feeling.

Materials-free fallback

Adult witnesses the pupil's four silent actions or follows the pupil's four directions. Keep a dated note of the sequence, cue and actual support when a recording surface is available.

Check and response

The first test is evidence of a starting point, not a failure. Do not replace it with a class model or claim exact timing without measuring it.

I do

I do

6–12 minutes

Watch the adult choose one small goal.

See how a guide or slow count can help.

The teacher model is not your before test.

Ready

Watch or direct four repeated moves with steady gaps. The adult controls the pace. No sound or personal story is needed.

One minute teacher demonstration

0 to 15 seconds Show a labelled adult first test with uneven gaps. Say: This is my real starting point for the model. Your before test stays yours.

15 to 30 seconds Make one small mark, look at its gap, then place a matching mark. Say: I keep one shape and practise its gaps.

30 to 45 seconds Make a four-action model on one slow count. Say: I keep this cue. I do not make it louder or faster.

45 to 60 seconds Point to the first test and goal. Say: I will practise, then compare the same four-part test. I cannot promise it will improve today.

Which feature could fit your own idea?

What to say

My goal is even gaps. I keep the same simple mark. I look at a gap, leave a similar gap, then make the next mark. I stop after four. For a steady action pattern I use a slow count, one, two, three, four, and make one small action on each count. I keep the same cue for practice and later tests. These are adult models.

What good looks like

The adult shows a simple repeatable method and one target: roughly even spaces for marks or a steady interval for actions. The pupil identifies the target and does not need to copy the adult's design.

Materials-free fallback

Model the four-part method through quiet speech or comfortable silent gestures. A pupil can direct the adult. Use the same cue agreed for the pupil's chosen technique.

Check and response

Check: Is the target A add as many marks as possible, B control the gaps or pace, or C make a perfect picture? B. If unclear, show two short rows or patterns and name only the chosen feature.

We do

We do

12–18 minutes

Practise the method with an adult or partner.

Change one thing at a time.

Choose your own goal for the next try.

Check your plan

Our target is even gaps. Which choice helps us practise that skill?

Choose, then explain your reason.

What to say

Let us use one simple cue. Keep the same mark or action. Try four parts, pause and notice the gaps or pace. Which one thing will you practise? Hinge: our target is even gaps. Should we A change the colour and size at once, B keep the mark and practise its gaps, or C add more marks so the row looks busy? Tell me why.

What good looks like

The pupil selects B and identifies the feature to practise. An adult may model one step, then return the next action to the pupil. Each pupil has their own goal rather than a copied group answer.

Materials-free fallback

Use four silent actions with one slow cue. The pupil may direct the adult. Point to or say A, B or C. No sound or art materials are needed.

Check and response

If the pupil chooses A or C, show only the target feature and ask a parallel question: For a steady four-action pattern, should we keep the cue the same or change the speed each time? Keep it the same. Continue when the pupil identifies one focused target.

What to say

Use your before record to choose one target. Practise a short round, pause, notice, then practise again. You may use more space for practice, but keep the four-part test for comparison. Record the cue, size or pace guide and any adult help. You will use the same technique in the next lesson. A pause is part of practice.

What good looks like

The pupil practises the chosen technique, retains the baseline and records a specific target and cue. Staff can identify the chosen feature and what help was used. A practice round is labelled as practice, not an invented final result.

Materials-free fallback

Use a silent four-action sequence or pupil-directed adult sequence. Adult retains a precise dated witness note of the before test, chosen cue, practice and pupil view. A device is not needed.

Check and response

Examples of a clear goal: I will leave similar gaps between four marks. I will keep a steady pace for four small actions. Accept a comfortable adapted version.

Optional stretch what to say

Set one skill goal. Keep your first test. Try a cue and ask for one useful comment. Say why you will keep or change the cue for practice. Plan a fair next test.

Optional stretch what good looks like

The pupil links a specific skill target to a chosen practice cue and a reasoned response to feedback. The record sets up a fair later comparison.

Optional stretch materials-free fallback

Adult records the same prompts orally, including the exact four-action sequence, cue, support and pupil's own reason.

Review

Review

32–37 minutes

Show your first test and one practice try.

Say what you notice. It may be the same.

Keep your work and cue for next time.

Teacher model only: My technique is four repeated marks with even gaps. My first row had one small gap. I practised leaving a similar space. I will keep the same mark, size and cue next time.

Teacher model only: My technique is four small actions at a steady pace. I used the same slow count for two practice rounds. I noticed the last action came early. Next time I will practise that final gap.

Teacher examples only. Keep your own work, words and choices as evidence.

What to say

Compare the real records. What stayed the same? What changed? You can say that you need more practice. A partner can notice one feature without ranking the work. In the final two minutes, put tools away, keep paintings flat and store the baseline and cue together.

What good looks like

The pupil makes a specific observation about gaps or pace and retains their own starting point. No improvement is assumed. Private sharing is enough. The next-period cue is clear.

Materials-free fallback

An adult repeats the witnessed sequence from the note and asks the pupil to confirm it. Keep staff observations distinct from the pupil's words.

Check and response

Useful: The gap after mark two is smaller. The first three actions were steady and the last came sooner. Vague praise alone is not the comparison.

Teacher guidance

Preparation

For five pupils: five books or arts logs, five pencils and fifteen small test spaces or sheets for before, practice and after records. Reuse books to avoid extra sheets.

Optional visual route: six drawing tools including the adult model. If using paint, use six brushes, five mats, three stable water pots, a small amount of familiar paint and cloths. Dry marks need no paint.

Quiet or silent route: fingertips or a small comfortable seated action. No instruments or recording device are required.

Adult model: a four-mark line or a four-action pattern, plus a visibly labelled original before and after demonstration. Keep the adult example separate from pupil evidence.

Retain each pupil's chosen technique, baseline and cue for the second period. An adult can make a precise dated observation note when writing materials are unavailable.

Teaching approach

Model the chosen technique in small steps. Compare a real first try and a later try. Keep each pupil's own choice and reason. You do not need to paint or perform expertly. Judge intention and explanation. Use the least help needed and record the support actually used. Scribe the pupil's own words, read them back and allow correction. Plan in any language.

Sound stays below talking volume. Use a raised open hand to stop. Offer the silent option before sound, seated movement and a private audience. No personal disclosure is required. Use familiar non-toxic materials and local procedures. Keep wet materials away from electrical items.

Prompt ladder

  1. Point to the instruction or place.
  2. Ask a short question.
  3. Rephrase the instruction.
  4. Provide a scaffold or choice.
  5. Model the first step only, then return the task.

Common misconceptions

Practice always makes the second version better. Record what actually happened. It may improve, stay similar or need more practice. A clear observation is useful evidence.

More marks or faster taps show more skill. The chosen target is control. Keep four marks or actions in the test and focus on the gaps or pace.

A fair before and after comparison can use completely different tasks. Use the same chosen technique and four-part test. Keep similar conditions and note help. If access requires a new form, keep the old record and make a fresh baseline for that form.

A polished adult example can replace a pupil's first try. Keep the pupil's real baseline, attempt and words. Label all adult samples as teacher models.

This lesson contributes to the Explore record. A whole award decision needs the full portfolio. Any optional next level evidence is additional.

A calm pause

Put tools down. Choose a quiet position. You can watch or use silent movement when you return.

Ask an adult for the agreed quiet space or return cue. You do not need to explain a personal feeling to the group.
