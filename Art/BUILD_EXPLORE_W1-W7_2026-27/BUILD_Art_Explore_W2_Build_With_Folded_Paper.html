Build With Folded Paper

BUILD · Art · Week 2. Trinity Arts Award Explore. Build With Folded Paper.

Objective: Take part in paper sculpture, learn safe folds and ways to make paper stand, and record what you learnt.

Success evidence: your own folded paper form, a record of taking part in activity two, and a learning point about a fold or base.

Retrieval

Arrival · retrieve

After drawing lines and tone, you will try paper sculpture. Fold a flat sheet into a form that rises from the table.

A sculpture has depth, height and width. View it from more than one side. A hand-sized paper form can be a sculpture.

Use a clear, flat table and dry paper. Keep drinks and heavy objects away. You need no sharp tool.

Choose a rectangle or wide strip. Feel how it bends, then keep it flat while we look at folds.

3 min
Starter

Starter · create the need

Look at the flat rectangle and the folded tent beside it. Both began as the same kind of paper. One lies across the table. The other has raised panels with a fold along the top.

Move your eyes around the tent. From the end, you can see the open space beneath it. From the side, you can see a broad paper panel. The form changes as your view changes.

What helped the paper rise? Point to the fold, then the lower edges touching the table. The fold joins the panels. The lower edges support them.

A drawing can suggest a raised form on a flat page. Here we make a form that really rises. You can explore the shape without drawing a picture on it. Colour and decoration can wait while you learn how it stands.

6 min
I Do

I Do · model

I put a rectangle flat on the table. I bring one short edge towards the other short edge. I line them up as well as I can, without pulling the paper hard.

I hold the edges still with one hand. With the other, I press along the bend to make a crease. I use the flat pad of a finger and move away from my body. A crease is a fold line that the paper tends to keep.

Now I open the sheet part way. I turn it so the crease is at the top and the two open edges rest on the table. I have made a tent form.

I move the lower edges a little farther apart. The form gets lower and its base gets wider. I let go to see what happens. I do not say it stands until it can stand without my hand.

If it falls, I set it up again and check the base. Falling is a clue to explore, not a reason to hide the work.

5 min
I Do

I Do 2 · connect

A fold is also like a hinge between two panels. I can open or close it. The panels stay joined along the crease while their angle changes.

Watch a second method with a strip. I fold a short part over and press. Then I turn the strip and fold the next part back the other way. I keep changing the direction to make a zigzag.

I stand the zigzag on its long lower edge. If I spread it too far, it may lie flat. If I squeeze it tight, it may lean. I try a middle position and let go.

There is no single shape everyone must make. A long low form and a short tall form can both be choices. I look at the base, the folds and the spaces to decide what I want to keep.

My note could say: opening the folds changed the spaces I could see. I point to those spaces as I speak.

5 min
We Do

We Do 2 · lab

Now make a practice fold with your own paper. You may begin with the tent or try a zigzag. Bring edges together, press the crease gently and open the form.

Set it on the table and let go. Look at where it touches the surface. If it leans, try spreading the base or opening one fold a little. Change one position at a time so you can see its effect.

Tell a partner or an adult what you tried. They can point to a place they want to see more clearly. Turn the form so both of you can look from that side.

Keep a short note about what the fold or base did. A scribe can write your own words. The form you have just made gives you something real to point at.

11 min
Independent work

Independent · evidence

Choose a form to explore on your own. Use fresh paper if you want to keep the practice form as well. Make the folds and arrange the panels in a way you find interesting.

Look at it from the front, side and end. You can move around the table or turn a base sheet under the form. Notice a shape or space that appears from one view.

Test whether it stands when you let go. You may choose a wider base or a lower form. You can also keep a form that needs support and explain what happened. Do not pretend it stood by itself if a hand was holding it.

  • Core: use a large rectangle and one guided fold. Make and position your own form, then point and say what you learnt for a verbatim scribe.
  • Extend: choose a tent or zigzag, explore how it stands and record your own learning about a fold, base or space.
  • Challenge: retain participation and learning, then compare two positions of the same folded form and explain their different effects.

Keep the form or a clear record of it with your learning note. A photo may show the artwork without showing anyone’s face. Label the record paper sculpture, activity two.
